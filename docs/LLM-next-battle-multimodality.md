# The Next Battle for Large Models: Is Multimodality Inevitable, or an Overhyped Narrative?

A deep dialectical examination of AI's future direction

---

## Introduction: An Unavoidable Debate Over Direction

In May 2024, OpenAI released GPT-4o. At the launch event, when the model analyzed facial expressions in real time and responded to questions in an emotionally rich voice, the audience gasped in amazement. That moment forged a near-instinctive consensus across the global tech world: the future of large models lies in multimodality.

But is this consensus correct?

When we discuss the "future direction," we are actually conflating two distinct questions: whether multimodality has value (almost everyone agrees it does); and whether, right now, multimodality should be treated as the top-priority main direction—even to the point of using it as a reason to downplay the deep optimization of pure-text language models (this is where genuine disagreement lies).

This article attempts to disentangle these two questions and, drawing on the technical progress, product cases, and market data of recent years, arrive at a more honest judgment.

---

## Dimension One: The Real Value of Perceptual Expansion

The most intuitive argument in favor of prioritizing multimodality is that human cognition itself is multimodal.

This argument is not hollow. In healthcare, Google's Med-PaLM M reached specialist-physician level in diagnosing chest X-rays, fundus images, and pathology slides, and Stanford research showed that the accuracy of combined vision-plus-text diagnosis was 23 percentage points higher than pure-text medical-record analysis. In education, the feature that lets students photograph a problem to solve it boosted engagement by 40%. In the creative industries, Midjourney, Suno, and Adobe Firefly gave tens of millions of ordinary people professional-grade creative production capability for the first time.

These are not demo cases, but commercial deployments with a real user base.

From an information-theoretic standpoint, this trend also has its own internal logic. The volume of video information generated on Earth each day far exceeds that of text information. A single frame of a 1080P image contains information equivalent to thousands of words. An AI that can only process text is exposed to only a tiny subset of the human information world.

---

## Dimension Two: Is the "Maturity" of LLMs Overstated?

However, the inference that "LLMs are already mature enough, so we should pivot to multimodality" does not hold up under close scrutiny.

What is the evidence for "mature enough"? ChatGPT has more than 200 million monthly active users, and its enterprise clients cover more than 90% of the Fortune 500. By user scale, this is indeed impressive.

But viewed through the lens of capability reliability, the picture is far more complex. Stanford's 2024 evaluation showed that LLMs still have a hallucination rate as high as 20-30% on the task of summarizing medical literature. The problem in the legal domain is even more concrete: reports keep surfacing of New York lawyers being sanctioned by courts for submitting fabricated case citations generated by AI. That 200 million people use a tool with such a high error rate every day speaks to the tool's popularity, not the maturity of its capabilities.

More importantly, OpenAI's o1/o3 series demonstrated the potential of another path. By abandoning the fast-iteration RLHF route and returning to deep reasoning training, o3's improvement on the ARC-AGI benchmark was several times the sum of all multimodal progress over the past two years. This shows that the reasoning depth of LLMs is far from hitting a ceiling. Diverting resources away from here toward multimodality carries a real cost.

---

## Dimension Three: The Debate Over Multimodality's Technical Depth

Advocates of multimodality often invoke an exhilarating scenario: GPT-4o looks at a photo and can understand emotions, analyze composition, and give a deep response by connecting the context. Isn't this "mature enough"?

Here there is a crucial technical detail worth taking seriously.

The architecture of nearly all current multimodal large models is pipeline-based: a visual encoder converts the image into tokens, and the language model processes those tokens. There is no genuine bidirectional interaction between visual reasoning and language reasoning—the visual input is ultimately "translated" into language concepts, and the core processing remains language reasoning.

There is an experiment that illustrates this problem: for the same image, if you use words to tell GPT-4o what the image contains ("a sad girl, with the ruins of war in the background"), the quality of its analysis is almost no different from looking at the image directly. Visual input is the "receiving end," language reasoning is the "processing end"—the multimodal "synergy" is, under the current architecture, mostly an illusion. A more accurate description is: the LLM is the core engine, and multimodality is peripheral input.

MIT research in 2024 also found that most multimodal models suffer systematic failures in real-world visual reasoning—especially in understanding spatial relationships, counting, and 3D structure, they are significantly weaker than humans. The physics errors in Sora-generated videos, such as liquid flowing backward and limbs clipping through bodies, likewise demonstrate this point: the model imitates visual patterns at the statistical level but has not genuinely understood physical reality.

---

## Dimension Four: The Stratification of Commercial Value

The commercial value of multimodality requires an important distinction.

What are enterprises using multimodal AI for? The core of Salesforce's visual analytics is recognizing product categories in images, HubSpot's image understanding is detecting how frequently brand logos appear, and factory safety inspection is identifying whether workers are wearing hard hats. What these tasks genuinely require is machine vision (computer vision), not multimodal large language models—ResNet, mature as early as 2019, is enough. Most commercial security-AI vendors indeed use lightweight, purpose-built vision models, because general-purpose multimodal large models are too costly and too slow at inference.

Andreessen Horowitz's 2024 report corroborates this: most enterprise AI projects cut multimodal features because inference costs exceeded expectations. The token cost of multimodal inference is several times that of text inference, which is a real constraint in a price-sensitive market.

There genuinely are high-value scenarios that require the deep capabilities of multimodal large models—complex medical-imaging analysis, cross-modal understanding of scientific research data—but these scenarios are better served by domain-specific models than by general-purpose multimodal large models. In medical-imaging diagnosis, the accuracy of GPT-4V (general-purpose multimodal) is 63%, while a purpose-built medical vision model (the specialized version of Med-Gemini) achieves 84%, and the gap does not narrow significantly as scale increases.

---

## Dimension Five: The Two-Sidedness of the Data Bottleneck

Multimodality advocates have an important argument: text data is nearly exhausted. The Meta research team estimates that high-quality text data on the internet will peak in 2026-2028. Image and video data far outnumber text data and are the way out of the "data wall."

This argument is real, but the chain of logic is incomplete.

The volume of multimodal data is large, but the density of effective training signal is extremely low. Videos on YouTube lack accompanying high-quality semantic annotations, and the alt text of images is extremely crude—so while the volume of multimodal data is large, much of it is noise. By contrast, synthetic data is another path for expanding text data—DeepMind's AlphaProof used purely synthetic mathematical-proof data to achieve a historic breakthrough at the International Mathematical Olympiad. In domains with an objective verification mechanism (whether a program runs, whether a proof holds), synthetic data can effectively break through the data wall.

Both paths have their value and limitations, but equating "text data peaking" directly with "we should prioritize developing multimodality" is a simplification that skips many intermediate steps.

---

## Dimension Six: The Asymmetric Risks of Safety and Ethics

The safety risks of multimodal technology are harder to handle than those of pure text, and they are often underestimated.

Deepfakes are the most typical safety hazard of multimodal technology. In 2024, a Hong Kong company was defrauded of 25 million US dollars via a multimodally forged video conference. The harmfulness of multimodal content is more insidious—context-dependent imagery, cross-lingual visual memes, and the spread of banned information under the cover of "normal" images—and the difficulty of moderation increases exponentially compared with textual content.

Content-provenance technologies such as the C2PA digital watermark work in the lab, but fail in real-world dissemination environments (transcoding, screenshots, compression). Regarding applications for visual emotion recognition, MIT Media Lab research further found that the correlation between facial muscle movements and inner emotional states is far lower than commonly assumed, so affective-computing applications built on this scientific assumption carry inherent accuracy defects and ethical risks.

This is not a repudiation of multimodal technology itself, but rather a claim that: at a stage where safety alignment is not yet mature, the rapid expansion of multimodal capabilities calls for greater prudence, rather than pressing forward quickly on the grounds that "governance will catch up."

---

## Dimension Seven: The Timing Problem in Resource Allocation

Taken together, the core of this debate is not "whether multimodality has value," but "at what time and in what proportion to invest in multimodality."

One interesting observation is that there is a clear divergence between what the world's top AI labs focus on in their research publications and what they focus on in their product launches. At the product level, multimodality is the marketing focal point, the star of the launch event; at the research level, the most profound advances (the reasoning breakthroughs of o1/o3, the systematization of chain-of-thought, self-consistency methods, and the verification mechanisms of synthetic data) are mostly in the field of pure language models.

This divergence tells us something: products move toward multimodality because it is visible and tangible to users, and the experience is more intuitive; but the truly hard problems of intelligence are still being mined by researchers in the soil of language reasoning.

Historically, in the development path of every generation of technology, there is a stage where "the narrative runs ahead of the technology." Part of the current discussion around multimodal large models falls into this category—its long-term direction may be correct, but the short-term proportion of resource investment may be overestimated by a grand narrative.

---

## Conclusion: Foundation and Roof, Not an Either/Or

The most honest answer is neither "multimodality is the future" nor "LLMs are the focus," but rather: both matter, but in sequence.

At the current stage, the reliability, reasoning depth, and safety alignment of language models remain the priority directions most worthy of concentrated resources. These capabilities are the foundation. The perceptual expansion of multimodality is real incremental value, but pouring large amounts of resources into the top layer while the foundation is not yet stable carries high structural risk.

This is not technological conservatism, but respect for engineering reality.

The release of GPT-4o was indeed impressive, and the commercial deployment of multimodality is genuinely advancing. But what truly changes the world is not the feature of "being able to see images," but the not-yet-realized whole—when AI can truly fuse perception, reasoning, and reliability. Until that day arrives, we need to keep a clear-eyed judgment between chasing the narrative and shoring up the foundation.
