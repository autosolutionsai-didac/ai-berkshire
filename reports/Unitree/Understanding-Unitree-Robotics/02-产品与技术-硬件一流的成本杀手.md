# Products & Technology — A Cost Killer with First-Rate Hardware

> *Understanding Unitree Robotics* series · Part 02
> Reading time: approx. 12 minutes

## What does Unitree actually sell?

A humanoid robot priced at 29,900 yuan — more than 200 times cheaper than a Boston Dynamics Atlas. Yet gross margin runs as high as 60%, surpassing Apple. Both facts hold at once, which tells you Unitree has done something genuinely different with its cost structure.

Unitree Robotics has three product lines, and the logic behind each is completely different.

### Line one: quadruped robots — a proven cash cow

Quadruped robots are Unitree's founding business, with cumulative sales exceeding **30,000 units** and a global market share of roughly **70%** (source: TrendForce).

| Product | Positioning | Price | Target customer |
|------|------|------|---------|
| Go2 Air | Consumer entry-level | approx. **$1,600** (approx. 11,000 yuan) | Hobbyists, education |
| Go2 Pro | Consumer, advanced | approx. **$2,800** (approx. 20,000 yuan) | Developers, research |
| B2 | Industrial-grade | in the **tens of thousands of yuan** | Power-line inspection, firefighting, logistics |
| As2 | Next-generation quadruped | — | Embodied-intelligence platform |

(Source: Unitree Robotics official website, prospectus)

The quadruped line has already completed the full cycle from research to commercialization:

- 2022: research/education revenue accounted for **68.61%** of quadruped sales
- First three quarters of 2025: commercial/consumer became the largest source, at **42.30%**; industry applications accounted for **26.12%**

(Source: prospectus)

State Grid, PetroChina and other large state-owned enterprises have already deployed Unitree quadruped robots at scale for inspection work. This means the quadruped business no longer depends on "selling to labs" — it now has a real repeat-purchase use case.

**The counter-argument, however**, is that the quadruped robot market has a limited ceiling. Global annual quadruped shipments run at roughly **30,000-40,000 units**, and Unitree already holds 70% of that — leaving little room for further growth. This is precisely why Unitree has to bet on humanoid robots.

### Line two: humanoid robots — the core bet behind the 42-billion-yuan valuation

In 2025, humanoid robot revenue surpassed quadruped revenue for the first time, accounting for about **52%** of total revenue, with shipments of **5,500 units** — first in the world.

| Product | Height | Price | Positioning | Key capability |
|------|------|------|------|---------|
| H1 | 180cm | approx. **$90,000** | Full-size flagship | Strong locomotion |
| G1 | 127cm | **$13,500-16,000** | Compact | Volume driver |
| **R1** | — | **29,900-39,900 yuan** | **Lowest-priced humanoid robot** | Consumer-tier trial |
| H2 | 180cm | — | New 2026 flagship | Flying kicks, backflips |

(Source: prospectus, public launch events)

The pricing of the R1 is especially worth noting — a humanoid robot starting at **29,900 yuan**, the lowest price globally. For reference, a single Boston Dynamics Atlas costs over a million dollars, and Tesla's Optimus is projected at **$20,000-25,000** (target price once in mass production).

**But the key question is**: who bought those 5,500 units?

| Customer type | Share of humanoid robot revenue |
|---------|-----------------|
| Research/education | **73.60%** |
| Commercial/consumer | 17.39% |
| Industry applications | 9.01% |

(Source: prospectus, first three quarters of 2025)

**Three-quarters of humanoid robots were sold to universities and research institutions.** That's not necessarily a bad thing — research customers are typically the early adopters of a new product category, and quadrupeds followed the same path. But it does mean humanoid robots still have a distance to travel before they're truly "working in factories" or "serving households."

**Counter-argument**: the research/education market itself has a ceiling — how many universities and labs in the world actually need humanoid robots? If commercial/consumer and industrial applications fail to open up, humanoid robot growth will quickly hit a wall.

**Supporting argument**: quadruped robots derived 69% of revenue from research in 2022, falling to 32% by 2025 — the same evolution curve is now playing out for humanoid robots; it's simply a matter of time.

### Line three: core components — the real hidden moat

Unitree's most easily overlooked competitive strength lies not in the finished robot, but in its components.

The domestic-sourcing rate for core components exceeds **90%**, with an in-house development list that includes:

| Component | Unitree's in-house status | Industry comparison |
|--------|------------|---------|
| Joint motors | In-house, torque density **45 Nm/kg** | Boston Dynamics: approx. 40 Nm/kg |
| Reducers | In-house | Most competitors buy externally |
| Controllers | In-house | Most competitors buy externally |
| LiDAR | In-house, cost **under 1,000 yuan** | Market average: several thousand yuan |

(Source: prospectus)

**The joint motor's torque density of 45 Nm/kg exceeds Boston Dynamics' 40 Nm/kg** — a marker of Unitree's genuine hardware strength.

The strategic significance of in-house component development is twofold:

1. **Cost control**: the cost of the self-developed motor is roughly **1/10** that of a Boston Dynamics hydraulic system (source: public industry analysis) — this is the fundamental reason the R1 can be priced at 29,900 yuan
2. **Supply chain independence**: not subject to being held hostage by external suppliers, which matters especially in the current geopolitical environment

**Counter-argument**: in-house component development is an advantage in the early stage, but once an industry scales up, standardized supply chains may become more efficient than in-house development. DJI's success was built on in-house development, but the smartphone industry proved that the standardized Qualcomm + TSMC path can win just as well.

## Where does the 60% gross margin come from?

A hardware company with a **60.27%** gross margin — that number needs an explanation.

### Reason one: full in-house development suppresses cost

The most expensive components in the finished machine (motors, reducers, controllers) are all developed in-house, avoiding the markup and middleman profit of external sourcing. According to industry analysis, Unitree's finished-unit cost is **30-50%** lower than comparable competitors.

### Reason two: a premium from research/education customers

Research and education customers are less price-sensitive and more sensitive to technical specifications. The G1 sold to universities is priced at **$13,500-16,000**, but the cost may be only **30-40%** of that price.

**There's a hidden concern here**: the 60% gross margin is, to some extent, propped up by the premium charged to research/education customers. If the sales mix shifts toward industrial customers and consumers in the future, gross margin will likely decline. Industrial customers have strong bargaining power, and consumers are more price-sensitive — with the R1 starting at 29,900 yuan, its gross margin is very likely well below 60%.

### Reason three: early signs of economies of scale

The cumulative 30,000-unit scale of the quadruped business has already amortized fixed production-line costs. If humanoid robot shipments reach **10,000-20,000 units** in 2026 (Wang Xingxing's publicly stated target, per public reporting), economies of scale will be released further.

**Counter-argument**: the 60% gross margin may be an "early-stage dividend" rather than a "long-term norm." Compare with DJI — its early gross margin was also very high, and as price wars set in and the market matured, gross margin gradually fell back into the 40-50% range. Unitree's gross margin will very likely go through a similar process.

## "First-rate hardware, second-rate AI" — Unitree's biggest structural contradiction

Unitree's hardware capability is beyond doubt — motor torque density exceeding Boston Dynamics, a domestic-sourcing rate above 90%, and globally leading cost control.

**But the ultimate value of a humanoid robot doesn't lie in "how fast it can run or how high it can jump" — it lies in "understanding instructions, making autonomous decisions, and completing complex tasks."** That's an AI capability, not a hardware capability.

One comparison makes the gap vivid:

| Dimension | Unitree Robotics | Tesla Optimus |
|------|---------|---------------|
| R&D spend | **90.2 million yuan** (7.73% of revenue) | On the order of billions of dollars (AI team of over 10,000) |
| AI approach | Reinforcement learning (BeamDojo framework) | **FSD end-to-end model transfer** |
| Training data | Primarily simulation environments | Real-world driving data from millions of Teslas |
| Embodied intelligence model | Open-source VLA/WMA, still immature | Closed-source, rapid iteration |
| AI autonomous decision-making | Moderate | **Industry-leading** |

(Source: prospectus, Tesla public materials)

Unitree's AI strategy is "open-source plus external partnerships" — it develops the BeamDojo reinforcement-learning framework in-house for motion control, while open-sourcing VLA (vision-language-action) and WMA (world-model-action) models to attract the community. But compared with Tesla's path of "transferring the autonomous-driving model directly to the robot," **a generational gap exists**.

Of the **4.2 billion yuan** raised in the IPO, **2.022 billion yuan** (48%) is earmarked for "intelligent robot model R&D" — which shows that Unitree itself is well aware that the AI brain is its biggest shortfall.

**The core question here is**: is 4.2 billion yuan enough?

- Tesla's AI team's annual R&D budget may exceed **$10 billion**
- OpenAI raised **$6.6 billion** in 2025
- Of Unitree's 4.2 billion yuan IPO raise, only 2 billion yuan goes to AI R&D — and that has to be spread over multiple years

**2 billion yuan versus tens of billions of dollars** — this is not a contest on the same order of magnitude.

**But the supporting argument is**: AI capability doesn't necessarily have to be built entirely from scratch in-house. China has a large number of AI large-model companies (Baidu, Alibaba, Zhipu, etc.), and Unitree could pursue a "hardware platform + AI interface" model — similar to how an Android phone doesn't need to build its own operating system. Unitree's value may lie not in "building the smartest brain," but in "building the best body."

---

## Bottom line

**Unitree Robotics is one of the world's best robotics hardware companies — 60% gross margin, full in-house development, and the world's lowest costs. But the endgame of humanoid robotics isn't "whose body is better" — it's "whose brain is smarter." The 42-billion-yuan valuation is a bet not just on the hardware moat, but on whether Unitree can close the AI-brain gap with Tesla.**
