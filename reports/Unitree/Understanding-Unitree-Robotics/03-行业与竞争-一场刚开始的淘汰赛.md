## Humanoid robots: an industry where "the market is small but the story is big"

Global humanoid robot shipments came in under 15,000 units in 2025 — fewer than a single mid-sized phone brand ships in one day. Yet Morgan Stanley projects this market could reach $5 trillion by 2050. Between today's reality and that long-range forecast lies an enormous gap.

First, the industry fundamentals:

| Metric | 2025 | 2030 forecast | 2035 forecast |
|------|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Global shipments | approx. **15,000 units** | **250,000 units** (Goldman Sachs) | — |
| Global market size | Several billion yuan | **Tens of billions of dollars** | **$38 billion** (Goldman Sachs) |
| China shipments | approx. **12,000 units** | — | — |
| Cost per unit | On the order of **100,000 yuan** (Q1 2026) | Expected to fall to the tens of thousands | — |

(Source: Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, GGII (High-Tech Industry Research Institute))

Reaching 250,000 units by 2030 means growing **17-fold** in five years. That's not impossible, but it requires a "killer application" to emerge.

2026 is widely seen within the industry as the **"make-or-break commercialization year"** for humanoid robots — the inflection points in production scale and cost are expected to arrive simultaneously. Whoever can establish real industrial and consumer use cases this year will hold the advantage in the next round of competition.

**Counter-argument**: industrial robots (robotic arms, etc.) are already a mature, hundred-billion-dollar market — why do we need "humanoid" at all? Many factory scenarios can be solved with purpose-built robots. "Humanoid" may be a false need. The real value of a humanoid robot lies in "generality" — but generality specifically requires the strongest AI, not the best hardware.

## Five key players, five different paths

### Player one: Unitree Robotics — the cost killer

**Core strategy**: full in-house hardware development → extreme cost-effectiveness → build a scale moat through shipment volume

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|------|------|
| World's number-one shipment volume (5,500 units) | A generational gap in AI capability |
| 60% gross margin, already profitable | R&D spend of only 90.2 million yuan |
| Full in-house development, 90%+ domestic sourcing | 73% of revenue relies on research/education |
| R1 priced at 29,900 yuan, the world's lowest | Team of only 480 people |

Unitree's playbook resembles early-stage BYD — don't build the most advanced technology, build the cheapest product, and trade scale for time.

### Player two: AgiBot — China's second pole

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| 2025 shipments | **5,168 units** (2nd globally) |
| Global market share | approx. **39%** |
| Core strategy | AI + supply-chain binding |

(Source: TrendForce)

AgiBot and Unitree have taken completely different paths — Unitree "develops everything in-house," while AgiBot "invests in and partners with supply-chain allies." Through investment and partnership, AgiBot has tied up a large number of supply-chain partners across the sensing layer (Orbbec, Dreame), the actuation layer (Blue Ocean Intelligence, ZhongDa Leader), and the manufacturing layer (Longyun Precision, Bozhon Precision), among others.

**The competition between Unitree and AgiBot is fundamentally a contest between "vertical integration vs. ecosystem alliance"** — similar to the early Apple-versus-Android dynamic.

Together, the two companies account for **roughly 80%** of global humanoid robot shipments, forming a de facto Chinese duopoly.

### Player three: Tesla Optimus — the biggest wildcard

| Timeline | Progress |
|--------|------|
| 2022 | Optimus prototype unveiled |
| 2025 | In-factory internal testing, shipments <5,000 units |
| **Q1 2026** | **Third-generation production version (Gen 3) design finalized** |
| **August 2026** | **Large-scale production line comes online** |
| **2027** | **Official external sales begin** |

(Source: Tesla public disclosures, industry reporting)

Tesla's core weapon isn't hardware — it's **AI**. Its FSD (Full Self-Driving) end-to-end model can be transferred directly to Optimus — meaning the real-world data collected daily by millions of Teslas is fueling the training of Optimus's brain.

Cost target: once mass production reaches the million-unit scale, per-unit cost is expected to fall to **$20,000-25,000**. If achieved, this price would strike directly at Unitree's low-to-mid-end market.

**Tesla's timeline is the key variable**: if Optimus is genuinely mass-produced and sold externally in 2027, Unitree has less than two years to build its own moat. If Tesla is delayed (as Musk's timelines frequently are), Unitree's window will be longer.

**Counter-argument (bullish on Unitree)**: Tesla's timelines have a consistent track record of unreliability — Cybertruck was delayed by 2 years, Roadster 2 by more than 5 years. Optimus's 2027 mass-production promise might be more realistic if discounted by half.

**Supporting argument (bearish on Unitree)**: even if Optimus is delayed by 2 years to 2029, the AI-driven generational leap it brings would still be something Unitree cannot defend against. Time only delays the problem — it doesn't remove it.

### Player four: Boston Dynamics — the old-guard aristocrat pivots

In 2024, Boston Dynamics made an important decision: abandoning the hydraulic drive it had stuck with for 30 years in favor of pure electric drive. That amounts to an admission that Unitree chose the right technical path from day one.

| Metric | Boston Dynamics |
|-----------|-----------|
| Electric-drive Atlas | 2026 order book already full |
| First customers | Hyundai, Google DeepMind |
| Industrialization path | Enters Hyundai's factories in 2028 |
| Positioning | **High-end**, price on the order of a million dollars |

(Source: CES 2026, Boston Dynamics public disclosures)

Boston Dynamics' strategy is "small volumes, high-end, deeply tied to major customers" — complementary to, rather than in direct competition with, Unitree's "high volume, low-to-mid end, broad coverage" approach.

**In the near term, Boston Dynamics poses limited threat to Unitree** — the two aren't competing for the same market. But over the long run, if high-end technology trickles down, Boston Dynamics' technical reserves could put pressure on the low-to-mid end.

### Player five: Figure AI — Silicon Valley's AI-native path

Figure AI is taking an "AI-first" approach — partnering deeply with OpenAI so the robot gets "smart" first, letting the hardware iterate more slowly.

It has already completed a pilot at a BMW factory, gaining real industrial-scene validation. Although shipment volume is small, it represents an entirely different competitive logic: **not "whose body is better," but "whose brain is better."**

If Figure's path proves out — that is, if "AI brain + standard hardware" can complete industrial tasks — then the value of Unitree's "hardware differentiation" would shrink substantially.

## Three possible competitive landscapes

Putting all five players together, three possible landscapes could emerge over the next 3-5 years:

### Scenario one: hardware wins (most favorable for Unitree)

If AI progress in humanoid robotics is slower than expected — if large models struggle to translate into embodied intelligence — then hardware cost-effectiveness remains the core competitive advantage.

- Unitree consolidates the global low-to-mid-end market through cost advantage plus scale effects
- Similar to today's drone market: DJI holds 70%+ global share on the strength of hardware and cost

**Probability assessment**: moderate. AI progress may be slow, but is unlikely to stall entirely.

### Scenario two: AI generational leap (least favorable for Unitree)

If Tesla Optimus achieves large-scale mass production in 2027-2028, and the FSD end-to-end model gives Optimus autonomous decision-making capability far beyond its competitors —

- Unitree's hardware advantage gets fully offset by the AI capability gap
- Unitree is reduced to a "hardware contract manufacturer" role, similar to today's Android phone contract manufacturers
- Its valuation shrinks sharply from 42 billion yuan

**Probability assessment**: low-to-moderate. Tesla's timeline is unreliable, and the Chinese market may present policy barriers to Tesla.

### Scenario three: layered coexistence (most likely)

The humanoid robot market splits into multiple tiers, with different players occupying different niches:

| Market tier | Dominant players | Positioning |
|---------|---------|------|
| High-end industrial (automotive, aerospace) | Boston Dynamics, Tesla | AI + high-end hardware |
| Mid-tier industrial (logistics, patrol/inspection) | Unitree, AgiBot | Cost-effectiveness + reliability |
| Consumer | Unitree (R1), new entrants | Extreme low price |
| AI application layer | Tesla, Figure, AI majors | Brain + ecosystem |

**Probability assessment**: relatively high. Similar to today's auto market — Tesla, BYD, and Toyota coexist across different segments.

**In scenario three, Unitree is most likely to become "the BYD of robotics"** — the leader of the global low-to-mid-end market: not the most advanced, but the most practical and most cost-effective. This is a good business, but not a "winner-take-all" one — its valuation ceiling will sit below the market's hopes for a "DJI of robotics."

## Geopolitics: a variable that can't be ignored

Unitree Robotics' overseas revenue share sits between **39% and 57%** (source: prospectus data from different periods). The United States has already issued security warnings about Chinese robotics companies.

| Risk point | Current status |
|--------|------|
| US market | Security warnings already issued |
| Technology export controls | Chip controls could extend to robotics |
| Supply chain | 90%+ domestic sourcing, limited exposure |
| Customer structure | Overseas customer concentration not fully disclosed |

Unitree's 90%+ domestic-sourcing rate is a structural advantage here — even if chip controls escalate, Unitree's dependence on US supply chains is far lower than that of companies like Huawei.

**But export-side risk is hard to control**: if the US or its allies restrict purchases of Chinese humanoid robots, Unitree's overseas market will take a direct hit.

**Counter-argument**: robots don't touch national-security core interests the way chips do — the priority for sanctions ranks below semiconductors and AI large models. And Chinese robots have such a large price advantage that many countries have no viable substitute in the near term.

## An easily overlooked industry feature: no network effects

Internet companies (WeChat, Taobao) benefit from strong network effects — the more users, the more valuable the product, and the moat deepens automatically.

**Humanoid robots have no network effects.** Your Unitree R1 doesn't get better just because your neighbor bought one too.

What does this mean?

- Low brand loyalty: whoever's next generation is cheaper or better switches the customer over
- Technical moats require continuous investment to maintain — you can't "coast and collect rent"
- Latecomers (Tesla) can enter directly with a better product, without needing to "catch up on user base"

**The humanoid robotics industry looks more like the auto industry than the internet industry** — no winner-take-all, only continuous competition. Unitree's number-one shipment position today doesn't guarantee it will still be number one in three years.

---

## Bottom line

**The humanoid robotics industry is on the eve of a boom where "the market is small but expectations are large." Unitree has secured the world's top shipment position through its cost advantage, but that position's moat isn't deep — there are no network effects, a generational AI gap persists, and Tesla's mass production looms in 2027. The most likely outcome is "layered coexistence," with Unitree becoming the low-to-mid-end leader — but not the winner that takes all.**
