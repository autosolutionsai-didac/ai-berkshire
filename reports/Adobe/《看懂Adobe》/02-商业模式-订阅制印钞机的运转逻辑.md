# How the Subscription Money-Printing Machine Runs

> Understanding Adobe series - Part 02 - Business model
> Reading time: about 10 minutes

---

## What kind of money-making machine is this?

The most direct way to judge the quality of a business is to look at three things: can it keep raising prices, how costly is it for customers to leave, and does its cash flow convert into profit at full value, without a discount. Adobe satisfies all three - and to a degree that puts it among a rare few in global software.

In this piece, we take the machine apart to see why it works this way, and where cracks are starting to appear.

First, a set of core financial figures:

| Metric | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 | Trend |
|------|--------|--------|--------|------|
| Total revenue | $19.41 billion | $21.40 billion | **$23.77 billion** | Consecutive acceleration |
| Revenue growth | 10% | 10% | **11%** | Still accelerating at a $24 billion scale |
| Subscription revenue share | >90% | >92% | **>93%** | Almost entirely recurring revenue |
| GAAP net margin | ~28% | ~29% | **29.5%** | Steadily rising |
| Free cash flow margin | ~40% | ~41% | **~42%** | Among the best in global software |
| ROIC | ~24% | ~26% | **26-33%** | Extremely high capital efficiency |

**A company with $24 billion in revenue whose growth rate is still accelerating and whose margins are still rising - this is extremely rare among large software companies.** Achieving this isn't luck - it's a structural advantage built into the business model itself.

## The depth of the moat: a triple lock-in

Adobe's moat isn't a single dimension - it's a compound barrier formed by three layers of lock-in stacked on top of one another.

### Layer one: skill lock-in

More than **850 million monthly active users** globally (a figure Adobe disclosed in 2025) use Adobe products. Among them, professional creative workers - designers, photographers, video editors, illustrators - have spent years mastering Photoshop's layer operations, Illustrator's pen tool, and Premiere's timeline-editing logic.

These aren't skills you learn in a day - they're **mastered at the level of muscle memory**. Design schools around the world teach Photoshop, not GIMP; film schools teach Premiere, not DaVinci Resolve (though the latter does hold a place in professional film production).

**Switching-cost math**: a mid-sized design team (10 people) moving from the full Adobe suite to alternatives faces a conservative estimate of 3-6 months of transition time, plus a 20-30% drop in production efficiency. For most companies, this hidden cost far exceeds Adobe's subscription fees.

**Counterpoint**: but skill lock-in isn't eternal. If the next generation of designers starts out on Canva or Figma, their "muscle memory" may never have belonged to Adobe in the first place. This is a slow-moving variable on a 5-10 year timescale, but the direction can't be ignored.

### Layer two: file-format lock-in

.psd, .ai, .indd, .pdf - these are the "universal language" of the global creative industry.

- The brand VI source files clients send to design firms default to the .ai format
- The layout files print shops accept default to PDFs exported from .indd
- The layered, editable files photographers deliver default to the .psd format
- Billions of legacy files worldwide can only be perfectly opened and edited by Adobe

**The essence of file-format lock-in is "network standard."** Just as HTTPS is the internet's communication standard and SWIFT is the interbank settlement standard, .psd/.ai/.pdf are the file-exchange standards of the creative industry. Once a standard forms, the cost of replacing it becomes the switching cost of the entire industry, not just a single user's switching cost.

**Counterpoint**: but Figma has already proven that "a new standard can be born" - in the UI/UX space, Figma's cloud-native format has become the de facto standard, and Adobe XD's .xd files have been thoroughly eliminated. If something similar happens in image editing or video, Adobe's format lock-in isn't unbreakable either - it's just that the installed base of legacy files in image and video is far larger than in UI/UX, making migration an order of magnitude harder.

### Layer three: workflow lock-in

This is the layer that's **most easily underestimated** of the three.

A complete creative production process typically looks like this:

```
Concept (Photoshop sketch) -> Vectorize (Illustrator) -> Layout (InDesign) -> Export (Acrobat PDF)
```

Or:

```
Video editing (Premiere Pro) -> Effects compositing (After Effects) -> Color grading (Premiere) -> Audio processing (Audition) -> Output
```

Adobe's Creative Cloud strings these tools together seamlessly - a Smart Object in Photoshop can update live inside Illustrator, and a Premiere timeline can be sent directly to After Effects for effects work and then returned. **Replacing Adobe isn't about replacing one tool - it's about replacing an entire production line.**

Currently no competitor can offer the same depth of end-to-end coverage. Canva can't do professional video editing, Figma can't do page layout, Midjourney can't do effects compositing.

## Pricing power: Adobe's most valuable asset

In 2025, Adobe did something bold: **it restructured Creative Cloud All Apps into two tiers.**

| Plan | Monthly price | Change |
|------|------|------|
| Standard (formerly All Apps) | **$54.99/month** | +16.7% (from $49.99 to $54.99) |
| Pro (new premium tier) | **$69.99/month** | Brand new tier, with more AI features and credits |

**A 16.7% price increase - that's extremely aggressive for a consumer tech product.** Netflix triggers a wave of discussion every time it raises prices by $1-2; Adobe added $5-20 in one move.

The result?

- FY2025 revenue grew **11%** (it rose, not fell)
- Q1 FY2026 subscription revenue grew **13%** (accelerating further)
- No meaningful customer churn appeared

**Being able to raise prices substantially without losing customers - that is pricing power.** In investment analysis, pricing power is one of the ultimate tests of a business's quality. Having pricing power means a company can raise prices to offset inflation and lift margins without depending on user growth.

**Counterpoint**: the negative effects of a price hike may show up with a lag. In March 2026, the FTC settled with Adobe for $150 million over subscription-cancellation disputes (early termination of annual plans required paying 50% of the remaining months' fees, without adequately disclosing this to users) - which suggests some of Adobe's "stickiness" comes from **institutional friction** rather than pure customer loyalty. If regulators force Adobe to simplify its cancellation process, the true voluntary renewal rate could turn out somewhat lower than the headline figures suggest.

## A new "razor-and-blades" model: generative credits

Starting in 2025, Adobe layered a new revenue model on top of its subscriptions - **generative credits**.

| Item | Detail |
|------|------|
| Standard plan | A set amount of complimentary AI generation credits each month |
| Pro plan | More credits + access to more advanced AI models |
| Credit-pack purchase | **$19.99 for 4,000 credits**, renewable once used up |
| Credit uses | AI image generation, AI outpainting, AI style transfer, AI video generation, etc. |

**This is a classic "razor-and-blades" model**: the subscription fee is the "razor" (locks in the user once), and credit consumption is the "blade" (produces ongoing incremental revenue).

The data shows this model is scaling quickly:

- Generative credit consumption **grew more than 45% quarter over quarter** (Q1 FY2026 data)
- Firefly subscription and credit-pack ARR **grew 75% quarter over quarter**
- AI-related ARR **grew more than 3x year over year**

**If this trend continues, Adobe's revenue structure will evolve from "pure subscription" to a dual engine of "subscription plus consumption."** The advantage of consumption-based revenue is a higher ceiling - the more users create and the more AI they use, the more Adobe earns.

**Counterpoint**: consumption-based revenue is less predictable than pure subscriptions. If an economic downturn causes companies to cut creative budgets, credit consumption would likely decline faster than subscription revenue. Also, the marginal cost of AI generation (GPU compute) isn't zero - if credit pricing isn't set carefully, it could erode margins.

## Three places where the moat is being eroded

A deep moat doesn't mean an impregnable one. Adobe's barriers are being nibbled away in three directions:

### Direction one: the non-professional market -> Canva

| Metric | Canva | Adobe Express |
|------|-------|---------------|
| Monthly active users | **180 million** (August 2025) | Not separately disclosed |
| New users added in 2025 | **85 million** | - |
| Revenue growth | **35%** (2025) | - |
| Positioning | Design for non-designers | Lightweight entry point into the Adobe ecosystem |

Canva has already captured the "non-professional design" segment - social media images, simple posters, PowerPoint replacements, and the like. This market originally didn't belong to Adobe (Adobe's tools are too complex for ordinary users), so Canva's rise looks more like **market expansion** than a direct customer grab.

But in 2025 Canva acquired the Affinity suite (Photo/Designer/Publisher) and made it **free** - the first time it has reached into professional territory. If freelancers and small teams leave Adobe as a result, the impact would no longer be merely "marginal."

### Direction two: UI/UX design -> Figma

| Metric | Figma | Adobe XD |
|------|-------|----------|
| UI/UX market share | **80-90%** | Essentially withdrawn |
| 2025 revenue growth | **41%** | - |
| Enterprise design-team usage rate | **67%** | - |
| IPO status | Confidentially filed S-1 | - |

**Adobe has already lost the UI/UX design battle.** In 2022, Adobe tried to acquire Figma for $20 billion, only to be blocked by EU and UK regulators. The consequence of this strategic failure: Adobe XD has effectively stopped competing, and Figma has become the de facto standard for product design.

The good news: UI/UX design is only a subset of the creative market. Adobe's core territory - image editing, video production, layout design, PDF - hasn't yet been touched by Figma.

### Direction three: AI image generation -> Midjourney and others

This is the most ambiguous threat, and the one most worth watching closely. We'll dedicate Part 03 to it.

## "The Visa of the creative industry" - how accurate is this analogy?

Let's use an analogy to understand the essence of Adobe's business model:

| Dimension | Visa/Mastercard | Adobe |
|------|----------------|-------|
| Essence | Payments infrastructure | Creative production infrastructure |
| Revenue model | A cut of every transaction | Monthly subscription + AI credit consumption |
| Network effects | Merchants and cardholders lock each other in | Clients and vendors lock each other in via file formats |
| Switching cost | Extremely high (bank and merchant system integration) | Extremely high (skills, files, workflow) |
| Pricing power | Yes (small annual rate tweaks) | Yes (16.7% price hike in 2025) |
| Profit margin | Net margin ~50% | Net margin ~30%, FCF margin ~42% |
| Cyclicality | Tied to consumer spending | Tied to demand for creative content |

Where the analogy holds: Adobe and Visa are both "infrastructure taxes" - users don't use them because they love them, but because the entire industry is built around them, so there's no real alternative.

Where the analogy breaks down: **Visa's network effect is two-sided and extremely strong** (more merchants -> more cardholders -> more merchants), whereas Adobe's network effect is weaker, relying more on file-format standards and skill lock-in. If file formats were ever displaced by an open standard (the way .doc was displaced by .docx), Adobe's lock-in strength would weaken.

## Business-model summary

Compressing the analysis above into one table:

| Dimension | Assessment | Key evidence |
|------|------|---------|
| Switching cost | **Extremely strong** | Skill lock-in + file format + workflow, three layers stacked |
| Pricing power | **Strong** | 2025 price hike of 16.7%, with revenue growth actually accelerating |
| Recurring revenue | **Extremely strong** | Subscription share >93%, ARR of $25.2 billion |
| New growth engine | **Early traction** | Generative credit consumption up 45%+ quarter over quarter, AI ARR up 3x+ year over year |
| Moat erosion | **Present but manageable** | Canva (non-professional) and Figma (UI/UX) each hold a piece, but the core territory remains intact |
| Biggest variable | **Long-term AI evolution** | If AI redefines creative workflows, the moat could be undermined from within |

**Conclusion: this is a well-running money-printing machine.** The triple lock-in effect, plus pricing power, plus the subscription model, plus the new AI engine, together make up one of the best business models in global software.

But this machine isn't without risk - **AI could be the lubricant that makes it run faster, or the explosive that scraps it.** That's exactly the subject of the next piece.
