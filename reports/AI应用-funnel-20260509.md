## Layer 3: Detailed Analysis (the 8 companies that passed the coarse screen)

### 3.1 Microsoft, MSFT (~$3.6 trillion)

**Business model**: A hybrid of subscription SaaS giant + public cloud + AI Copilot monetization. Microsoft 365 Commercial adds $5-10/seat/month for Copilot pricing, on top of the Azure OpenAI service (billed by token).
**Financial quality**: Revenue growth ~15%, net margin ~36%, free cash flow near $80 billion/year. Incremental AI ARR estimated to exceed $20 billion.
**Moat (★★★★★)**: ① Extremely deep enterprise workflow embedding (Excel/Outlook/Teams); ② an installed base of 2 billion+ seats is a distribution-channel moat; ③ capital and technology ties to OpenAI; ④ Azure is OpenAI's preferred training cloud.
**Risks**: ① Instability in the OpenAI relationship (precedent of governance turmoil); ② antitrust regulation; ③ resistance to Copilot's standalone $30/seat pricing, with adoption slower than expected.
**Valuation quick take**: PE 33, PEG ~1.8 — neutral to rich relative to other cloud giants, but with the highest degree of realized AI monetization.

### 3.2 Alphabet, GOOG (~$2.4 trillion)

**Business model**: Search advertising (~56% of revenue) + YouTube + Google Cloud + Android + AI (Gemini/Workspace AI).
**Financial quality**: Search cash flow is still expanding, cloud has turned profitable. Overall net margin ~28%, OCF > NI.
**Moat (★★★★★)**: The search-box traffic gateway, massive first-party data, in-house TPU chips, the Gemini model family.
**Risks**: ① AI search (Perplexity/ChatGPT search) is siphoning off a portion of query share; ② Search AI Overview may dilute ad CPC; ③ EU antitrust action.
**Valuation quick take**: PE 22 is the cheapest among the tech giants, reflecting a discount for "AI disrupting search." If search monetization transitions smoothly, this is the most undervalued name in the AI application sector.

### 3.3 Meta, META (~$1.5 trillion)

**Business model**: Advertising (>97% of revenue) + Reality Labs. AI mainly empowers ad ranking/targeting/creative generation, monetizing indirectly.
**Financial quality**: The advertising business's net margin keeps rising; AI investment has driven capex higher (estimated $70-90 billion/year).
**Moat (★★★★☆)**: Network effects from 3 billion+ users, advertiser switching costs.
**Risks**: ① Reality Labs continues to lose money; ② whether the massive capex has reasonable ROI remains to be verified; ③ TikTok competition has not gone away.
**Valuation quick take**: PE ~24, PEG ~1.4 — broadly neutral. That AI has lifted Meta's ad ROI by 20-30% is a fact already confirmed by its earnings reports.

### 3.4 Adobe, ADBE (~$240 billion)

**Business model**: Three subscription businesses — Creative, Document, and Marketing Cloud. Firefly (generative image/video) is already embedded in Photoshop/Premiere/Express; Acrobat AI Assistant is a separately priced add-on subscription.
**Financial quality**: Revenue growth 10-12%, net margin 30%+, ROE 35%+, free cash flow $8-9 billion.
**Moat (★★★★★)**: ① The creative workflow (PSD/AI file formats) is almost the sole industry standard; ② high switching costs to replace the subscription; ③ has acquired Frame.io, Substance, and Marketo to form a full-stack suite.
**Risks**: ① Midjourney, Runway, and Canva (with AI) are eroding light users; ② generative AI is "democratizing" creativity, and whether this erodes Adobe's user stickiness is the biggest uncertainty.
**Valuation quick take**: PE 28, PEG ~1.7 — the valuation has actually been suppressed during the AI transition period (the market fears disruption), making this a potential "wrongly punished" name.

### 3.5 AppLovin, APP (~$130 billion)

**Business model**: Mobile advertising platform + the AXON AI engine, pay-for-performance programmatic advertising. Has divested its own games business.
**Financial quality**: Revenue growth 30-50% (unusually steep after the AI engine upgrade), net margin 30%+, extremely high ROE (small net assets + high profit).
**Moat (★★★★☆)**: ① AXON's model is trained on massive advertising-effectiveness data, making it hard for competitors to catch up; ② already the platform of choice for small and mid-sized developers.
**Risks**: ① advertiser budgets are highly cyclical; ② Apple ATT/privacy policy; ③ market debate over whether the "AI dividend" is a one-time event.
**Valuation quick take**: PE 38, PEG ~1.0 — a reasonable-to-low valuation for a company growing 30%+ (last year's rally was enormous; this PEG level was reached only after this year's pullback).

### 3.6 10jqka / THS (~RMB 70 billion)

**Business model**: Financial data + securities trading software, C-end subscriptions + B-end institutional clients. Wencai's large model is embedded, and institutional willingness to pay is high.
**Financial quality**: Net margin 35%+, ROE 28%, almost no debt.
**Moat (★★★★☆)**: Near-monopoly among retail-investor tools in the A-share market; a duopoly with Wind on the institutional side via iFinD.
**Risks**: ① retail-investor activity is strongly cyclical; ② large models are lowering the barrier to financial Q&A, raising the long-term question of disruption by general-purpose LLMs.
**Valuation quick take**: PE 35 sits above the historical median; 2026 A-share trading activity is driving EPS growth, with a PEG of 1.0-1.2.

### 3.7 Salesforce, CRM (~$270 billion)

**Business model**: CRM SaaS leader + Data Cloud + Agentforce (AI Agent).
**Financial quality**: Revenue growth has slowed to 8-10%, net margin ~17%, ROE 12% (weighed down by heavy M&A goodwill).
**Moat (★★★★☆)**: High degree of CRM process embedding, but HubSpot/Microsoft Dynamics keep eating into the mid-market.
**Risks**: ① Agentforce is still early-stage, and whether the $2-per-agent pricing can scale remains to be verified; ② large-customer growth slowed last year.
**Valuation quick take**: PE 38 reflects the discount for "old SaaS struggling to reinvent itself"; the AI Agent is the key variable but is not yet a sufficiently safe-margin investment.

### 3.8 Oracle, ORCL (~$500 billion)

**Business model**: Traditional database + OCI cloud + AI training/inference cloud (large deals with OpenAI, xAI).
**Financial quality**: Revenue growth 12-15% (driven by OCI), ROE 80%+ (highly levered), but the debt ratio of 85% already exceeds the threshold.
**Moat (★★★★☆)**: High database migration cost, OCI's AI compute supply advantage (direct NVIDIA procurement, rapid power infrastructure buildout).
**Risks**: ① mismatch between the capex on large AI deals and their ROI (large orders but long payback); ② the debt ratio is too high, making it sensitive to interest rates.
**Valuation quick take**: PE 32 reflects an "old tree, new shoots" narrative; the stock has already risen 80%+ over the past 18 months, narrowing the margin of safety.

### Final Pick ①: Microsoft (MSFT, NASDAQ)

#### Duan Yongping's Perspective: Nature of the Business

Microsoft is a hybrid of subscription SaaS + public cloud + AI price add-ons. There are three AI monetization paths:

1. **Copilot add-on subscription** ($30/month/seat added on top of M365 Commercial, E3/E5), a pure subscription extension with 70%+ gross margin.
2. **GitHub Copilot** ($10-39/month/seat), a highly sticky subscription targeting developers.
3. **Azure OpenAI Service** (billed by token), an application-layer monetization within "selling shovels," with a gross margin of about 50-60% (dragged down by GPU depreciation).

Duan Yongping asks "what is a good business" — the answer is a business with pricing power, a high repeat-purchase rate, and no need for continuous heavy capital investment. Of Microsoft's three paths, only ① and ② fit perfectly; ③ is "a heavy-capital business it has no choice but to run in order to control the distribution channel." Overall, though, a subscription base with $70 billion+ in ARR makes Microsoft the best "cash cow + growth" combination in AI applications.

**Repeat-purchase rate**: 95%+ M365 enterprise renewal rate — consumer-product-level stickiness.
**Per-unit price**: Copilot has lifted M365 ARPU from ~$22 to ~$50 (for E5+Copilot users), doubling per-seat revenue.
**Duan Yongping's score**: B+. "This is a good business, but not as good as Coca-Cola — because the Azure piece is burning a lot of cash."

#### Buffett's Perspective: Moat

| Moat | Strength | Evidence |
|--------|------|------|
| Brand/pricing power | ★★★★★ | The $30 Copilot add-on has been accepted by enterprises |
| Switching costs | ★★★★★ | Decades of Excel/Outlook/Teams workflow embedding |
| Network effects | ★★★★ | Teams + Copilot deepening collaboration |
| Scale effects | ★★★★★ | Azure spans 60+ global regions, with exclusive compute partnership with OpenAI |
| Technology/licensing barriers | ★★★★ | Capital ties to OpenAI (49% profit share up to a cap), in-house Phi models |

**Will the moat still be there in 10 years?** Most likely yes. Office's enterprise workflow embedding is 30 years in the making, and AI makes it harder, not easier, to replace (because Copilot learns from an enterprise's own data). But watch: ① a rupture in the OpenAI relationship would cost Microsoft its model differentiation; ② Google Workspace + Gemini penetrating small businesses/education; ③ open-source models potentially lowering the cost for enterprises to build in-house.

**Can it still make money once models are commoditized?** The key question. If everyone eventually gets access to an equivalent GPT-5, Microsoft's differentiation lies in: ① a data flywheel (Copilot uses an enterprise's own knowledge graph); ② the distribution channel; ③ continued pricing power. Even with model commoditization, the three-layer moat of distribution + channel + data should still support a subscription premium. But pricing power could be compressed by 30-50%.

#### Munger's Perspective: Risks

1. **Instability in the OpenAI relationship**: The governance standoff between Sam Altman and Microsoft is a source of black-swan risk. The rise of Anthropic + AWS and xAI + Oracle undermines Microsoft's "exclusive AI model" narrative.
2. **Risk of being directly displaced by foundation-model companies**: Low. Microsoft is itself one of the foundation-model companies (OpenAI investment, in-house Phi, acquisition of the Inflection team).
3. **Regulation**: The FTC and EU antitrust authorities are already eyeing Microsoft's "bundled sales" and "AI investment compliance." The Copilot bundled-pricing strategy could be forced to unbundle.
4. **Capex running out of control**: Annualized capex of about $80 billion (including Azure); if AI demand slows, severe depreciation drag will follow.
5. **Why smart people wouldn't buy it**: Because PE 33 is not cheap, and growth has already slowed from ~25% for cloud to ~15%, putting it in the "great company, mediocre price" zone.

#### Li Lu's Perspective: Civilizational-Level Trends

Li Lu emphasizes "judging whether it will still be around in 20 years." Microsoft's dual moat (the Windows operating system + the Office productivity suite) has already survived four paradigm shifts — PC, internet, mobile, and cloud — and AI is the fifth. Historically:

- It did not win on mobile (its phone OS lost to iOS/Android), but it protected its core productivity business by making Office cross-platform.
- It won second place in the cloud (behind only AWS), and in the AI era it could catch up or even overtake.

AI is a paradigm that "amplifies the value of existing channels," not one that "disrupts channels" — at least at the application layer. Microsoft is the player that "can't help but benefit" under this paradigm.

**Civilizational positioning**: ★★★★★. AI expands the overall market for enterprise productivity tools, and Microsoft is both an infrastructure provider and an end-application provider — a double winner that is "selling shovels and selling the mine."

#### Overall Recommendation: ★★★★

- Moat (★★★★★) + AI monetization realization (★★★★★) + valuation (★★★) = overall ★★★★
- Not ★★★★★ because the combination of PE 33 and 15% growth means the 5-10 year expected compound return won't be especially high (annualized 12-15%).

#### Trading Suggestions

- **Buy zone**: PE < 30 (corresponds to a market cap of ~$3.3 trillion, possibly reachable on a near-term pullback)
- **Add-on zone**: PE < 25 (on a deep pullback)
- **Trim zone**: PE > 40 (too expensive)
- **Core-position candidate**: Yes. The most certain name in the AI application sector.

### Final Pick ②: Adobe (ADBE, NASDAQ)

#### Duan Yongping's Perspective: Nature of the Business

Adobe is an "infrastructure-type SaaS" for the creative workflow. When 90% of a business's customers are in a state of "must use it, and forget about switching once they've bought in," that's a good business.

1. **Creative Cloud** (Photoshop/Illustrator/Premiere/Lightroom, etc.): 60% of revenue. Firefly is embedded in these tools, raising the value of use through "generative features."
2. **Document Cloud** (Acrobat, Sign): 20% of revenue. Acrobat AI Assistant is a separately priced $5-10/month add-on.
3. **Digital Experience Cloud** (Marketo, Adobe Experience Manager, etc.): 20% of revenue, serving B2B marketing automation.

**Duan Yongping's core question**: Why do customers renew? Because .PSD/.AI file formats are the industry standard, and leaving Adobe means abandoning a decade of past work. AI doesn't let customers "escape" — it makes "staying" with Adobe more comfortable.

But there's a counter-argument too: **generative AI is drastically lowering the barrier to design, which could dilute the professional-user base** — things that used to require Photoshop can now also be done with Canva + Midjourney. Adobe's moat is in the professional market, but the incremental market (light creators) is being carved up by AI-native tools.

**Repeat-purchase rate**: ~85% annual renewal for Creative Cloud individual plans, 95%+ for enterprise plans.
**Duan Yongping's score**: A-. "This is a consumer-product-level good business, but you have to worry about new entrants picking off the light-user tail."

#### Buffett's Perspective: Moat

| Moat | Strength | Evidence |
|--------|------|------|
| Brand/pricing power | ★★★★★ | "Photoshop it" has become a verb; the Firefly add-on subscription has been accepted |
| Switching costs | ★★★★★ | File formats + learning curve + team collaboration workflow |
| Network effects | ★★★ | Designer community + tutorial ecosystem |
| Scale effects | ★★★★ | Marketing and education partnerships across 30+ countries |
| Technology/licensing barriers | ★★★★ | Firefly's commercially safe training corpus (trained only on Adobe Stock data, eliminating copyright risk) |

**Will the moat still be there in 10 years?** Moderately likely, but with uncertainty.

For: ① PSD/AI file formats have already become the de facto industry standard; ② professional designers' tool habits are extremely stable; ③ Adobe has acquired Frame.io (video collaboration), attempted Figma (blocked by antitrust, but signaling intent), and Substance (3D materials), building out a complete ecosystem.

Against: ① Canva's monthly active users already exceed 150 million, and it embeds Stable Diffusion/Midjourney-style AI tools; ② Figma has already beaten Adobe XD in the UX/UI design space; ③ CapCut/Runway are rapidly siphoning off share in video.

**Can it still make money once models are commoditized?** The key question. Adobe's differentiation has to shift from "model quality" to "workflow embedding + commercial safety." Firefly's positioning around "commercial indemnification" is exactly the kind of differentiation Buffett would appreciate most (enterprise customers care most about risk).

#### Munger's Perspective: Risks

1. **Disruption of the light-user market by AI-native tools**: The biggest risk. Canva + Midjourney + Runway let "non-professionals produce professional-looking work," which could erode the base of the professional market long term.
2. **Risk of being directly displaced by foundation-model companies**: Moderate. OpenAI's Sora and Google's Imagen/Veo target end users directly — but these tools don't yet have a "workflow"; they are currently point capabilities.
3. **Aftereffects of the failed Figma acquisition**: Adobe lost the high ground in UI/UX design and must now catch up through in-house development.
4. **Broad enterprise SaaS slowdown**: When customer budgets are tight, Adobe's "add-on pricing" is among the easiest line items to cut.
5. **Why smart people wouldn't buy it**: ① The stock has been flat to declining over the past 24 months (high opportunity cost); ② the market fears generative AI will disrupt Adobe's core value.

#### Li Lu's Perspective: Civilizational-Level Trends

Creative tools are the "amplifiers" of human civilization — text (Word), image (Photoshop), video (Premiere), music, 3D. Each time a new medium emerges, the tool vendors serving it have enjoyed a wave of dividends.

The essence of AI is to lower the creative barrier for every medium by another order of magnitude. This is a double-edged sword for Adobe:
- The upside: Adobe has a "full-media tool lineup," and AI expands the market for every one of its product lines;
- The downside: AI also makes "creating without Adobe" a reality, which could dilute Adobe's tool-market standing over the long run.

Looking at the 10-20-year horizon, Li Lu would ask: "What is the ultimate form of creative work?" — most likely "natural-language instruction + AI generation + manual fine-tuning." Adobe must hold its workflow position in the "manual fine-tuning" step. Judging by Firefly's "Generative Fill" feature inside Photoshop, Adobe is treating AI as an extension of the tool rather than a replacement for it — this is the right direction.

**Civilizational positioning**: ★★★★. Creative tools serve a civilizational-level need, but Adobe is not the only beneficiary, and it faces long-term decentralization challenges.

- Points added: valuation is the cheapest among SaaS giants; points deducted: the "disruption" narrative is depressing market sentiment.

### Final Pick ③: AppLovin (APP, NASDAQ)

#### Duan Yongping's Perspective: Nature of the Business

AppLovin is a "tool-type platform that monetizes advertising via AI." Business model:
1. **AppDiscovery** (70%+ of revenue): mobile app developers acquire users by advertising through AppLovin, paying for performance (CPI/CPM). The AXON AI engine optimizes bidding, targeting, and creative.
2. **In-app-store ad placements** (10%).
3. **MAX mediation** (10%): developers connect to multiple ad networks through MAX.
4. **DSP** (connecting TV/online): a new business, launched starting in 2025.

Duan Yongping likes businesses where "the customer comes back on their own." AppLovin's customers (mobile game/app developers) come back not out of loyalty but because **AXON delivers a higher ROI than Meta's or Google's UAC**. This is naked "ROI-driven repeat purchase" — the upside is a stable customer source, the downside is no emotional stickiness: the moment performance dips, customers leave immediately.

**Core data point**: After AXON 2.0 launched (2024), customer ad ROAS rose 30-50%, driving a steep acceleration in AppLovin's revenue. This is a real, sustainable piece of business value.

**Duan Yongping's score**: B+. "This is a good, efficiency-driven business, but it doesn't have the emotional stickiness of Adobe or Microsoft."

#### Buffett's Perspective: Moat

| Moat | Strength | Evidence |
|--------|------|------|
| Brand/pricing power | ★★★ | Programmatic advertising carries no brand premium |
| Switching costs | ★★★ | Ad creative and audience data accumulate on the platform |
| Network effects | ★★★★ | Data flywheel: the more advertisers, the more training data for AXON, the higher the efficiency |
| Scale effects | ★★★★★ | The advertising-effectiveness data needed to train the model has a scale threshold |
| Technology/licensing barriers | ★★★★ | The AXON model is the core IP |

**Will the moat still be there in 10 years?** Moderate uncertainty.

For: ① the data flywheel genuinely exists; ② customer switching costs are moderate (retraining a model takes 3-6 months); ③ the DSP business is expanding into TV/online advertising, lifting the addressable market from about $100 billion (mobile app advertising) to about $500 billion (all programmatic advertising).

Against: ① Meta/Google are doing the same thing with AI; ② AXON's lead window may be only 2-3 years; ③ privacy policy (Apple ATT third-party tracking) continues to tighten.

**Can it still make money once models are commoditized?** Not necessarily. If Meta or Google upgrade their own ad engines to match AXON's level, AppLovin will quickly lose its differentiation. This is exactly the type of moat Buffett would be **wary** of — a technology-lead moat that's easy for capital to catch up on.

#### Munger's Perspective: Risks

1. **The AI dividend is a one-time event**: The ROAS lift delivered by AXON 2.0 is a one-time upgrade dividend; growth in 2027-2028 could slow sharply. This is exactly the kind of "mean-reverting" risk Munger watches for most.
2. **Apple's privacy policy**: iOS ATT has already dealt a sustained blow to third-party ad attribution; if it tightens further (e.g., fully disabling IDFA), AppLovin's business model would take a serious hit.
3. **Customer concentration**: A high share of mobile gaming customers, and the gaming industry itself is highly cyclical. AppLovin has expanded into TV advertising via DSP to diversify, but effectiveness remains to be proven.
4. **Risk of being directly displaced by foundation-model companies**: Low. Meta/Google won't enter the third-party advertising platform market (they have their own traffic), but they may make their own ad systems more efficient, indirectly squeezing AppLovin's share of customer budgets.
5. **Accounting controversy**: In 2025, short-selling firms (Fuzzy Panda, Culper) questioned its attribution methodology and app-store "traffic arbitrage" practices.
6. **Why smart people wouldn't buy it**: ① the stock has risen 5-8x over the past 24 months, so it's no longer an early-stage opportunity; ② short-seller reports have caused valuation volatility; ③ debate over the sustainability of a one-time AI dividend.

#### Li Lu's Perspective: Civilizational-Level Trends

Advertising is the oldest form of digital-economy monetization, and the evolution from search → social → programmatic → AI-driven is a clear progression. The essential proposition of AI-era advertising is "achieving higher conversion efficiency with less exposure" — a civilizational-level "efficiency improvement" trend.

But Li Lu would caution: **the winner won't necessarily be today's leader**. Trade Desk, Meta, and Google all have comparable AI advertising capabilities, and who ultimately wins the AI advertising era is uncertain. AppLovin is the current leader, but whether it can hold that position for 10 years is questionable.

**Civilizational positioning**: ★★★. AI advertising is a genuine trend, and AppLovin is currently its sharpest executor, but it is not the most certain winner.

#### Overall Recommendation: ★★★

- Moat (★★★★) + AI monetization (★★★★★) + valuation (★★★, PEG 1.0 but PE 38 in absolute terms) = overall ★★★
- Points deducted: ① cyclicality risk; ② one-time dividend; ③ short-seller controversy not fully resolved.

### 5.2 Historical analogies

**Lessons from the dot-com bubble (1999-2002)**:
- The hot "internet application" names of that era (Yahoo, Pets.com, Webvan) mostly went to zero;
- The real winners were players with "an existing business model amplified by the internet" (Amazon went from bookstore to everything-store, Apple moved from Mac to iPod/iPhone);
- **Lesson 1**: Pure AI-native companies won't necessarily all win; old giants with existing business models are actually the beneficiaries.
- **Lesson 2**: The real entry point comes only after the bubble bursts (Amazon fell 90% in 2001-2002 before delivering a subsequent 100-fold return).

**Lessons from the SaaS golden age (2010-2020)**:
- Whoever turned "software" into "subscription" first, won;
- The AI application layer is currently doing the same thing — turning "subscription" into "AI add-on subscription" — the logic is identical.
- **Lesson**: Degree of embedding + subscription depth determines AI monetization capability; Microsoft and Adobe are the biggest beneficiaries.
