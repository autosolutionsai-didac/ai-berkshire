# AI Has Blown a Hole in Big Tech's Fortress Wall

There's a question I've turned over in my mind for a long time: in the internet era, startups actually had it harder and harder. From 2015 to 2023, the number of newly minted unicorns in China's internet sector declined year after year, and the US saw a similar trend. Platform giants kept getting bigger, and "winner take all" became almost a law of nature.

But once the large-model era arrived, I started noticing some anomalies. Midjourney's team is about 100 people, generating $500 million in annual revenue — $4.7 million in revenue per employee, 2.6 times Google's figure. Cursor's team is under 50 people, yet it has put Microsoft's GitHub Copilot on the defensive in the developer-tools race. Perplexity is valued at over $10 billion and is challenging Google Search head-on. DeepSeek, using perhaps only a tenth of Google's resources, trained a model that rivals GPT-4.

This isn't an isolated phenomenon — it's a signal of some structural shift.

I've tried to think this through clearly: What's the underlying logic behind this shift? Will it persist? Are big companies truly powerless against it?

## The Internet Era: Why Small Companies Kept Losing Ground

To understand the opportunity in the AI era, you first have to understand why small companies couldn't win in the internet era.

**The core reason: big companies monopolized the "means of production."**

The business model of internet platforms is essentially a flywheel: the platform acquires users → users generate behavioral data → the data trains recommendation/search/advertising models → the models improve user experience and ad efficiency → which attracts more users. The faster this flywheel spins, the harder it becomes for latecomers to break in.

Take search as an example: Google processes 8.5 billion search queries every day. Every search, every click, every instance of "the user searched for A but clicked the third result instead of the first" is training data. A startup, even with a better algorithm, simply can't get search quality up without this data. Microsoft poured billions of dollars into Bing, yet its market share has stayed stuck in the single digits.

E-commerce is the same story. Taobao holds billions of records of product data and user browsing and purchase behavior, and that data makes its recommendation system ever more precise. A new e-commerce platform, in its cold-start phase, has none of that data, so its recommendations are naturally worse, the user experience suffers, users don't stick around, and the data pool stays even smaller — a negative spiral.

Social networks have even stronger network effects. All your friends are on WeChat, so you simply can't move to a different messaging app. This isn't a question of whether the product is good — it's a question of switching cost.

So the internet era's "moat formula" can be summarized as: **massive user base → massive proprietary data → better models → better experience → more users**. Once this cycle is established, it is almost unbreakable.

Small companies are structurally disadvantaged in this game. Unless you can find an entirely new category (the way Douyin found short video), the odds of winning a head-on fight within an existing category are extremely low.

## What Large Models Changed

With the arrival of large models, every link in the "moat formula" above began to change.

### Change One: Knowledge Got Democratized

In the internet era, the core moat was **models driven by proprietary data**. Without that data, you couldn't train that model.

The pretraining stage of large models did something unprecedented: **it absorbed nearly all of the public information on the internet into the model.** Wikipedia, academic papers, code repositories, news, books, forum discussions — trillions of words' worth of knowledge got compressed into the model's parameters.

Then open source happened. DeepSeek open-sourced V3 and R1, Meta open-sourced the Llama series, Alibaba open-sourced the Qwen series. This means that **a 10-person startup team can obtain, for free, a model that cost hundreds of millions of dollars to train and that embodies most of humanity's public knowledge.**

This would have been unimaginable in the internet era. You could never have gotten Google to open-source its search ranking model to you, or gotten Taobao to share its recommendation algorithm along with the training data.

But in the large-model era, this is exactly what's happening.

Does this mean the data moat has vanished entirely? Not quite. Big companies still hold enormous amounts of proprietary data — transaction records, social graphs, browsing behavior. But the key shift is: **for most AI applications, the type of data that's needed has changed.**

A traditional search engine needs billions of search logs to optimize ranking. But an AI coding assistant needs high-quality code-completion interaction data, and an AI legal assistant needs legal case data and user consultation data. This kind of **domain-specific interaction data has to be accumulated from scratch, and big companies have no first-mover advantage here.**

Cursor's coding data, Perplexity's search-conversation data, Midjourney's image-generation preference data — all of this was accumulated by these companies themselves through product iteration. Google has 8.5 billion traditional search queries, but it doesn't have conversational search-interaction data like Perplexity's.

**The data moat hasn't disappeared, but the "starting line" of the moat has been reset.**

### Change Two: The Exponential Collapse in Inference Cost

Some might say: even if the model is open source, running it is still expensive. Can a startup afford the cost of large-scale inference?

Two years ago this was indeed a real problem. But inference costs have fallen far faster than most people expected.

In early 2023, GPT-4's inference cost was roughly $60 per million output tokens. By the end of 2025, DeepSeek V3 delivered comparable capability at a cost of under $1. **In two years, the inference cost for equivalent capability fell by more than 98%.**

This trend is still continuing. On the hardware side, each new generation of Nvidia GPUs boosts inference efficiency by 2 to 3 times. On the software side, techniques like quantization, speculative decoding, and model distillation keep pushing costs down. Open-source inference frameworks (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, and others) let even small teams deploy models efficiently.

Big companies do have a genuine cost advantage at hyperscale inference — Google has its own TPUs, Amazon has its Trainium chips. But the key point is: **the vast majority of AI applications don't need hyperscale inference.** A vertical AI product serving 10,000 enterprise customers doesn't need Google-grade infrastructure. Inference cost has already fallen to the point where it's "no longer a bottleneck for startups."

### Change Three: The "Double Multiplier Effect" of Small Teams

This is the change I think is most underrated.

AI isn't just a startup's "product direction" — it's also a startup's "productivity tool." An AI-native startup team can use AI to write code, do design, run data analysis, handle customer service, and generate content. **A 20-person startup in the AI era may have the productive output of a 200-person company from the traditional era.**

This creates a "double multiplier": AI is both the product you sell to customers and the tool you use internally to boost efficiency.

Big companies are, of course, also using AI tools. But the way big companies use AI is "additive" — bolting an AI step onto an existing process. The way startups use AI is "native" — the entire workflow is designed around AI from the ground up.

The distinction is similar to: a traditional automaker bolting a motor onto a gasoline car to make a hybrid, versus Tesla designing the chassis from the ground up for pure electric. Additive retrofits can never match the efficiency of native design.

**AI cannot eliminate the coordination costs of a large organization.** A 100-person team needs management layers, cross-department coordination, processes and approvals. That coordination overhead doesn't vanish just because everyone is using AI. But a 20-person team never needed any of that in the first place — AI directly amplifies each person's output, with no organizational friction eating into the gains.

Midjourney's case is very telling: a team of about 100 people, no outside investors, no sales team, no marketing department, $500 million in annual revenue, revenue per employee 2.6 times Google's. Even back in 2023, when it had just crossed $200 million in revenue, the team was only 40 people. This kind of efficiency could not have happened in any prior technology era.

## The Strongest Counterarguments, and Honest Responses

Granting that the arguments above hold, there are several counterarguments I think carry real weight, and they deserve a direct response.

### Counterargument One: Distribution Channels Are Still in Big Tech's Hands

This is the strongest counterargument.

Google can slot Gemini into Search (2 billion users a day), Microsoft can slot Copilot into Office (400 million paying users), and Apple can build AI features into every iPhone. Startups have no such distribution power.

This objection is correct. The distribution advantage is real, and it's very powerful in the short run.

But history teaches a consistent lesson: **bundled distribution can bring in initial users, but it can't retain them if the product isn't the best one available.**

Microsoft had Internet Explorer preinstalled on every computer, but Chrome still won. Microsoft had Bing built into Windows, but Google Search still won. Google had Hangouts integrated into Gmail and Android, but WhatsApp still won.

Why? Because when users choose between "good enough" and "the best," they tend to pick the best. When the switching cost to an alternative is low enough, the advantage of bundled distribution gets swamped by the gap in product quality.

The switching cost for AI products is usually low. Moving from Google Search to Perplexity just takes changing a bookmark. Moving from GitHub Copilot to Cursor just takes downloading a new editor. It's not like migrating away from WeChat to another chat app — you don't need to carry your social graph with you.

**More importantly, startups shouldn't compete within big companies' existing scenarios — they should create new ones.** Cursor isn't building a better autocomplete plugin for VS Code — it redefined the entire "AI-assisted coding" workflow. Perplexity isn't building a better search engine — it created a new category: "conversational information retrieval."

Once you create a new category, big companies' distribution advantage stops being so useful, because their distribution channels were built for the old category.

### Counterargument Two: Network Effects Haven't Disappeared

Some argue that AI products also have network effects: more users → more interaction data → better models → more users attracted. Isn't that exactly the same as the internet era?

The difference lies in the **strength and shape** of the network effect.

A social network's network effect is "exponential" — network value is proportional to the square of the number of users (Metcalfe's Law). If you have 10 friends on WeChat, WeChat's value to you is 100; if you have 100 friends on WeChat, the value is 10,000. This kind of network effect is almost impossible to break.

The data network effect of AI products is **"logarithmic"** — the marginal value of the first 1,000 data points is far greater than the marginal value of the 10,000,000th to 10,001,000th data points. Model training exhibits diminishing marginal returns.

This means the difficulty of catching up as a latecomer is much smaller than in the social-network race. You don't need 8.5 billion search records like Google — you might only need 100 million high-quality data points to reach 90% of the effect. And in a vertical domain, you might only need 1 million domain-specific data points to be enough.

**The network effects of the AI era are weaker, slower, and easier to catch up to.** That's good news for startups.

### Counterargument Three: Big Companies Are Also Going All-In on AI, and They Have the Talent and Resource Advantage

That's true — Google, Microsoft, Meta, and ByteDance are all betting heavily on AI. They have the world's top research teams, near-unlimited compute budgets, and ready-made engineering infrastructure.

But there's a structural contradiction here that they cannot escape — **the innovator's dilemma.**

Google Search contributes about $175 billion in ad revenue every year. If Gemini gives users direct answers and they stop clicking on search results, what happens to that ad revenue? It's not that Google doesn't know AI search is better — it's that Google can't let AI search be too good, because that would cannibalize its own core business.

Microsoft Office generates more than $50 billion a year from enterprise subscription fees. If Copilot can genuinely automate 80% of Office tasks, will companies still need that many Office seats? Microsoft faces a bind: make Copilot too capable and it may reduce demand for Office; make Copilot not capable enough and it loses to startups.

This isn't a matter of capability — it's a matter of incentive structure. It's not that big-company executives aren't smart; it's that their KPIs and organizational incentive mechanisms dictate that they must protect existing revenue first. **In a scenario where innovation could disrupt one's own core business, the rational choice for a big company is precisely to play it conservative.**

Perplexity, by contrast, has no search-ad revenue to protect, and Cursor has no IDE licensing fees to preserve. They can go all-in on building the best possible product without worrying about "self-disruption."

This contradiction is especially acute in the AI era, because AI threatens not big companies' peripheral businesses but their core cash cows.

### Counterargument Four: Enterprise Customers Trust Big Brands More

In the enterprise market, customers really do tend to favor big companies. The old saying "nobody ever got fired for buying IBM" still carries some weight in the AI era. Compliance requirements, service guarantees, brand trust — these are all advantages that big companies have.

But two things have changed here:

First, AI technology is too new for big companies to have established a reputation for "reliability" in AI applications. When Google's Bard (now called Gemini) launched, factual errors caused the stock to plunge. Microsoft's Copilot has gotten decidedly mixed reviews in the enterprise. Big brands have no inherent trust advantage when it comes to AI.

Second, more and more enterprise customers are realizing that in AI, the "best product" often comes from a startup rather than a big company. Cursor has a better reputation among programmers than GitHub Copilot. Jasper has built a stronger brand in marketing content generation than Microsoft has. **Trust follows product quality, not company size.**

Of course, in heavily regulated industries (healthcare, finance, government), big companies' compliance capabilities are a genuine advantage. Startups need more time to build trust in these areas. This is a question of startups needing to carefully choose their entry point, not a fundamental barrier.

## Special Considerations in the Chinese Context

The analysis above is mainly framed from a global perspective. China has some special circumstances worth discussing separately.

**Chip export bans have, if anything, spurred efficiency innovation.** US export restrictions on chips to China have left Chinese AI companies unable to get their hands on the most advanced GPUs. But this constraint gave rise to efficiency miracles like DeepSeek — training models on par with international top performers using less compute and smarter algorithms. Constraints breeding creativity is a pattern that recurs throughout the history of technology.

**China's internet-platform landscape is loosening up.** WeChat's monopoly on social has been breached by Douyin, and Taobao's dominance in e-commerce has been challenged by Pinduoduo. Competition among the big platforms weakens any single platform's control and also gives AI startups more channel options.

**But China's regulatory environment also places tighter constraints on AI startups.** Content moderation, data compliance, model registration — these compliance costs are a significant burden for small companies. That said, they also create a certain barrier to entry, which in turn protects startups that have already completed the compliance process.

## So, Which Kind of AI Startup Has the Best Odds

None of the above is meant to suggest that "any random AI startup can win." Quite the opposite — startups need to be very clear about where their structural advantages actually lie, avoid big companies' strengths, and amplify their own.

**First, build at the application layer, not the foundation-model layer.** Training foundation models genuinely requires huge capital and an intense concentration of top talent. The application layer doesn't. The competitive factors at the application layer are a deep understanding of user needs, domain expertise in a vertical, and extremely fast product iteration — all of which are strengths of startups.

**Second, create new workflows rather than optimizing old ones.** Don't build "a better Google Search" — build "conversational information retrieval." Don't build "better code completion" — build "an AI-native coding environment." Don't build "a better slide-deck tool" — build "an automated pipeline from data to insight to presentation." A new workflow neutralizes both big companies' distribution advantage and users' inertia.

**Third, pick a vertical instead of a general-purpose platform.** General platforms are big companies' home turf. But in verticals like law, healthcare, architectural design, supply-chain management, and education, big companies lack domain expertise and lack the motivation to go deep into these "small" markets. A vertical AI product needs only the domain-specific data and a deep understanding of users' workflows to build an advantage big companies can't easily replicate.

**Fourth, arm your own team with AI and keep it ruthlessly lean.** Don't expand the team prematurely. Midjourney's story — about 100 people generating $500 million in revenue, $4.7 million in revenue per employee — shows that in the AI era, a team doesn't need to be big, it needs to be sharp. Every additional person adds a unit of coordination cost. Push AI tools to their absolute limit and let every person operate as "an army of one."

## An Honest Conclusion

My core judgment is this: **in the AI era, the structural environment startups face is far better than it was in the internet era.** Not because big companies have gotten weaker, but because three underlying conditions have changed:

First, open-source large models have democratized baseline capability, sharply narrowing the gap between startups and big companies at the starting line.

Second, AI tools empower small teams far more than they empower large ones, because small teams don't suffer from organizational-friction losses — producing a "double multiplier effect."

Third, the threat AI poses to big companies' core businesses has made the innovator's dilemma sharper than ever before. The more successful a big company is, the more painful self-disruption becomes for it.

But I wouldn't say "big companies are doomed to fail." At the infrastructure layer, in general-purpose platforms, in heavily regulated industries, and in scenarios that require hyperscale distribution, big companies still hold enormous advantages.

The real picture is this: **AI has opened a window, and within that window, small companies have an unprecedented opportunity to build their own moats — through better products, faster iteration, and deeper domain knowledge.** That window won't stay open forever. Once the industry landscape settles, big companies will come roaring back with their capital and distribution advantages.

So for AI founders, the key question isn't "whether there's an opportunity" — the opportunity is structural, and it genuinely exists. The key is **speed**: can you, within the window, build something that big companies can't buy even with money — a deep understanding of a vertical, a base of highly sticky user relationships, a culture of rapid product iteration.

None of that can be bought with money, and none of it can be produced by throwing more people at the problem. Only a small, focused team can pull it off.
