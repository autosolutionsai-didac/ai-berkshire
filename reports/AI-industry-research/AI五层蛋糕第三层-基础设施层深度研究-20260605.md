# The AI Five-Layer Cake, Layer Three: $725 Billion Is Being Poured In — Who's Catching It?

> Research date: June 5, 2026
> Coverage: 10 core infrastructure-layer companies
> Data sources: company filings (SEC 8-K/10-Q), sell-side research, industry databases

---

## I. Core logic: where does the four major cloud providers' $725 billion flow?

### 1. An unprecedented scale of capital expenditure

In 2026, the five hyperscale cloud providers (Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle) are expected to spend a combined $725 billion in capital expenditure, up 64% year-over-year from $443 billion in 2025, and nearly triple the $256 billion spent in 2024.

Breaking it down: Amazon leads with roughly $200 billion, followed closely by Google at $175-185 billion, Microsoft above $120 billion, Meta at $115-135 billion, and Oracle at roughly $50 billion. What do these numbers mean? Some hyperscalers' capital intensity (capex/revenue) has already reached 45-57% — historically unheard of, equivalent to plowing back one dollar into infrastructure for every two dollars earned.

Goldman Sachs projects that cumulative hyperscaler capex over 2025-2027 will reach $1.15 trillion, 2.4 times the $477 billion spent over 2022-2024. This is not "cyclical" spending growth — it is a structural paradigm shift.

### 2. Where is the money going?

Of this $725 billion, roughly 75% (about $545 billion) is directed straight at AI infrastructure, flowing into four main areas:

**First, servers and GPU clusters.** This is the biggest slice of the cake. Nvidia's GPUs (GB200/B200) remain in short supply, but server assembly, racking, and power distribution are the real physical bottleneck. Dell shipped $25 billion of AI servers in fiscal 2026, with fiscal 2027 guidance doubling to $50 billion. Celestica, the leading contract manufacturer, has already broken through $4 billion in quarterly revenue.

**Second, networking switches and interconnects.** The core constraint of an AI cluster isn't single-card compute, but the efficiency of inter-card communication. The Ethernet upgrade path from 400G to 800G and on to 1.6T is a rigid requirement. Arista leads in the 800G Ethernet switch market, while Amphenol holds roughly a 33% share in high-speed connectors. Optical modules (Innolight, Coherent) and optical fiber (Corning) form the physical bedrock of network transmission.

**Third, power and power-distribution systems.** A typical AI data center rack's power draw has jumped from a traditional 5-10 kW to 50-120 kW. Power demand across the entire data center is growing multiple times over, and power-distribution units, UPS systems, transformers, and 800V DC systems have become critical bottlenecks. Vertiv and Eaton are the two giants in this lane, with nVent rapidly cutting in on racks and power distribution.

**Fourth, liquid cooling systems.** Once rack power crosses 50 kW, traditional air cooling fails, and liquid cooling becomes the only viable solution at the physical layer. Demand for liquid cooling driven by Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 racks has directly boosted order books at nVent, Vertiv, and others. nVent's organic orders grew 65% in Q3 2025, almost entirely from liquid cooling.

### 3. Why are the leaders already expensive, yet opportunities remain?

The infrastructure layer's investment logic differs fundamentally from the semiconductor layer: the semiconductor layer is winner-take-all (Nvidia holds over 80% of the AI training GPU market), whereas the infrastructure layer is multi-polar — every sub-segment has its own leader, and valuation gaps between those leaders can be enormous.

The main tension in current market pricing: Vertiv trades at roughly 80x P/E, Arista at roughly 58x. Both companies' "AI premium" is already fully priced in, and any quarterly miss could trigger a 20-30% pullback.

But the market is overlooking two facts:

**First, the valuation gap itself creates an arbitrage opportunity.** nVent's business (racks, liquid cooling, power distribution) overlaps heavily with Vertiv's, yet it trades at only 33x forward P/E — less than half of Vertiv's. Eaton trades at roughly 41x P/E, and its data-center power-distribution business is growing no slower than Vertiv's. Innolight trades at a 2026 forecast P/E of just 28x, yet it commands over 40% of the global 800G optical-module market and 50-70% of the 1.6T market.

**Second, "shovel stocks" carry far more certainty than "gold miners."** Regardless of which AI model ultimately wins, data centers will need fiber, connectors, power systems, and liquid cooling. Demand at this layer does not depend on the competitive landscape at the model layer — it depends on hyperscaler capex, and the direction of that capex is already crystal clear.

The core question is: which companies at this layer are truly irreplaceable, and which are merely temporary beneficiaries of the cycle?

### 1. nVent Electric (NVT) — The undervalued liquid-cooling dark horse

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

nVent Electric was spun off from Pentair in 2018 as an electrical connection and protection solutions company, with core products spanning data-center racks, liquid-cooling systems, power distribution, and cable management. The company has completed a strategic pivot from industrial electrical products to data-center infrastructure amid the AI wave, and in 2025 it was added to Nvidia's partner network, directly serving liquid-cooling deployment for GB200 NVL72 racks.

nVent is positioned at the "last meter" of the data center — from in-rack cable management to liquid-cooling piping to power-distribution units. That position looks unglamorous, but once rack power crosses 100 kW, in-rack thermal management and power-distribution design become the actual physical bottleneck.

**Core financials**

- Full-year 2025 revenue: $3.9 billion, up roughly 30% year-over-year (13% organic growth)
- Data-center revenue: roughly $1.0 billion (about 26% of the total), up 67% from $600 million in 2024
- Q1 2026 revenue: $1.24 billion, up 53.5% year-over-year (strong organic growth)
- Backlog: $2.6 billion, an all-time high, up more than 3x year-over-year
- Full-year 2025 gross margin: 37.7%; Q1 2026 gross margin: 35.9% (down 2.9 percentage points year-over-year)
- Current market cap: roughly $26.3 billion
- Forward P/E: roughly 34x
- Source: SEC 8-K (Q1 2026), company filings

**Irreplaceability analysis**

nVent's distinguishing feature is its "Nvidia certification." Being added to Nvidia's partner network means that when hyperscalers procure GB200 racks, nVent's liquid-cooling solution is part of the "reference design." This certification barrier will be hard to break in the near term. In addition, nVent built and rapidly ramped a new liquid-cooling plant in Minnesota in 2025, giving it a lead in capacity expansion speed.

That said, it must be acknowledged that nVent's moat isn't deep. Liquid-cooling technology itself isn't especially complex (cold-plate liquid cooling comes down to precision manufacturing and systems integration), and competitors like Vertiv and CoolIT are all fighting for this market. nVent's edge lies more in "first-mover advantage + certification + delivery speed" than in an insurmountable technical barrier.

**Valuation judgment**

At roughly 34x forward P/E, nVent looks clearly cheaper against Vertiv's 80x and Eaton's 41x. The $2.6 billion backlog gives high revenue visibility for 2026-2027. If liquid-cooling penetration rises from 15% in 2025 to a projected 40% by 2028 as expected, nVent should be able to sustain revenue growth above 30%.

But two risks deserve attention: first, gross-margin compression — Q1 already showed a 2.9-point decline, with material inflation and tariffs (roughly a $90 million negative impact) eating into margins; second, if hyperscalers develop their own in-house liquid-cooling solutions, nVent's value proposition would be significantly diminished.

**Risk factors**

- Gross-margin compression: raw-material cost inflation, tariff impact, fixed-cost dilution during capacity ramp-up
- Intensifying competition: Vertiv, CoolIT, and even in-house liquid-cooling development by hyperscalers
- Customer concentration: rising share of large hyperscaler projects increases dependency

**One-line takeaway:** nVent is the clearest valuation gap in the infrastructure layer — a 34x forward P/E, less than half of Vertiv's, with Nvidia certification and a $2.6 billion backlog providing a margin of safety, but gross-margin trends bear close watching.

### 2. Celestica (CLS) — The biggest winner in AI server contract manufacturing

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Celestica is a Canadian electronics manufacturing services (EMS) provider that builds AI servers and network switches on contract for hyperscalers. In AI infrastructure buildout, Celestica plays the role of "assembler" — putting together complete server racks from Nvidia's GPUs, Broadcom's networking chips, and various thermal and power modules.

That sounds like it carries no technical barrier, but in practice, only a handful of EMS providers can deliver AI servers on time, at quality, and at scale. Celestica's edge lies in its deep ties to a few core hyperscalers (reportedly primarily Google and Meta).

**Core financials**

- Full-year 2025 revenue guidance: $16.0 billion (initial), later raised
- Q1 2026 revenue: $4.05 billion, up 53% year-over-year
- Full-year 2026 revenue guidance raised to: $19.0 billion, adjusted EPS guidance $10.15
- The CCS (Connectivity & Cloud Solutions) segment is the growth engine, with Hardware Platform Solutions revenue up 82% year-over-year (Q2 2025)
- Current market cap: roughly $52.8 billion
- P/E: roughly 47x
- Source: SEC 8-K (Q1 2026), company filings

**Irreplaceability analysis**

The EMS industry's moat is "relationship lock-in" rather than technology. Once a hyperscaler's server design is integrated with Celestica's production lines, the cost of switching suppliers is extremely high (re-certification, production-line retooling, delivery-continuity risk). Celestica's relationships with its core customers have already lasted many years and are unlikely to be replaced in the near term.

But over the long run, EMS pricing power is limited. Celestica is fundamentally paid on a cost-plus basis for labor and materials, with gross margins historically fluctuating between low single digits and the low teens. When AI-server demand growth slows, a price war is inevitable.

**Valuation judgment**

A 47x P/E is rich for an EMS company. Historically, the EMS industry (Flex, Jabil) has traded at a P/E of 10-20x. Celestica commands a premium because the market is pricing it as a "pure AI play" — AI-related business already accounts for more than 60% of the $19.0 billion 2026 revenue guidance.

The question is: once AI capex growth cools from 64% in 2026 to 20-30% in 2027, Celestica's growth rate will decelerate sharply, and the 47x P/E would lose its support.

**Risk factors**

- Extremely high customer concentration: the top two customers may account for more than 50% of revenue
- EMS is inherently low-margin; valuation compression risk is large once growth slows
- Once AI servers become standardized, contract-manufacturing barriers to entry fall

**One-line takeaway:** Celestica is the highest-beta contract-manufacturing play on the current AI capex cycle — 53% revenue growth is striking, but a 47x P/E has already priced in a good chunk of future growth, making it better suited to a trend trade than a long-term hold.

### 3. Innolight (300308.SZ) — The strongest player in the optical-module lane

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Innolight is the global leader in high-speed optical transceiver modules, headquartered in Jinan, Shandong, with production primarily in China. The company is a tier-one supplier to core customers including Nvidia and Google. In AI data centers, high-speed communication between GPUs depends on optical modules — without them, even the most powerful GPU is just an island.

Innolight's core edge lies in commanding over 40% of the global 800G optical-module market and 50-70% of the 1.6T optical-module market. That kind of market position is rare across the entire AI supply chain — almost no other link has a single supplier holding such a high share.

**Core financials**

- Full-year 2025 revenue: 38.24 billion yuan, up 60.25% year-over-year
- Full-year 2025 net profit attributable to shareholders: 10.80 billion yuan, up 108.78% year-over-year, formally joining the "10-billion-yuan net-profit club"
- Q1 2026 revenue: 19.50 billion yuan, up 192% year-over-year
- Q1 2026 net profit attributable to shareholders: 5.74 billion yuan, up 262% year-over-year
- Gross margin on optical transceiver modules: rose from 34.65% in 2024 to 42.61% in 2025, and further to 46.06% in Q1 2026
- Net margin: 32.40% (Q1 2026)
- Current market cap: roughly 1.29 trillion yuan
- 2026 forecast P/E: roughly 28x (based on forecast net profit of 22.5 billion yuan)
- Source: company annual report (Shenzhen Stock Exchange filing), THS (10jqka)

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Innolight's irreplaceability rests on three pillars: first, scale of production capacity (the world's largest optical-module manufacturing base); second, the pace of technology transitions (maintaining a leading share through the 800G-to-1.6T shift); and third, customer-certification barriers (passing the rigorous certification of top North American hyperscalers takes 2-3 years).

More critically, the competitive landscape for 1.6T optical modules is more concentrated than for 800G. Innolight's 50-70% share in 1.6T means competitors (Coherent, Lumentum, etc.) are clearly lagging on this generation of product.

But the risk is equally clear: geopolitics. If the United States further restricts Chinese optical-module makers' access to the North American supply chain, Innolight's business would take a fatal hit. While this hasn't happened yet, it's a sword of Damocles hanging overhead.

**Valuation judgment**

A 28x forecast P/E is very cheap for a company growing more than 100% with a net margin above 30%. Compared with Coherent's P/E above 150x, Innolight's value proposition stands out sharply.

But this low valuation itself reflects the market's pricing of geopolitical risk. If you can stomach that risk, Innolight may be the best value proposition in the entire AI infrastructure layer.

**Risk factors**

- Geopolitical risk: US-China tech decoupling could directly affect North American customers
- Technology-generation transition risk: competitive dynamics could shift with the next generation of products (3.2T)
- A-share liquidity and sentiment volatility amplification

**One-line takeaway:** Innolight is the "highest-certainty, lowest-valuation" name in the entire infrastructure layer — a 28x P/E buying more than 40% global share and 262% profit growth. If you can bear the geopolitical risk, this is close to a layup.

### 4. Arista Networks (ANET) — The undisputed king of AI network switches

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Arista is the leader in data-center Ethernet switching, holding more than 40% share in high-speed network switching (400G/800G). The core challenge in AI data centers isn't single-card compute — it's efficient communication among thousands of GPUs, and Arista is the key supplier solving exactly that problem.

The company's EOS (Extensible Operating System) is its core technical moat. Unlike Cisco's traditional network architecture, Arista's EOS is a software-defined, programmable network operating system better suited to the dynamic traffic-management needs of AI clusters.

**Core financials**

- Full-year 2025 revenue: $9.0 billion
- Q1 2026 revenue: $2.71 billion, up 35.1% year-over-year
- Full-year 2026 revenue guidance: $11.5 billion, of which roughly $3.5 billion is AI-related
- 800G Ethernet switch port shipments: up 3x quarter-over-quarter in Q2 2025
- Cumulative shipments: 150 million ports
- Current market cap: roughly $150 billion
- P/E: roughly 58x
- Source: SEC 8-K (Q1 2026, Q4 2025), company releases

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Arista's position in the AI Ethernet market is nearly unshakeable. Its EOS operating system, refined over years of iteration, has become the de facto standard in hyperscaler data centers. More importantly, Arista is making a heavy bet on "AI Ethernet" (as distinct from Nvidia's InfiniBand approach), developing XPO and CPO (co-packaged optics) technology aimed at reaching InfiniBand-level performance without sacrificing Ethernet ecosystem compatibility.

If Ethernet ultimately becomes the mainstream approach for AI networking (rather than InfiniBand), Arista would be the biggest beneficiary.

But that is also the biggest risk: if Nvidia's InfiniBand continues to hold the advantage in hyperscale clusters, Arista's AI-networking addressable market would be squeezed.

**Valuation judgment**

A 58x P/E is on the high side within the infrastructure layer, but given Arista's market position and software stickiness, this valuation has some justification. The key variable is the share of AI-networking revenue — if the $3.5 billion of AI revenue in 2026 doubles to $7.0 billion in 2027, the 58x P/E would be supported.

Not cheap, but not outlandish either. Worth watching on any pullback.

**Risk factors**

- InfiniBand competition: Nvidia continues to push its proprietary networking approach
- Elevated valuation: a 58x P/E is highly sensitive to any negative news
- Long-term threat from hyperscalers developing in-house switching chips

**One-line takeaway:** Arista is the "must-have" for AI Ethernet, with a rock-solid market position, but a 58x P/E means "a good company but not cheap" — better to add on dips once Ethernet's dominance in AI clusters becomes clearer.

### 5. Vertiv (VRT) — The data-center power-plus-liquid-cooling leader, but valuation is a problem

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Vertiv is a global leader in critical data-center infrastructure, with products spanning power management (UPS, power distribution), thermal management (precision air conditioning, liquid cooling), and IT management systems. In the AI wave, Vertiv leads the dual "power + cooling" lane.

Power and cooling are the hardest physical bottlenecks in data centers. GPUs can be bought from Nvidia (albeit with a wait), but a data center's power hookup and cooling systems require a 12-24 month construction cycle. Vertiv has the most complete product line and the largest delivery capacity in this lane.

**Core financials**

- Full-year 2025 revenue: roughly $9.5-10.0 billion (estimated from cumulative quarterly figures)
- Q1 2026 revenue: $2.65 billion, up 30% year-over-year (23% organic growth)
- Full-year 2026 revenue guidance: $13.25-13.75 billion (up 27-29% year-over-year)
- Full-year 2026 adjusted EPS guidance: $5.97-6.07 (up 43% year-over-year)
- Q1 2026 adjusted operating margin: 20.8%, up 430 basis points year-over-year
- 2025 acquisitions of PurgeRite (liquid-cooling services, roughly $1.0 billion) and Great Lakes (racks, roughly $200 million)
- Current market cap: roughly $60 billion
- P/E: roughly 80x
- Source: SEC 8-K (Q1 2026, Q4 2025), company filings

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Vertiv's moat lies in its "end-to-end" capability. Most competitors do either power or cooling alone, while Vertiv can offer a one-stop solution spanning UPS, power distribution, liquid cooling, and monitoring. For hyperscalers, reducing the number of suppliers means lowering integration risk and shortening delivery time.

The two 2025 acquisitions further strengthened this capability — PurgeRite filled out liquid-cooling services (installation, maintenance, pipe flushing), while Great Lakes rounded out the rack product line.

But Vertiv's history bears watching: in 2020, short-seller Muddy Waters targeted Vertiv, alleging insufficient financial transparency. Though later disproven, that episode is a reminder for investors to stay cautious about the quality of Vertiv's earnings.

**Valuation judgment**

An 80x P/E is the richest in the entire lineup. Even accounting for 2026 adjusted EPS of $6 and 43% growth, this valuation implies the market expects Vertiv to sustain growth above 30% for the next 3-5 years. Once the capex cycle slows, the downside from an 80x P/E is substantial.

**Risk factors**

- Valuation too high: an 80x P/E leaves very little margin for error on results
- Intensifying competition: Eaton, nVent, Schneider Electric, and others are all fighting for the same slice of the pie
- Acquisition-integration risk: a string of acquisitions could dilute management's focus

**One-line takeaway:** Vertiv is the benchmark for data-center power and liquid cooling with the most complete product line, but an 80x P/E has already priced in the next three years of growth in full — unless you believe AI capex will keep beating expectations, there's no margin of safety at this price.

### 6. Eaton (ETN) — An old-line electrical giant's new data-center story

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Eaton is a century-old electrical-management company with products spanning power distribution, UPS systems, transformers, and circuit breakers. Unlike Vertiv, Eaton's data-center business is just one part of a diversified portfolio (which also includes aerospace, automotive, and industrial segments), but data centers are becoming its fastest-growing engine.

Eaton's core value in data centers lies in power-distribution systems and UPS. As total data-center power rises from tens of megawatts to hundreds of megawatts or even gigawatt scale, the reliability and efficiency of power-distribution systems become critical. Eaton's 800V DC power-distribution solution represents the technical direction for next-generation data centers.

**Core financials**

- Full-year 2025 revenue: $27.4 billion
- Q1 2026 revenue: $7.5 billion, up 17% year-over-year (a quarterly record)
- Full-year 2026 adjusted EPS guidance: $13.00-13.50 (up roughly 10% year-over-year)
- Q1 adjusted EPS: $2.81 (a Q1 record)
- 2026 acquisitions to date: Fibrebond (modular data centers), Resilient Power Systems, Boyd Thermal (liquid cooling), Ultra PCS
- Current market cap: roughly $163.6 billion
- P/E: roughly 41x
- Source: SEC 8-K (Q1 2026), company filings

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Eaton's moat differs from Vertiv's: Vertiv has high "data-center purity," while Eaton has broad "brand + channel + certification." Eaton's power-distribution products carry decades of safety-certification history, making a supplier switch an extremely high-risk proposition for data-center operators.

The recent string of acquisitions (Fibrebond, Boyd Thermal, etc.) shows Eaton accelerating its expansion into data-center liquid cooling and modular solutions, attempting to replicate Vertiv's "end-to-end" model. But integration takes time, and the results remain to be verified.

Another advantage for Eaton is "diversification as a hedge." Even if data-center capex slows, its aerospace and electrification (EV charging infrastructure) businesses can still support growth. This makes Eaton more resilient than Vertiv in a downturn.

Eaton has also announced plans to spin off its Mobility business as a separate public company, sharpening its focus on electrical management and data centers — a clear strategic intent.

**Valuation judgment**

A 41x P/E isn't cheap for a company growing revenue 17% with roughly 10% EPS growth, but given Eaton's diversification and century-old brand premium, this valuation sits in "reasonable but on the pricier side" territory. Compared with Vertiv's 80x, Eaton offers a better risk-reward profile in the data-center power lane.

**Risk factors**

- Growth isn't fast enough: 17% revenue growth is on the slower side among AI-themed names
- Business diversification also means "lower purity," making it less favored by AI-themed funds than a pure play like Vertiv
- The string of acquisitions will take time to integrate and could weigh on margins in the near term

**One-line takeaway:** Eaton represents "buying a top-tier asset at a reasonable price" — a 41x P/E, a century-old brand, diversification as a hedge, and a leading position in data-center power distribution make it better suited to a long-term hold than Vertiv, though its near-term catalysts are weaker than a pure-play stock's.

### 7. Coherent (COHR) — The "aristocrat" of the optical-module lane

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Coherent is a global leader in photonics and laser technology that, through its acquisitions and integration of Finisar and II-VI, has become a full-line supplier across optical modules, optical components, and lasers. In AI data centers, Coherent supplies 800G/1.6T optical transceiver modules along with underlying optical components.

Unlike Innolight's "pure optical module" positioning, Coherent's edge lies in "vertical integration" — end-to-end capability from optical chips (VCSELs, EMLs) to optical components to optical modules. That means Coherent can be self-sufficient on core optical components, unconstrained by upstream supply chains.

**Core financials**

- Fiscal 2025 (ended June 2025) revenue: $5.81 billion, up 23% year-over-year
- Networking segment revenue grew 48%, the fastest-growing segment
- Fiscal Q1 2026 (ended September 2025) revenue: $1.58 billion, up 17% year-over-year
- 1.6T optical transceiver modules: began mass-production shipments in Q4 2025
- March 2026: Nvidia announced a $2.0 billion strategic investment in Coherent
- Current market cap: roughly $70.0-81.0 billion
- Trailing P/E: roughly 150-200x; forward P/E: roughly 48x
- Source: SEC 8-K (FY2025, Q1 FY2026), company announcements

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Nvidia's $2.0 billion strategic investment is the strongest possible endorsement. This investment is not merely financial — it signals that Nvidia has chosen Coherent as a core partner for its next-generation optical-interconnect technology, including CPO and silicon photonics. As AI clusters evolve toward higher bandwidth, the technical barriers at the optical-component layer will keep rising, and Coherent's depth at this layer far exceeds Innolight's.

But Coherent's problem is being "large but unfocused." The company's business also includes industrial lasers, materials processing, and other non-data-center segments, which drag down overall growth. Moreover, in the 800G optical-module market, Coherent's share is far below Innolight's (roughly 15-20% vs. more than 40%).

**Valuation judgment**

A trailing P/E of 150-200x is suffocating, but the forward P/E of roughly 48x is more meaningful (since earnings are in a rapid ramp phase). Nvidia's $2.0 billion investment provides an implicit valuation anchor — Nvidia wouldn't place such a large bet on an overvalued company.

But a 48x forward P/E is still 72% more expensive than Innolight's 28x. Given Innolight's dominant position in 800G/1.6T optical modules, Coherent's valuation premium has to be justified by "vertical integration + Nvidia's endorsement + geopolitical immunity (as a non-Chinese company)."

**Risk factors**

- High business complexity: non-data-center businesses dilute valuation purity
- Optical-module market share trails Innolight
- Integration risk: organizational-efficiency issues following multiple large acquisitions

**One-line takeaway:** Coherent is Nvidia's "hand-picked" optical-interconnect partner with unmatched vertical-integration capability, but a 48x forward P/E is 72% more expensive than Innolight's — if you're avoiding geopolitical risk, Coherent is the alternative, but you'll pay a premium for it.

### 8. Corning (GLW) — The "unavoidable" name in optical fiber

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Corning is a global oligopoly supplier of optical-fiber preforms and fiber-optic cables, occupying an extremely distinctive position in the AI supply chain: no matter how optical-module technology evolves, optical signals must ultimately travel through fiber. Corning is essentially the "toll booth" for fiber.

The company's optical communications segment is its growth engine — 2025 optical communications revenue was $6.3 billion, up 35% year-over-year, with enterprise data-center fiber revenue up 61% year-over-year. In January 2026, Corning signed a $6.0 billion multi-year fiber-supply agreement with Meta, a record for the industry.

**Core financials**

- Full-year 2025 core revenue: $16.41 billion, up 13% year-over-year
- 2025 optical communications segment revenue: $6.3 billion, up 35% year-over-year
- 2025 core EPS: $2.52, up 29% year-over-year
- Q1 2026 core revenue: $4.35 billion, up 18% year-over-year
- Q1 2026 optical communications revenue: roughly $1.85 billion, up 36% year-over-year
- Operating margin reached 20.2%, hitting the "Springboard Plan" target a year ahead of schedule
- Meta's $6.0 billion multi-year contract (signed January 2026)
- Management's target: double revenue to $40 billion by 2030
- Current market cap: roughly $166.0-173.0 billion
- P/E: roughly 96x
- Source: SEC 8-K (Q1 2026), investor-relations announcements

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Corning's position in the optical-fiber-preform market is close to "irreplaceable." The global optical-fiber-preform market is highly concentrated (an oligopoly of Corning, Japan's Shin-Etsu Chemical, and China's Yangtze Optical Fibre and Cable), technical barriers are extremely high (preform formulas and drawing processes require decades of accumulated know-how), and capacity-expansion cycles are long (a new preform production line takes 2-3 years to build).

More importantly, Corning has launched a new generation of high-density fiber-connectivity solutions for AI data centers, working directly with Nvidia and hyperscalers. Jensen Huang has publicly named Corning multiple times as a key partner in AI data-center optical interconnects.

**Valuation judgment**

A 96x P/E looks expensive, but it needs to be understood that Corning's valuation encompasses a large amount of non-data-center business (display, environmental, specialty materials, etc.), which grows slowly but sits on a large base, dragging down overall EPS growth. Looking at the optical communications segment's growth trajectory alone (35-36% growth), the valuation becomes easier to understand.

The question is whether Corning's overall growth rate (18% revenue, 30% EPS) can support a 96x P/E. The 2030 target of doubling revenue to $40 billion implies roughly 20% compound annual growth over the next five years, meaning the current valuation would need a significant combination of "P/E compression + growth" to deliver a reasonable return.

**Risk factors**

- Elevated valuation: a 96x P/E isn't cheap for a company with 18% overall growth
- Non-data-center businesses are a drag: segments like display panels are highly cyclical
- Fiber demand is tightly bound to the data-center construction cycle

**One-line takeaway:** Corning is the most certain name in the "data has to travel through fiber" logic chain — an oligopoly position, Jensen Huang's endorsement, and a $6.0 billion Meta contract — but a 96x P/E has already priced in a great deal of optimism; it's safer to wait for a pullback below 70x.

### 9. Dell (DELL) — Number one in AI server shipments, but margins are the sore spot

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Dell is the world's largest AI server shipper by dollar value. In fiscal 2026 (ended January 2026), Dell's AI-optimized server revenue reached $25 billion, up more than 150% year-over-year. Full-year ISG (Infrastructure Solutions Group) revenue was $60.8 billion, up 40% year-over-year.

Dell's position in the AI supply chain is "channel and integration" — it doesn't make GPUs (Nvidia does), doesn't make networking chips (Broadcom does), and can even outsource server assembly to Celestica, but Dell holds the broadest enterprise-customer relationships and the most complete after-sales service network. For enterprise customers not buying directly from Nvidia, Dell is the go-to channel for purchasing AI servers.

**Core financials**

- Fiscal 2026 ISG revenue: $60.8 billion, up 40% year-over-year
- Fiscal 2026 AI-optimized server revenue: $25 billion, up more than 150% year-over-year
- Fiscal 2026 Q4 AI-server revenue: $9.0 billion (a quarterly record), up 342% year-over-year
- Fiscal 2026 ISG operating income: $7.1 billion, a 14.8% operating margin
- But AI-server operating margin: mid-single digits (roughly 5%), far below traditional servers
- Fiscal 2027 AI-server revenue guidance: roughly $50 billion (a doubling)
- AI-server backlog: $43 billion
- Current market cap: roughly $276 billion
- P/E: roughly 19-33x (varies by source and calculation method)
- Source: SEC 8-K (FY2026 Q4, FY2027 Q1), Futurum Group analysis

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Dell's "irreplaceability" isn't technical — it's about channel. For the vast majority of enterprises (non-hyperscalers) buying servers, Dell is the default choice. That channel advantage, built over 20 years in the traditional server market, is now extending naturally into AI servers.

But Dell's pricing power on AI servers is very limited. The GPUs belong to Nvidia; Dell is just doing assembly, testing, and after-sales support, and its gross-margin room is being heavily squeezed. Management has explicitly stated that AI-server operating margins sit in the "mid-single digits" (roughly 5%), far below the 15%-plus margin on traditional servers.

That means: the more Dell's AI-server revenue grows, the more its overall margin could actually decline. "Revenue growth without profit growth" is the structural contradiction Dell faces.

**Valuation judgment**

A P/E of roughly 19-33x (depending on the calculation method) puts Dell among the cheapest valuations in the infrastructure layer. But the low valuation has its reasons — Dell is essentially a "toll-collector" model, making thin margins on high volume in AI servers.

If one is bullish on Dell, the logic can only be: (1) AI-server penetration accelerates on the enterprise side (spreading from hyperscalers to small and mid-sized businesses), or (2) Dell raises its value-add through software and services (lifting margins).

**Risk factors**

- Low AI-server margins: mid-single-digit operating margins cap profit upside
- GPU supply depends on Nvidia: shipment timing is out of Dell's control
- Declines in the traditional PC and storage businesses could weigh on overall results

**One-line takeaway:** Dell is the undisputed champion of AI-server shipment volume, and its $43 billion backlog secures growth visibility, but mid-single-digit margins mean "impressive activity but thin profit" — the cheap valuation makes sense, and it suits investors seeking certainty over upside more than those chasing leverage.

### 10. Amphenol (APH) — The "hidden champion" of high-speed connectors

**Company overview and bottleneck positioning**

Amphenol is the world's second-largest connector manufacturer (behind only TE Connectivity), but in the high-speed interconnect space for AI data centers, Amphenol is the de facto leader, with roughly 33% market share. The company's products span high-speed copper-cable connectors between GPUs, backplane connectors, and power connectors — components that look unremarkable but are, in fact, the critical physical layer determining AI cluster performance.

In January 2026, Amphenol completed its acquisition of CommScope's Connectivity and Cable Solutions business (roughly $4.1 billion in annual revenue), further cementing its near-monopoly position in data-center fiber and copper connectivity.

**Core financials**

- Full-year 2025 revenue: $23.1 billion, up 52% year-over-year (38% organic growth)
- Q1 2026 revenue: $7.6 billion, up 58% year-over-year (a record)
- Q1 2026 adjusted EPS: $1.06, up 68% year-over-year
- Q1 2026 orders: $9.4 billion (a record), a book-to-bill ratio of 1.24
- IT Datacom segment accounts for roughly 38% of revenue, up 110% year-over-year (Q4 2025)
- Adjusted operating margin: 27.5% (full-year 2025, industry-leading)
- Current market cap: roughly $185 billion
- P/E: roughly 38-43x
- Source: company filings, Seeking Alpha, Simply Wall St

**Irreplaceability analysis**

The connector industry's moat is extremely deep but unglamorous. The reasons: (1) certification barriers — every connector must pass end-customer certification, a 6-12 month process; (2) a high degree of customization — every connector spec inside an Nvidia GB200 rack is different, and once the design is locked in, switching costs are extremely high; (3) small size but high stakes — a high-speed connector might cost only tens of dollars, but if it fails, the entire rack goes down, so customers demand extremely high quality and are reluctant to risk switching suppliers.

Amphenol's 27.5% operating margin is top-tier among industrial companies, a direct reflection of its pricing power and irreplaceability.

**Valuation judgment**

A P/E of 38-43x is medium-to-high within the infrastructure layer, but given Amphenol's growth (58% revenue growth, 68% EPS growth), margins (27.5% operating margin), and market position (33% share), this valuation is better supported than many peers.

Crucially, Amphenol's business is extremely diversified — beyond AI data centers, it also serves automotive, defense, industrial, and mobile-device markets. This diversification provides a buffer in a downturn, similar to Eaton.

The $4.1 billion of revenue from the CommScope acquisition hasn't yet fully flowed through to profit; as integration proceeds, synergies should be realized over 2026-2027.

**Risk factors**

- CommScope integration risk: integrating a $4.1 billion-scale acquisition takes time
- AI data-center connector growth could slow after 2027
- A P/E of 38-43x is at a historical high for a connector company

**One-line takeaway:** Amphenol is, in my view, the "highest-quality" name among these ten companies — a 33% market share, 27.5% operating margin, 1.24 book-to-bill ratio, and diversified business structure — a P/E of 38-43x isn't cheap, but it's the best "shovel stock" you can buy at a reasonable price.

### The three most worth deep research:

**First: Innolight (300308.SZ).** A 28x P/E buying 262% profit growth and more than 40% global share is the most glaring valuation mismatch on this entire list. The only question worth answering is whether you can bear the geopolitical risk. If the answer is yes, this may be the best investment opportunity in the entire AI infrastructure layer.

**Second: Amphenol (APH).** This is the name that best balances "quality" and "price." A 33% market share, a 27.5% operating margin, diversification that provides downside protection, and incremental room for growth from the CommScope acquisition. A P/E of 38-43x isn't a bargain-bin price, but what you're buying is a first-rate business.

**Third: nVent Electric (NVT).** The cheapest valuation among comparable names (less than half of Vertiv's), with Nvidia certification and a $2.6 billion backlog providing certainty. It's the best risk-reward choice in the liquid-cooling lane — provided you can tolerate the short-term pain of gross-margin compression.

### Three names to wait on for a better price:

Vertiv (80x), Corning (96x), and Arista (58x) are all good companies, but their current valuations already fully reflect optimistic expectations. Absent a surprise catalyst, these three are better suited to a "watchlist" than an "immediate buy."

### Names requiring more nuanced treatment:

Dell's shipment volume is staggering but its margins are worrisome, Celestica has high beta but its EMS nature limits long-term value, and Coherent has Nvidia's endorsement but is priced 72% richer than Innolight. The investment case for all three is less than "clean," and each only becomes attractive under specific conditions.

---

**A final word:** The window of opportunity in the infrastructure layer will not stay open forever. Once hyperscaler capex growth cools from 64% in 2026 to a likely 20-30% in 2027, growth rates across every company at this layer will step down. Until then, picking the right names matters more than timing the market.
