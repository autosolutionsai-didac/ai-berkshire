# The First Layer of AI's Five-Layer Cake: Who's Choking Off AI's Power Supply?

*In-depth research report | June 2026*

---

## Core logic: why energy is AI's ultimate bottleneck

Every discussion about AI eventually runs into the same wall: there isn't enough power.

It's not a shortage of chips, not a shortcoming in algorithms, not a lack of data — it's electricity. Physical electricity, from the power plant all the way to the racks inside the data center. This is the most primal, most cumbersome, and most certain bottleneck in the entire AI value chain.

The data in the International Energy Agency's (IEA) 2026 report is startling: global data center electricity consumption is projected to roughly double, from 415 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2024 to 945 TWh by 2030, a compound annual growth rate of about 15% — more than four times the growth rate of electricity consumption across all other industries globally. Electricity consumption by AI-dedicated data centers specifically is growing even faster, expected to triple between 2025 and 2030. In the United States alone, data centers consumed about 4.4% of the nation's electricity in 2023; by 2028 that share is projected to surge to 6.7%-12%. By 2030, U.S. data centers are expected to consume more electricity than the combined total of all other energy-intensive industries — aluminum, steel, cement, chemicals, and the rest.

(Sources: IEA, "Energy and AI," 2026; Brookings Institution, "Global energy demands within the AI regulatory landscape," 2026)

These numbers alone are startling enough. But what should really keep investors up at night isn't the demand growth itself — it's the rigid constraints on the supply side.

Transformers — the most critical, most unglamorous piece of equipment in the power grid — now have lead times that have stretched from 24-30 months before 2019 to 3-5 years today. Average lead times for large power transformers have reached 128 weeks (about 2.5 years); generator step-up (GSU) transformers average 144 weeks (about 2.8 years), with some high-capacity models requiring a 4-year wait. Wood Mackenzie data shows a roughly 30% supply-demand gap for power transformers in the U.S. in 2025, with a 10% gap for distribution transformers as well. Worse still, domestic U.S. manufacturers can meet only 20% of power-transformer demand, leaving 80% dependent on imports.

(Sources: Wood Mackenzie Q2 2025 Survey; PV Magazine, "U.S. transformer market faces severe supply constraints," May 2026; Power Magazine, "Transformers in 2026: Shortage, Scramble, or Self-Inflicted Crisis?," 2026)

Switchgear — the critical equipment connecting data center campuses to the grid — is already sold out through 2028 and beyond. A data center can go from land acquisition to completion in 12-18 months, but grid interconnection can take 4-10 years. What does this mean in practice? It means "having money but still not being able to buy power" is not a figure of speech — it's a fact. 30%-50% of large data centers planned to come online in 2026 will be delayed or canceled, and 11 gigawatts of announced capacity show no sign of breaking ground.

(Sources: World Economic Forum, "Is power grid connectivity the strategic bottleneck for AI?," May 2026; Fortune, "U.S. data center development has hit snags," March 2026)

Facing this dilemma, hyperscale cloud providers are responding aggressively: building their own power plants to bypass the public grid entirely. Amazon and Talen signed an $18 billion direct nuclear-supply agreement. Oracle and Bloom Energy signed a 2.8-gigawatt fuel-cell agreement. Meta and Vistra signed a 2,609-megawatt nuclear power purchase agreement. The underlying logic of all these deals is the same sentence: the grid is too slow, so we'll do it ourselves.

Capital expenditure plans among 51 U.S. utilities have jumped to $1.4 trillion, up 27% year over year. But even with that much money being poured in, equipment lead times, talent shortages, and permitting processes mean grid expansion still can't keep pace with AI's appetite.

(Source: Tech Insider, "US Utilities Plan $1.4T for AI Data Centers: 27% Capex Surge," 2026)

This is why the energy layer is the most certain investment opportunity in AI's five-layer cake. Chips can be replaced (Nvidia's position is not eternal); software can be disrupted (OpenAI's edge today could be gone tomorrow) — but the physical constraints on power infrastructure are rigid. The fact that a transformer takes four years to deliver won't change because of anyone's slide deck. The fact that the grid needs upgrading won't stop just because an AI bubble bursts — because even without AI, the U.S. grid itself is already old enough to need large-scale replacement.

The certainty of this investment thesis comes from the rigidity of demand (AI's power consumption can only grow, not shrink) and the bottleneck on the supply side (equipment lead times measured in years, slow capacity expansion). In the five-layer cake, the closer you get to the "dirty, unglamorous work" at the bottom layer, the less exciting it looks — and the more certain it is.

Below is an analysis of 10 companies that hold the keys to AI's power lifeline.

## 1. AZZ Inc. (AZZ) — Hot-dip galvanizing coatings: the "anti-corrosion layer" for grid steel

**Bottleneck positioning**

What AZZ does is simple: it galvanizes steel. It doesn't sound glamorous, but every transmission tower, every substation support structure, every bridge's steel frame needs hot-dip galvanizing to survive 30-50 years of outdoor service. Grid expansion means more transmission towers and substations, and every single one needs AZZ's services. This is the most unglamorous but least avoidable link in the AI energy chain.

(Source: AZZ FY2026 full-year earnings report, April 22, 2026; StockAnalysis.com)

**Irreplaceability analysis**

AZZ is North America's largest independent post-fabrication hot-dip galvanizing company, operating 42 galvanizing plants. The moat in this business isn't deep technology — it's "cumbersome geographic monopoly": hot-dip galvanizing plants are capital-intensive (a single plant costs tens of millions of dollars to build) with limited service radius (the cost of transporting steel components limits how far a plant can reach), which naturally produces an oligopoly in every regional market. New entrants have to face both high capital expenditure and entrenched local customer relationships that are hard to pry loose. AZZ's network of 42 plants across the country is its moat — dense enough, broad enough, and hard for competitors to pick off one region at a time.

**Valuation assessment**

At a P/E of 12.8x, AZZ is the cheapest name in the entire AI energy chain. With a 22.3% EBITDA margin, steady revenue growth, and ample free cash flow, this valuation looks almost like a giveaway compared with peers. The reason the market gives it such a low valuation is simple: galvanizing isn't exciting, and nobody's going to hype up a stock on a "hot-dip galvanizing" narrative. But that is exactly what a value investor should like: market neglect equals margin of safety.

**Risk factors**

Revenue growth of only 4.6% is not a high-growth story. If steel prices swing sharply or infrastructure investment falls short of expectations, results will suffer. Longer term, if grid expansion slows more than expected (say, if an AI bubble bursting reduces data center demand), AZZ's growth thesis would weaken — but it wouldn't go to zero, since grid-aging replacement demand alone is enough to support the base business.

**Bottom line**: A regional-monopoly hidden champion trading at 13x P/E — the most reasonably valued and most overlooked company in the AI power chain.

## 2. Archrock (AROC) — Natural gas compression services: the "blood pump" of AI power

**Bottleneck positioning**

Natural gas is the largest fuel source for U.S. electricity generation (roughly 40%). Throughout the entire journey from wellhead to power plant, natural gas needs continuous compression to flow through pipelines — that's what Archrock does. Without compression services, natural gas can't leave the well, can't move through the pipeline, and can't reach the power plant. As AI data center electricity consumption surges, demand for gas-fired generation surges with it, and demand for compression services surges along with that. This is the "circulatory system" of the AI energy chain.

**Core financial data**

|------|------|
| Market cap | $6.1 billion |
| Share price | $34.61 |
| P/E (TTM) | 18.7x |
| 2025 full-year revenue | $1.49 billion (+28.7%) |
| 2025 adjusted EBITDA | $900 million |
| 2026 EBITDA guidance | $865-915 million |
| Dividend yield | 2.56% |

(Source: Archrock 2025 full-year and Q1 2026 earnings reports; StockAnalysis.com)

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Archrock is the largest natural gas compression service provider in the United States. Its moat comes from scale and customer stickiness: compression equipment is a heavy asset (a single unit costs millions of dollars), and once installed at a customer's wellsite, switching costs are extremely high. Archrock has roughly 3.6 million active horsepower of compression units, with utilization holding above 95% for 11 consecutive quarters, and 85% of 2026 horsepower already under contract. This "heavy assets + long-term contracts + high switching costs" model naturally forms a moat. The company also further consolidated its market position with its early-2026 acquisition of Total Operations Processing Services (TOPS).

**Valuation assessment**

At a P/E of 18.7x and a forward P/E of 17.7x, Archrock is fairly to reasonably valued among energy service companies. A 2.56% dividend yield provides an extra cushion. The 2026 EBITDA guidance is slightly below 2025 (because 2025 included some one-time gains), but the core business continues to grow. Compared with USA Compression (USAC, P/E 29x), Archrock's valuation is more attractive.

**Risk factors**

Natural gas price volatility affects upstream production, which in turn affects compression service demand. If natural gas prices stay persistently low, some marginal oil and gas wells could be shut in, reducing compression demand. In addition, debt levels are relatively high (long-term debt of $2.4 billion, leverage of 2.6x), which increases pressure on financing costs in a rising-rate environment.

**Bottom line**: The undisputed leader in natural gas compression, trading below 19x P/E with a 2.5% dividend yield — a direct beneficiary of AI power demand, with a reasonable valuation and a solid cushion.

**Bottom line**: Archrock's competitor in the same lane — a 7%+ dividend yield suits income-oriented investors, but the valuation is a bit pricier and the MLP structure adds complexity.

**Bottleneck positioning**

Hubbell sits across the entire chain from grid "upgrade" to "interconnection." Its products cover surge arresters, insulators, connectors, and switches for transmission and distribution grids, as well as wiring systems, power distribution equipment, and lighting inside data centers. In short: everywhere between the substation and the server rack that needs to carry power, there's a Hubbell product. Grid expansion needs Hubbell, and data center construction needs Hubbell too — both demand streams land on the same company.

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Hubbell's moat is "wide and shallow": it isn't the sole supplier of any single product, but its breadth of products across the electrical infrastructure space, the depth of its distribution network, and the density of its customer relationships together form an overall advantage. In Q1 2026, data-center-related revenue surged 40% year over year, while grid infrastructure grew 12%. The company is now entering the 765-kilovolt ultra-high-voltage transmission market, an addressable market worth $1.5 billion over the next decade. Its 18.35% ROIC (well above the industry average of 11.43%) demonstrates efficient capital allocation.

A broad product line means no single catalyst can dramatically drive results. If grid investment or data center construction slows, growth could fall back to low single digits. Larger competitors (Eaton, ABB, Schneider) could squeeze Hubbell's share on the largest projects.

**Bottom line**: An "all-around player" in electrical infrastructure. A P/E of 29x is on the low side among peers, its 18% ROIC demonstrates operating quality, and the dual drivers of data centers and the grid provide growth certainty.

## 5. Vistra (VST) — Nuclear + natural gas generation: powering AI directly

**Bottleneck positioning**

Vistra doesn't sell equipment — it generates power directly. With roughly 50 gigawatts of generating capacity (after completing the Cogentrix acquisition), including nuclear, natural gas, coal, solar, and storage, it is one of the largest competitive power producers in the United States. It sits at the most fundamental link of all — where the electricity actually comes from. Its nuclear assets in particular (Comanche Peak, Perry, and others) have become the object of tech giants' bidding wars: Meta signed a 2,609-megawatt nuclear power purchase agreement, and the Perry nuclear plant received NRC approval to operate through 2046.

**Core financial data**

| Share price | $152.79 |
| P/E (TTM) | 25.8x |
| Forward P/E | 16.6x |

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Nuclear power plants are Vistra's deepest moat. Building a new nuclear plant takes 10-15 years, an NRC license is close to impossible to replicate, and existing nuclear plants have extremely low operating costs, zero carbon emissions, and extremely stable generation output. These characteristics make nuclear the ideal baseload power source for AI data centers. Vistra also holds substantial natural gas generation assets (adding 5,500 megawatts after the Cogentrix acquisition), which can provide peaking capability. The combination of "nuclear for baseload, natural gas for peaking" matches perfectly with data centers' 24/7 power demand.

**Valuation assessment**

A TTM P/E of 25.8x doesn't look cheap, but the forward P/E is only 16.6x, because the 2026 EBITDA guidance ($6.8-7.6 billion) represents a substantial step-up from 2025. From an EV/EBITDA perspective, the current multiple is roughly 7x, which is on the reasonable side among independent power producers. The analyst consensus price target is $225, implying about 47% upside. The current share price ($152) has already pulled back about 30% from its 52-week high ($220), offering a decent entry window.

**Risk factors**

Electricity price volatility (particularly in the ERCOT market) directly affects profits. If natural gas prices fall and drag down power prices, revenue could fall short of expectations even as electricity consumption rises. The Cogentrix acquisition (expected to close in the second half of 2026) carries integration risk. Although nuclear plants have a good safety record, a single accident would be devastating to the stock.

**Bottom line**: The largest competitive power producer in the U.S., trading at a forward P/E of 16.6x. Its nuclear-plus-natural-gas combination perfectly matches AI power demand, and with the stock down about 30% from its high, it's one of the best value-for-money large-cap names in the energy layer.

## 6. Powell Industries (POWL) — Custom medium-voltage switchgear: the "power gatekeeper" of data centers

**Bottleneck positioning**

Every large data center needs medium-voltage switchgear to manage and distribute the power entering the campus. That's exactly what Powell does — custom medium/low-voltage switchgear and power distribution systems. It's called "custom" because every data center's power architecture is different, and standardized products can't meet the need. As switchgear sells out through 2028 and beyond, becoming a physical bottleneck in AI infrastructure, Powell — a specialist in this niche — has seen its order book explode.

**Core financial data**

| Metric | Value |
|------|------|
| Market cap | $11.0 billion |
| Share price | $301.50 |
| P/E (TTM) | 58.9x |
| Forward P/E | 51.9x |

| TTM revenue | $1.13 billion (+4.5%) |
| Backlog | $1.8 billion (+33%) |
| Cash | $501 million |

(Source: Powell FY2025 and Q2 FY2026 earnings reports; StockAnalysis.com)

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Powell's moat lies in "customization capability + engineering experience + customer trust." Medium-voltage switchgear isn't a standardized commodity — it requires custom design and manufacturing for each project's power architecture, with high engineering complexity and long delivery cycles. Powell has accumulated 70 years of experience in this field, and once a customer has qualified it as a supplier, they rarely switch. The company recently landed its largest single order in its history — an AI data center project worth more than $400 million using a "behind-the-meter" design (i.e., self-built generation facilities feeding power directly), which is precisely the emerging trend of hyperscale cloud providers bypassing the grid. Its backlog stands at $1.8 billion (+33%), with new bookings doubling (Q2 FY2026 new orders of $490 million versus $249 million a year earlier) — order visibility is extremely high.

**Valuation assessment**

A P/E of 59x looks expensive by any measure. But the issue is that its $1.8 billion backlog (about 1.6x annual revenue) is still accelerating, which means high certainty of revenue over the next 2-3 years. If you calculate a forward valuation based on revenue and margins once the backlog converts, the P/E could drop to 30-35x. But the market has already priced in this growth to some degree — the stock has risen more than fivefold from its 2024 low ($56) to over $300. At the current price, there isn't much of a margin of safety left to chase.

**Risk factors**

The valuation is extremely high, and if order growth slows or data center construction falls behind schedule, the risk of a share price pullback is significant. The company is relatively small (market cap of $11.0 billion, annual revenue of only $1.13 billion), so changes in orders from a single major customer could cause substantial swings in results. Manufacturing capacity expansion takes time — if demand arrives too quickly and capacity can't keep up, orders could end up going to competitors instead.

**Bottom line**: Sitting right at the bullseye of AI data centers' power bottleneck, with an extremely high certainty of explosive order growth — but a P/E of 59x has already priced in a great deal of optimism, and it's best suited to buying on a pullback.

## 7. NuScale Power (SMR) — The only NRC-certified SMR: a "future bet" on nuclear power

**Bottleneck positioning**

If existing nuclear plants (Vistra, Talen) are the "existing-stock solution" to AI's power needs, NuScale is the "incremental solution." It is the only company in the world to receive U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) standard design approval for a small modular reactor (SMR), with a single-module output of 77 megawatts electric. The selling point of an SMR is that it's smaller, more flexible, faster to build than a traditional nuclear plant, and can be deployed right next to a data center. In theory, it's the ultimate solution for AI data centers to "source power on-site."

**Core financial data**

| Metric | Value |
|------|------|
| Market cap | $4.4 billion |
| Share price | $12.09 |
| P/E | Not applicable (ongoing losses) |
| Q1 2026 revenue | $565,000 (-95.5% year over year) |
| Net loss (TTM) | $386 million |
| EPS (TTM) | -$1.82 |
| Cash and capital resources | ~$1.2 billion |

(Source: NuScale Q1 2026 earnings report; StockAnalysis.com)

**Irreplaceability analysis**

NuScale has exactly one moat: NRC certification. That certification took 15 years and several billion dollars to obtain, and any competitor starting from scratch would need at least 10 years to catch up. But this moat is "conditional" — if NuScale's technology path ultimately proves commercially unviable (too expensive, too slow to build), the certification loses its meaning.

Key progress: the 6-gigawatt deployment plan in partnership with TVA (Tennessee Valley Authority) and ENTRA1 is the largest nuclear deployment project in U.S. history, with a power purchase agreement expected to be finalized by the end of 2026. The RoPower project in Romania (6 modules) is also moving forward. But all of this remains at the "agreement/planning" stage, still a long way from actual revenue contribution.

**Valuation assessment**

A $4.4 billion market cap, less than $20 million in annual revenue, and a TTM loss of $386 million — this is entirely an "option-pricing" story. Buying NuScale isn't buying a company with revenue; it's buying a long-dated option on the thesis that "SMRs become a standard power source for AI data centers." The $1.2 billion cash reserve means the company won't fold in the near term, but the burn rate (roughly $400 million in losses per year) means that if the TVA contract and commercialization don't go smoothly, it will likely need to raise capital again in 2-3 years, and equity dilution at that point would be unavoidable.

**Risk factors**

Extremely high risk. No stable revenue, with the valuation entirely propped up by one or two flagship projects. The construction cost and timeline for SMRs could, in practice, run far over expectations (the cancellation of the UAMPS project is a cautionary tale). At the current burn rate, $1.2 billion in cash lasts only about 3 years. Even though the technology path has NRC certification, there remains a huge gap between certification and mass commercialization. Beta of 2.22 — extremely high volatility.

**Bottom line**: The purest play on the AI-nuclear narrative, but essentially a high-priced option — if TVA's 6GW project comes through, the stock could multiply several times over; if the project is delayed or canceled, most of the $4.4 billion market cap could evaporate.

## 8. Talen Energy (TLN) — Nuclear plant direct-supplying AWS: a "dedicated power plant" for AI data centers

**Bottleneck positioning**

Talen did something that shook the entire power industry: it sold 1,920 megawatts of capacity from its Susquehanna nuclear plant (2.5 gigawatts, two units) directly to AWS, signing a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA) through 2042 with a total contract value of about $18 billion. This pioneered an entirely new model — "nuclear plant to data center" direct connection. Talen is no longer a traditional power utility in the conventional sense — it's more like a platform building custom power solutions for hyperscale cloud providers.

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Talen's moat is a triple lock of "location + contract + assets." The Susquehanna nuclear plant sits right next to a PJM grid hub, and AWS is already building a cluster of data centers nearby. The $18 billion long-term PPA (through 2042) provides extremely high revenue certainty, with contracted price escalation of 2% annually starting in 2028. Once fully delivered, by 2030-2032, it could generate about $1.4 billion in annual revenue. Based on 2026 FCF guidance of $5.0-5.5 billion, the current market cap implies roughly 47-52x P/FCF, which is indeed not cheap. The analyst price target of $1,216 (+27%) reflects a high growth expectation from the market.

The issue is: GE Vernova's growth logic is extremely clear (transformer shortage + gas turbine demand + grid upgrades), but the current share price ($961) has already fully priced in these positives. Any execution shortfall below expectations could trigger a significant pullback.

**Risk factors**

An elevated valuation is the biggest risk. Integrating Prolec will take time and could weigh on margins in the near term. If AI infrastructure investment slows due to macroeconomic or policy factors, the extremely long order backlog could face cancellation or delay risk. The company only spun off from GE as an independent entity in 2024, so it has a short operating history as a standalone company.

**Bottom line**: The most comprehensive "super arms dealer" in the AI energy chain. Its $163.0 billion backlog provides extremely strong revenue certainty, but at a $258.2 billion market cap trading above 50x forward P/E, it requires balancing growth validation against valuation.

## 10. Bloom Energy (BE) — Fuel cells powering data centers directly: the "ultimate solution" for bypassing the grid

**Bottleneck positioning**

If grid interconnection takes 4-10 years, why not just bypass the grid entirely? Bloom Energy's answer is: solid oxide fuel cells (SOFC), installed directly next to the data center, generating power from natural gas or hydrogen, ready to use as soon as they're installed. The lethal appeal of this approach is speed — fuel cells can be deployed in months, while traditional grid expansion takes years. Oracle (2.8 gigawatts), AEP ($2.65 billion), AWS, CoreWeave, and Equinix have all already signed contracts.

**Core financial data**

| Share price | $292 |
| P/E (TTM) | ~12,259x (near-zero profit) |

| Total backlog | ~$20.0 billion |
| Beta | 3.75 |

(Source: Bloom Energy Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 earnings reports; StockAnalysis.com; 24/7 Wall St)

**Irreplaceability analysis**

Bloom Energy's moat is its lead in this particular technology path. Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFC) aren't a new concept, but Bloom is the only company in the world able to commercialize them at scale. Its products can run on natural gas, biogas, and hydrogen, with a future path to a smooth transition to zero-carbon fuels. Fast deployment (months rather than years), a small footprint, and high operating efficiency (electrical efficiency of about 60%, well above gas turbines' 30-40%) make it the top choice as "emergency power" for hyperscale cloud providers.

Its $20.0 billion total backlog ($6.0 billion products + $14.0 billion services) and Brookfield's $5.0 billion financing framework validate confidence from top-tier customers. If Oracle's 2.8-gigawatt order is fully executed, that single customer alone could support years of revenue.

**Valuation assessment**

An $83.0 billion market cap against TTM net income of just $6 million and a forward P/E of 126x — by any standard, this is "faith-based pricing." The market isn't pricing Bloom Energy on today's profits, but on the prospect that "fuel cells become the standard power source for AI data centers."

It's worth noting that the average analyst price target is $263, below the current share price of $292. That means mainstream Wall Street analysts believe the current stock price is already expensive. With a beta of 3.75, this stock's volatility is nearly four times the market's — it soars when it rises (from a 52-week low of $20 to $323, a 15x gain) and could fall just as brutally.

**Risk factors**

Valuation is the biggest risk — an $83.0 billion market cap with almost no profit support. The long-term reliability and maintenance costs of fuel cells could surface problems once deployed at large scale. If natural gas prices rise sharply, the economics of fuel-cell power generation would deteriorate. Competitive risk: if traditional generation solutions (gas turbines, diesel generators) catch up in deployment speed, or if grid expansion accelerates, Bloom's "bypass the grid" narrative would be undermined. Technology substitution risk: next-generation energy storage technologies (such as long-duration storage) could offer another way to bypass the grid.

**Bottom line**: The most aggressive "disruptor" in the AI energy layer. Its $20.0 billion backlog and 130% revenue growth are impressive, but its $83.0 billion market cap has almost no profit to back it up — a high-payoff but extremely high-risk bet.

**The three most reasonably valued:**

1. AZZ (P/E 13x): The lowest valuation, the most stable business, and the most overlooked. 1. GE Vernova: A $163.0 billion backlog, full-chain coverage capability, and the Prolec transformer acquisition make it the core arms dealer of the AI energy chain. Its valuation is elevated, but it's the best entry point for understanding the entire AI energy bottleneck.
