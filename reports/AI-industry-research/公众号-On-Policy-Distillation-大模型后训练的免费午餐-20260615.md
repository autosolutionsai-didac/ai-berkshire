# Training large models for 10x less cost with better results — how?

In 2025, training a model on par with GPT-4 cost several million dollars in compute for the reinforcement learning stage alone. Over the past six months, leading models like Qwen3, Gemma 2, and MiMo have all independently switched to a new method — same results, compute cost cut by 90%.

This isn't clickbait. Thinking Machines Lab published measured data on the AIME 2024 math competition: the new method used 1,800 GPU-hours to reach 74.4% accuracy, while traditional reinforcement learning used 17,920 GPU-hours to reach only 67.6%.

**Cost down tenfold, performance actually better.** In machine learning, this is about as close as it gets to a "free lunch."

This method is called On-Policy Distillation (OPD for short). It comes from a paper published by Google DeepMind at ICLR in early 2024 — "On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes."

Below we break down three things: what OPD actually does, why it works so well, and which companies are already using it.

---

## The dilemma of large-model post-training

To understand OPD, you first need to understand the problem it's trying to solve.

Large model training happens in two stages: pre-training and post-training. Pre-training learns the basic patterns of language from massive amounts of text; post-training makes the model smarter and more obedient. Today we're discussing post-training.

Post-training currently has two mainstream methods, each with a fatal weakness.

**Method one: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**

You prepare a batch of high-quality "question-answer" pairs and have the model imitate them. For example, showing the model 100 math problems with standard worked solutions and having it mimic them.

The problem: the model learns from the teacher's text, but at inference time it generates its own text. These two distributions differ. It's like reading 100 perfect-score essays but writing something completely different from them — the mistakes you're prone to making never show up in the perfect-score essays. The technical term is "train-inference distribution mismatch," and it's the most fundamental bottleneck of supervised fine-tuning.

**Method two: Reinforcement Learning (RL)**

The model generates its own answer, and it's only told whether the final result is right or wrong.

The advantage is obvious: the model learns on text it generated itself, so there's no distribution mismatch. But the disadvantage is equally obvious — if a math problem takes 50 steps and you only say "wrong," the model has to guess on its own which step went bad. This is called "sparse reward." Learning anything from so little information requires massive sampling and highly complex optimization algorithms, making training expensive and unstable.

![GKD outperforms traditional methods across all three tasks](../../assets/opd/fig1-gkd-performance.png)

Laying out the traits of both methods in a table, the gap becomes obvious:

| Method | Sampling | Reward signal |
|------|--------|--------|
| Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) | Off-policy (text comes from teacher/expert) | Dense (token-by-token supervision) |
| Reinforcement Learning (RL) | On-policy (text generated by the student) | Sparse (only final-outcome feedback) |
| **OPD (this article's focus)** | **On-policy (text generated by the student)** | **Dense (token-by-token teacher feedback)** |

OPD's approach: let the model learn on its own text while providing precise feedback at every single step. Sounds like eating from two cakes at once? Exactly — that's why it's called a "free lunch."

## Core idea: the student does the work, the teacher grades it step by step

Back to a more intuitive analogy.

Suppose you're a new hire and the company has assigned you a senior mentor. The traditional training approach (SFT) is: the mentor writes the model answer, and you copy it out. Reinforcement learning is: you do the work yourself, and once you're done you're only told "pass" or "fail."

OPD's approach is entirely different: **you do the work your own way, and then the mentor annotates your work line by line** — "this step's thinking was right," "this step started going off track," "this choice right here directly caused the later mistake."

The key difference: the teacher is annotating something the student actually wrote, not a model answer the student would never have written in a million years.

Concretely, the training process has four steps:

**Step one: the student generates its own text.** Give the student model a question and have it write a complete answer according to its current ability. These answers carry the student's own "accent" and "flaws" — which is exactly what we want.

**Step two: the teacher scores token by token.** Hand the student's answer to the teacher model, and the teacher gives its own probability distribution over every single token the student wrote. This step is computationally cheap — it only requires a single forward pass, no sampling needed. This is also one of the key reasons OPD is cheaper than reinforcement learning: RL requires repeated sampling and evaluation, while the teacher only needs to "read" the student's answer once.

**Step three: compute the token-by-token gap.** At each token position, compare the difference between the teacher's and the student's probability distributions. This difference is the dense reward signal — it can tell the student precisely: at token position 37, you made a bad choice, and the teacher would have gone in a completely different direction at this spot.

**Step four: update the student model.** Use these token-by-token signals to update the student's parameters so it makes better choices in similar positions next time.

## How should the teacher "grade"?

This section gets a bit into the technical weeds. If you want to skip the equations, just read the "plain-language translation" after each one — it won't affect your understanding of what follows.

OPD uses a metric called KL divergence to measure the "gap between teacher and student." But KL divergence has two directions, and which one you choose matters enormously.

**Forward KL divergence** (used by traditional distillation):

$$D_{KL}(P_{teacher} \| P_{student}) = \sum_x P_{teacher}(x) \cdot \log \frac{P_{teacher}(x)}{P_{student}(x)}$$

Plain language: every topic the teacher might test, you must review. Every token the teacher considers possible, the student can't miss a single one.

Effect: the student's output ends up spread thin and conservative — touching everything a little but mastering nothing. The technical term is "mode covering."

**Reverse KL divergence** (used by OPD):

$$D_{KL}(P_{student} \| P_{teacher}) = \sum_x P_{student}(x) \cdot \log \frac{P_{student}(x)}{P_{teacher}(x)}$$

Plain language: every topic you review must be correct, but you don't have to cover every possible topic. Whatever direction the student picks must be one the teacher also endorses.

Effect: the student concentrates its effort on mastering one optimal strategy rather than spreading itself across every possibility. The technical term is "mode seeking."

![Quality-diversity trade-off between forward KL and reverse KL](../../assets/opd/fig2-kl-tradeoff.png)

Why is reverse KL better suited to distilling large models? Because we don't need the small model to learn everything the large model can do (that's impossible) — we need the small model to do its own best at whatever it can do. Reverse KL specifically encourages this "specialize" rather than "cover everything" learning strategy.

There's also a beautiful coincidence here (which is actually a mathematical necessity): computing reverse KL requires sampling on the student's own outputs — it mathematically requires the "student to do the work itself." Forward KL, by contrast, only needs the teacher's samples, which is why traditional distillation never needed the student to generate its own text.

The core design of "the student learns on its own homework" isn't just intuitively sensible — it's a mathematical requirement baked into the loss function itself. Form and objective are perfectly self-consistent.

**From an information-theoretic angle:** in reinforcement learning, the model generates a response of several hundred tokens and gets back a single score at the end — each iteration yields only O(1) bits of information. In OPD, the teacher provides a full probability distribution at every single token — if the response has N tokens, each iteration yields O(N) bits of information. It's like grading an exam: reinforcement learning only writes a total score at the bottom of the page, while OPD writes annotations next to every single question. The information gap spans two to three orders of magnitude.

This is the fundamental reason OPD's data efficiency is so high.

## GKD: one knob, from traditional distillation all the way to OPD

The paper proposes a specific algorithm called GKD (Generalized Knowledge Distillation), a unified framework. Its core is a single parameter λ:

- **λ = 0**: learning entirely on the teacher's text → degenerates into traditional distillation
- **λ = 1**: learning entirely on the student's own text → pure OPD
- **0 < λ < 1**: a mix of both types of data

![GKD algorithm pseudocode](../../assets/opd/fig3-algorithm1.png)

The engineering implementation is highly intuitive: at each training step, use the current student model to generate a response, have the teacher model score that response token by token, compute the gap, and update the student's parameters. The whole pipeline is nearly as simple as ordinary fine-tuning — the only extra overhead is an added "student sampling" step.

The paper found that the closer λ is to 1, the better the results — in other words, the student learns far better on its own text than on the teacher's text.

## Experimental results: let the data speak

The paper ran experiments across three very different tasks; GKD's improvement over traditional distillation ranged from 1.7x to 2.1x — not a few percentage points, but more than double.

| Task | Dataset | Improvement over traditional distillation |
|------|--------|----------------|
| Summarization | XSum | 2.1x |
| Machine translation | WMT | 1.7x |
| Math reasoning | GSM8K | 1.9x |

![GSM8K math reasoning comparison, OPD outperforms all methods](../../assets/opd/fig4-gsm8k.png)

But the most striking finding was about data efficiency:

![Data efficiency comparison — GKD with 5% of the data beats traditional distillation with 100% of the data](../../assets/opd/fig5-data-efficiency.png)

> **GKD using just 5% of the data beat traditional distillation using 100% of the data.**

If you had 100,000 training examples, running all of them through traditional distillation would still underperform running just 5,000 through GKD.

The paper also has a blockbuster experiment: a small model with only 77 million parameters, distilled via GKD, beat the 540-billion-parameter PaLM on multiple tasks — a 7,000x gap in size, yet the smaller model performed better.

![MMLU/BBH general-capability comparison](../../assets/opd/fig6-mmlu-bbh.png)

It's not that small models can't learn — it's that the previous teaching method wasn't good enough.

## Industrial deployment: from paper to production

First, a set of real-world numbers:

| Method | GPU-hours | AIME'24 accuracy |
|------|---------|---------------|
| Traditional reinforcement learning | 17,920 | 67.6% |
| OPD | 1,800 | 74.4% |

This is a real measurement from Thinking Machines Lab (October 2025) on the American Invitational Mathematics Examination. Cost cut by nearly tenfold, accuracy up by nearly 7 percentage points.

Adoption across the industry has been extremely fast:

**Google Gemma 2 (August 2024):** the paper authors' own "home-grown" application — Google's own open-source model directly applied this method.

**Qwen3 (May 2025):** Alibaba's Tongyi Qianwen was the first to systematically lay out OPD's industrial-scale practice in its technical report. It explicitly stated that OPD requires only one-tenth of the GPU time of a full reinforcement-learning pipeline to reach comparable or even better results.

**MiMo-V2-Flash (January 2026):** validated OPD's effectiveness in multimodal settings, showing this method isn't limited to pure text.

**GLM-5:** Zhipu also adopted a similar on-policy distillation technique in its new-generation model.

From an academic paper in 2024 to a mainstream post-training paradigm by 2026, OPD's industrialization speed has been remarkably fast.

## OPD's limitations and future

OPD has one structural precondition: **you need a sufficiently strong teacher model.** In reinforcement learning, the reward signal can come from a rule-based verifier (for example, directly checking whether a math answer is correct) and doesn't necessarily require a larger language model. But in OPD, the teacher must be a model clearly stronger than the student on the target task.

This points to two of the most notable directions for future evolution:

**Hybrid OPD + RL training.** Qwen3's practice has already hinted at this path — first use OPD to quickly build up baseline ability (since it's cheap and stable), then use RL to fine-tune along specific dimensions. This could become the standard post-training pipeline going forward.

**Distillation of reasoning models.** With the emergence of reasoning models like o1/o3, OPD offers a very promising framework for how to efficiently distill long-chain reasoning ability into smaller models. DeepSeek-R1 has already demonstrated the enormous potential of distilling from a large reasoning model to a smaller one.

Beyond that, self-distillation (having a model be its own teacher) and multi-teacher fusion are also directions worth watching, though they remain in early exploration.

---

## Conclusion

OPD's core insight is actually quite simple: it's far more effective to have a student learn from its own mistakes than to have it copy someone else's correct answers. It's just that "learning from your own mistakes" requires a good teacher's step-by-step guidance, rather than simply being told a final grade.

When you put this simple insight into a rigorous mathematical framework (reverse KL divergence + on-policy sampling), you get a method that's an order of magnitude cheaper — and performs better to boot.

> The second half of the large-model race may no longer be about who throws in more money, but about whose training method is smarter. OPD is the opening shot of this shift — but definitely not the last.
