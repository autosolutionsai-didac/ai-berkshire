> Research date: June 5, 2026
> Data as of: June 3, 2026
> Disclaimer: This report is personal investment research only and does not constitute investment advice

## Core thesis: the chip layer is the most value-dense layer in the AI value chain

The second layer of the AI five-layer cake — the chip layer — is the most value-dense battlefield in the entire value chain. Not because it is the largest, but because it is the least substitutable. Power can be supplied via nuclear, natural gas, or solar in various combinations; data centers can be built by different contractors; models can be open-source, closed-source, or distilled. But the physical bottlenecks at the chip layer are true hard constraints: if you can't make HBM, you can't make an AI accelerator; if you don't have CoWoS packaging capacity, your chip is just a bare die; if you can't buy an EUV lithography machine, you don't even qualify to manufacture.

**How big is the market?** According to Bloomberg Intelligence, the AI accelerator market was approximately $116 billion in 2024, is expected to break $200 billion in 2026, and will exceed $600 billion by 2033 (source: Bloomberg Intelligence, January 2026). That's just the accelerators themselves. Add HBM memory (approximately $54.6 billion in 2026, source: Bank of America), advanced packaging, EDA tools, lithography equipment, and specialty materials, and the total addressable market of the chip layer already exceeds $300 billion in 2026.

**Nvidia's dominance — and its cracks.** Nvidia holds roughly 75-80% of AI accelerator market revenue (source: Silicon Analysts), with full fiscal-year 2026 revenue of $215.9 billion, of which data center revenue was $193.7 billion (source: Nvidia Q4 FY26 8-K). The CUDA ecosystem's moat runs extremely deep — over 5 million developers worldwide, hundreds of thousands of already-trained models, and deep integration across every mainstream AI framework — this is not something that can be replicated in a year or two. But cracks are appearing: in 2026, custom-ASIC growth (+45%) systematically overtook general-purpose GPU growth (+16%) for the first time. Google's TPU is already on its seventh generation, and Meta's MTIA and OpenAI's Broadcom-partnered custom chip are both entering mass production in the second half of 2026. Nvidia's share has fallen back from a 2024 peak of 87% to 75% — a structural trend, not a cyclical fluctuation.

**HBM: the AI chip's "ammunition."** The HBM capacity required per AI accelerator is growing exponentially: Nvidia's H100 uses 80GB of HBM3, the B200 uses 192GB of HBM3E, and Rubin uses 288GB+ of HBM4. Bank of America projects the HBM market will reach $54.6 billion in 2026, up 58% year over year (source: Bank of America, December 2025). SK Hynix holds a 62% share, with Micron at 21%, overtaking Samsung (17%). The key fact: HBM yields are extremely low (yields on 12-layer stacked HBM3E run about 60-70%), and capacity-expansion cycles are long (a new production line takes 18-24 months). The supply-demand gap is expected to persist through 2028. That means HBM suppliers will hold very strong pricing power over the next two years.

**Advanced packaging: the real bottleneck everyone overlooks.** TSMC's CoWoS (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate) packaging is the only mature, mass-production-ready method for integrating a GPU die with HBM. Monthly capacity was around 35,000 wafers at the end of 2024 and is planned to expand to 130,000 wafers by the end of 2026 — nearly a 4x increase — and it's still oversubscribed (source: TrendForce, April 2026). The average selling price of a single CoWoS wafer has already approached 7nm-process levels (source: TrendForce, April 28, 2026), meaning packaging is turning from a "back-end process" into a genuine profit center. And the ABF packaging substrates that CoWoS needs are supplied globally mainly by a handful of companies — Ibiden, Unimicron, Shinko Electric — creating yet another nested bottleneck.

**Specialty gases: the invisible chokepoint.** NF3 (nitrogen trifluoride) is used for semiconductor chamber cleaning, and WF6 (tungsten hexafluoride) is used in tungsten-deposition processes — both are indispensable specialty gases for advanced-node manufacturing. In August 2025, a fire at Japan's Kanto Denka Kogyo NF3 plant sent shockwaves through the global chip supply chain (source: TrendForce, August 14, 2025). After Mitsui Chemicals exited NF3 production in March 2026, Kanto Denka became Japan's sole major NF3 supplier. WF6 is even tighter — in 2026, Japanese suppliers raised prices to Korean customers by 70-90%, and existing inventory will only last through May-June 2026 (source: LDeepAI analysis). This is a company with a total market cap of just $1.4 billion holding a chokehold on TSMC, Samsung, and Rapidus.

**This report's framework.** We start from 10 companies, covering the entire chip-layer chain from design tools (Synopsys) → lithography equipment (ASML) → specialty gases (Kanto Denka) → manufacturing (TSMC) → packaging substrates (Ibiden) → memory (SK Hynix) → optical interconnect (Coherent) → accelerator design (Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom). For each company, we answer one core question: **What is this bottleneck actually worth, and is the market pricing it correctly?**

## 1. SK Hynix (000660.KS) — Buying AI's "ammunition depot" at 7x P/E

### Company overview and bottleneck position

SK Hynix is the world's second-largest memory chipmaker, but in the HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) track — the most critical one in the AI era — it is the undisputed number one. HBM is the AI accelerator's "ammunition": without HBM, no matter how powerful the GPU, it just spins its wheels. SK Hynix holds a 62% shipment share and roughly 57% revenue share of the HBM market (source: Astute Group, 2026), far ahead of Micron (21%) and Samsung (17%).

SK Hynix's lead is no accident. It has been co-developing HBM with Nvidia since 2013, beat Samsung to HBM3E mass production by two years, and is the preferred supplier for every generation of Nvidia GPU. HBM4 has completed development, claiming a 40% improvement in power efficiency and a 10Gbps data rate, and will be integrated into Nvidia's Rubin platform (source: SK Hynix's official site and multiple media reports).

### Irreplaceability analysis

**Extremely strong.** HBM is not ordinary DRAM — it requires stacking 8-16 layers of DRAM dies vertically using TSV (through-silicon via) and micro-bump bonding, with yield as the core barrier. SK Hynix's 12-layer HBM3E yield leads Samsung's by roughly 6-12 months. More critically, each HBM generation requires jointly designing custom interfaces and signaling protocols with the GPU maker; SK Hynix's collaboration with Nvidia now spans more than 10 years — a depth of integration that cannot be caught up to simply by spending money.

Substitution risk: Micron has demonstrated an HBM4 sample at 11Gbps (slightly higher than SK Hynix's 10Gbps), narrowing the technology gap. Samsung has also claimed to be the first to meet Nvidia's HBM4 pin-speed requirement. Competition in the HBM4 generation will be fiercer than in HBM3E.

### Valuation judgment

**This is the biggest mispricing in this report.** A P/E of 5.8-7x for a company with a 72% operating margin, in a state of undersupply, with capacity sold out through 2028, is an absurd undervaluation. The market assigns a low P/E because the memory industry has historically been highly cyclical (booming and busting on a 3-4 year cadence), and investors worry HBM demand is transient. But this time is different — AI's demand for HBM is not a one-time equipment-purchase cycle, but a continuously expanding infrastructure need. Even assuming margins fall from 72% to 50%, the current valuation still implies an expectation of "profits cut in half."

### Risk factors

1. **Cyclicality risk**: The memory industry has never had a permanent upcycle. If AI investment slows in 2027-2028, HBM prices could come under pressure
2. **Technology-transition risk**: In the HBM4 generation, Samsung and Micron could narrow the gap, and share could retreat from 62%
3. **Korean governance discount**: Korea's chaebol structure and weaker tradition of shareholder returns could limit the room for valuation re-rating
4. **Geopolitics**: US-China tech decoupling could affect supply-chain positioning

### One-line takeaway

**Buying one of the world's most profitable chip companies at 7x P/E — even at half that valuation, it would still be cheap. This is the clearest mispricing in the chip layer.**

## 2. Kanto Denka Kogyo (4047.T) — A $1.4 billion invisible chokepoint

### Company overview and bottleneck position

Kanto Denka Kogyo is one of Japan's largest specialty-gas manufacturers, with core products NF3 (nitrogen trifluoride) and WF6 (tungsten hexafluoride). NF3 is used for chamber cleaning in semiconductor manufacturing equipment — required at every process step; WF6 is used for tungsten deposition in advanced processes, a key material for FinFET and GAA transistor structures.

After Mitsui Chemicals exited NF3 production in March 2026, Kanto Denka became Japan's sole large-scale NF3 supplier, holding roughly 90% of Japan's NF3 capacity (source: Sourceability, 2025 reporting). Its customers include TSMC, Samsung, Micron, Kioxia, and Japan's newly-established Rapidus. The August 2025 NF3 plant fire briefly triggered panic across the global chip supply chain, underscoring this small company's pivotal position in the value chain.

### Irreplaceability analysis

**Close to the sole supplier in the Japanese market.** Producing NF3 and WF6 requires precision fluorochemistry and rigorous safety controls (fluorine gas is highly toxic), and there are only a handful of qualified global suppliers. China has some capacity, but purity does not meet semiconductor-grade standards; Korea's SK Materials has NF3 capacity but mainly serves internal demand. The WF6 situation is even tighter — in 2026 Japanese suppliers raised prices to Korean customers by 70-90%, with inventory only sufficient to last through mid-year (source: LDeepAI analysis).

Kanto Denka's moat is not patents, but process know-how and safety certification. Getting an NF3 plant from groundbreaking to customer certification takes 2-3 years — that is the substitution window. After the fire, Kanto Denka accelerated capacity expansion, and the FY2027 guidance of 45% revenue growth reflects exactly that capacity coming online.

### Valuation judgment

**Complicated.** A P/E of 50x doesn't look cheap, but considering (1) FY2027 profit is guided to grow 51% (which would bring the P/E down to roughly 33x); (2) NF3/WF6 are in a structural shortage; and (3) the $1.4 billion market cap corresponds to an invisible bottleneck holding a trillion-dollar value chain hostage — this valuation isn't unreasonable. The real question is: how long can the specialty-gas price increases last? If 2027-2028 brings a wave of new capacity that flips supply and demand, the current valuation would look rich.

### Risk factors

1. **Safety-incident risk**: The 2025 fire proved fluorochemical production carries major safety hazards; another incident could be fatal
2. **Demand volatility**: Specialty-gas demand is highly correlated with fab utilization rates; a semiconductor downcycle would directly hit revenue
3. **Competitive entry**: Chinese firms are developing high-purity NF3 capacity that could, long term, break Japan's monopoly
4. **Liquidity risk**: A $1.4 billion market cap Japanese small-cap; foreign buying faces liquidity constraints

### One-line takeaway

**A $1.4 billion market cap holding a chokehold on a trillion-dollar value chain — WF6 price increases of 70-90% show extreme supply-demand tightness, but safety-incident risk and cyclicality need close monitoring.**

## 3. Ibiden (4062.T) — The "foundation" beneath the GPU

### Company overview and bottleneck position

Ibiden is a leading global manufacturer of ABF (Ajinomoto Build-up Film) packaging substrates. The ABF substrate is the "bridge" between a chip and a circuit board — every Nvidia GPU, AMD CPU, and Broadcom ASIC sits on one. Without a substrate, a chip is just a bare die that can't be soldered.

The ABF substrate market is highly concentrated: Unimicron (~22%), Ibiden, Shinko Electric, AT&S, and Nan Ya PCB together hold 74% of the market (source: Intel Market Research, 2026). Ibiden's differentiation lies in high-end AI/GPU substrates — Nvidia's H100 and B200-series GPU substrates are supplied mainly by Ibiden and Shinko. In February 2026, Ibiden announced a 500 billion yen capex plan for AI-substrate expansion, with customer prepayments already reaching 92.1 billion yen (source: Ibiden IR materials).

### Key financial data

| Metric | Data | Source |
|------|------|------|
| FY2025 revenue forecast | ~106.65 billion yen | Investing.com |
| Share price (6/2/2026) | 21,135 yen | Yahoo Finance |
| Market cap | ~4.3-5.9 trillion yen (~$29-40 billion) | Multiple sources (variance may reflect different dates) |
| P/E (TTM) | ~27x | CompaniesMarketCap |
| Customer prepayments | 92.1 billion yen | Ibiden IR |
| Capex plan | 500 billion yen | Ibiden announcement, 02/2026 |

### Irreplaceability analysis

**Moderate-to-strong.** While ABF substrates have high technical barriers (multilayer precision routing, thermal-expansion-coefficient control), the competitive landscape is not as concentrated as HBM's. Unimicron has a larger market share, and Shinko Electric (now acquired by Resonac) is also a strong competitor. Ibiden's advantages are: (1) a deep tie-up with Nvidia (GPU substrate supplier relationship); (2) customer prepayments backing the 500 billion yen expansion, indicating customers are willing to pay to lock in capacity.

Substitution risk: the ABF substrate expansion cycle is about 12-18 months — shorter than HBM's but still offering a protective window. Glass substrates are the long-term alternative technology direction; Intel is already researching them, but mass production is at least 2028 or later.

### Valuation judgment

**Reasonable, on the pricier side.** A 27x P/E isn't cheap for a Japanese manufacturer, but the 500 billion yen expansion plan means revenue and profit will grow substantially over the next 2-3 years. If AI-substrate revenue mix rises from roughly 30% currently to 50%+, there's room for further valuation re-rating. But note: large-scale capex also means near-term free cash flow will be under pressure.

### Risk factors

1. **Capex risk**: 500 billion yen is a massive commitment; if AI demand falls short, it could lead to overcapacity
2. **Rising competition**: Unimicron and AT&S are both expanding AI-substrate capacity; price-war risk exists
3. **Technology substitution**: Glass substrates could disrupt ABF substrates long term, but pose no material threat in the near term (within 3 years)
4. **Customer-concentration risk**: Heavily dependent on a handful of large customers such as Nvidia

### One-line takeaway

**The GPU's "foundation" supplier — 92.1 billion yen in customer prepayments shows real demand, and 27x P/E is reasonable for a company mid-way through a major expansion phase, but it's not the cheapest option.**

## 4. Nvidia (NVDA) — A $5 trillion monopolist, and 26x P/E isn't expensive

### Company overview and bottleneck position

Nvidia needs no introduction. It defines the AI accelerator market, holding 75-80% of revenue share, with the CUDA ecosystem serving as the "operating system" of AI computing. Every company training a large model worldwide uses Nvidia GPUs — not because there's no alternative, but because switching costs are too high.

Nvidia's bottleneck position is not the hardware itself (both AMD and ASICs are catching up), but its **software ecosystem**. CUDA has more than 5 million developers, 300+ acceleration libraries, and native support across every mainstream AI framework. This is a classic network effect: more users → richer libraries → new users increasingly prefer CUDA → even more users. Breaking this cycle would take 5-10 years.

| Data center revenue | $193.7 billion (+68%) | Nvidia Q4 FY26 8-K |

| Forward P/E | ~21.6-25x | GuruFocus / StockAnalysis |

### Irreplaceability analysis

**Extremely strong, but slowly diluting.** The CUDA ecosystem moat is real, but the rise of custom ASICs means the five or six largest customers (Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, OpenAI) are developing their own chips. Broadcom has confirmed six large XPU customers. This won't destroy Nvidia (general-purpose GPUs remain the training standard), but it will erode its monopoly in the inference market.

Nvidia's response strategy is to go "full-stack" — from chips to systems (DGX SuperPOD) to software (NIM, CUDA-X) to networking (Spectrum-X) — locking customers into the entire ecosystem. That's a smart strategy, but it also means Nvidia is transitioning from a high-margin chip company into a systems integrator, which could put pressure on long-term gross margins.

### Valuation judgment

**A forward P/E of 22-25x is genuinely not expensive for Nvidia.** FY2026 revenue of $215.9 billion, assuming FY2027 growth of 40-50% (Wall Street consensus around 45%), would put revenue at $300 billion+. At a 30% net margin, net profit would be about $90 billion, and a $5.28 trillion market cap corresponds to roughly 22x forward P/E. For a company growing 40%+ with gross margins above 73%, this valuation sits in a reasonable-to-cheap range.

On the other hand, the $5.28 trillion market cap means the market has already priced in an expectation that Nvidia will sustain high growth for the next five years. If AI investment goes through a 2018-crypto-style adjustment (Nvidia's FY2019 revenue fell 7%), this valuation might not hold up.

### Risk factors

1. **Custom-ASIC erosion**: The drop from 87% to 75% share has already happened, and could continue falling to 60-65%
2. **Export controls**: US restrictions on chip exports to China have already impacted roughly 10-15% of Nvidia's potential revenue
3. **Valuation sensitivity**: At a $5 trillion market cap, valuation is extremely sensitive to growth expectations — every 10-percentage-point drop in growth could trigger a 15-20% share-price adjustment
4. **Capex cycle**: Hyperscaler capex can't grow 50%+ forever; when the inflection point arrives is the central question

### One-line takeaway

**The best AI company in the world; a forward P/E of 22-25x for 40%+ growth is reasonable. The risk isn't the valuation — it's the timing of the growth inflection.**

## 5. TSMC (TSM) — A dual monopoly in advanced process and CoWoS

### Company overview and bottleneck position

TSMC is the only foundry in the world that can mass-produce 3nm/2nm advanced processes (Samsung has production lines but with a significant yield gap), while also monopolizing CoWoS advanced packaging — the key step of integrating a GPU die with HBM. In other words, **whether you use an Nvidia GPU, a Broadcom ASIC, or a Google TPU, it ultimately passes through a TSMC fab.**

In Q1 2026, advanced processes (7nm and below) accounted for 74% of TSMC's total revenue, with 3nm at 25% and 5nm at 36% (source: TSMC Q1 2026 6-K). CoWoS monthly capacity expanded from roughly 35,000 wafers at the end of 2024 to a planned 130,000 wafers by the end of 2026, yet remains oversubscribed, with Nvidia reserving most of the capacity (source: multiple TrendForce reports, 2026).

| P/E (TTM) | ~34.8x | GuruFocus |

### Irreplaceability analysis

**The strongest — nearly impossible to replace.** Advanced-process foundry work is the manufacturing industry's highest technical barrier — a single 3nm fab costs over $20 billion to build, takes 3-4 years to construct, and requires another 1-2 years for yield ramp. Samsung trails TSMC by 1-2 process generations, and Intel Foundry is still in restructuring. In CoWoS packaging, TSMC is likewise the de facto standard.

The only real "substitution" would come from the demand side: if chip-design companies shift to chiplet architectures to reduce reliance on a single large die, or if the UCIe standard matures enough for multiple packaging houses to do heterogeneous integration, TSMC's monopoly could loosen somewhat. But that's a five-plus-year story.

### Valuation judgment

**A trailing P/E of 35x and forward P/E of 22x are reasonable for the world's least substitutable manufacturer.** Annual capex of $52.0-56.0 billion means TSMC is paving the way for growth over the next 3-5 years. If CoWoS packaging truly becomes a profit center (with ASP approaching 7nm levels), TSMC's profit structure will improve further.

On the other hand, TSMC faces geopolitical risk — the situation in the Taiwan Strait is a Sword of Damocles hanging over every TSMC investor. The build-out of US fabs (Arizona) can partially mitigate this risk, but at a 30-50% higher cost.

### Risk factors

1. **Geopolitical risk**: The Taiwan Strait situation is a tail risk that cannot be ignored
2. **Rising capital intensity**: Investment doubles with each process generation, which could pressure returns on capital
3. **Customer concentration**: Apple + Nvidia + AMD + Qualcomm account for 50%+ of revenue, giving large customers strong bargaining power
4. **US fab costs**: The Arizona fab costs 30-50% more than facilities in Taiwan, which could drag on margins

### One-line takeaway

**The single most irreplaceable manufacturer on Earth — a 22x forward P/E is a reasonable price. The only real risk is geopolitics.**

## 6. Broadcom (AVGO) — King of custom ASICs

### Company overview and bottleneck position

Broadcom is the undisputed leader in custom AI ASICs. It has designed seven generations of TPUs for Google, designed MTIA for Meta, signed a multi-year agreement with OpenAI to develop 10GW-class custom accelerators, and is reportedly designing chips for other hyperscale customers (including, reportedly, Anthropic). CEO Hock Tan said in early 2026 that he has "clear line of sight" to a 2027 target of over $100 billion in AI chip revenue, backed by a $73 billion AI order backlog (source: Broadcom earnings and multiple media reports).

Broadcom's positioning is that of a "custom chip design plus advanced-packaging IP arms dealer" — customers tell it what performance they need, and it designs the chip and coordinates manufacturing at TSMC. The elegance of this model: Broadcom doesn't bear the inventory and sales risk of the chip itself; it earns high-margin revenue from design services and IP licensing.

### Irreplaceability analysis

**Strong, but being diluted.** In custom ASICs, Broadcom's main competitor is Marvell Technology (which designs Trainium for Amazon). Google is also reportedly in talks with Marvell to open a second source for inference chips. But Broadcom's edge lies in scale and experience — the accumulated know-how from seven TPU generations, plus full-stack capability in SerDes, network IP, and advanced packaging, make it the first choice for most hyperscale customers.

Substitution risk: if hyperscale customers build out their own chip-design teams (Google already has a chip team numbering in the thousands), long-term reliance on Broadcom could diminish. But designing an advanced AI ASIC takes 2-3 years and billions of dollars of investment, so customers are unlikely to fully in-source in the near term.

### Valuation judgment

**A forward P/E of 31-37x isn't expensive for 134% AI-revenue growth, but watch for drag from non-AI businesses.** Broadcom isn't a pure-play AI company — it also has VMware (enterprise software), networking equipment, and storage, all growing far slower than AI. Looking at the AI business alone, the valuation is more attractive; but as a whole company, the complexity of the business mix must be considered.

### Risk factors

1. **Customer-concentration risk**: Google may account for 40%+ of AI revenue; if Google accelerates in-house chip development, the impact would be significant
2. **Marvell competition**: Direct competition forming around Amazon and possibly Google inference chips
3. **VMware integration risk**: The $69 billion VMware acquisition, completed in late 2023, is still being integrated and could distract management
4. **Sustainability of pricing power**: As customers' own chip-design capabilities grow, Broadcom's design-services premium could be compressed

### One-line takeaway

**The biggest winner in custom ASICs — a $73 billion order backlog provides more than three years of visibility, and 31-37x forward P/E is reasonable. But being a non-pure-AI company is a valuation discount factor.**

## 7. Coherent (COHR) — The age of photonics has arrived

### Company overview and bottleneck position

Coherent is a leading global manufacturer of optoelectronic devices, with core products being indium phosphide (InP)-based laser chips and optical transceiver modules used for high-speed optical interconnects in AI data centers. As AI clusters scale from a single rack to spanning across data centers, copper cabling hits its physical limits (severe signal attenuation beyond about 100 meters), leaving fiber optics as the only option. The core of an optical transceiver module is the InP laser chip — a compound semiconductor that cannot be made from silicon.

Coherent is transitioning from 4-inch to 6-inch InP wafers, operating 6-inch lines in Sherman, Texas and Jarfalla, Sweden (source: TradingKey analysis, 2026). Its data-center business has a book-to-bill ratio above 4x — meaning for every $1 earned, over $4 in new orders comes in. This is a classic signal of severe undersupply. Nvidia has signed a $2 billion strategic partnership with Coherent (source: LongYield analysis).

| Data-center revenue | $1.2 billion (+33.6%) | Futurum Group |

| FY2028E revenue forecast | $10.6 billion | Analyst forecasts |

### Irreplaceability analysis

**Strong, but the track is widening.** InP optical devices are currently in severe global shortage (roughly a 70% demand gap), and Coherent is one of the largest-capacity suppliers (the other is Lumentum). The barrier to mass-producing 6-inch InP wafers is high — material preparation, epitaxial growth, and device-processing yield control all require years of accumulated expertise.

Substitution risk: silicon photonics technology is maturing, with Intel, Cisco, and Broadcom all pushing it forward. Silicon photonics can be manufactured with conventional silicon processes, at lower cost and higher yield, but still lags InP in high-performance scenarios (800G/1.6T long-distance transmission). Long term, silicon photonics could displace InP in short-to-medium-distance scenarios, but InP's position in the high end should remain solid over the medium term (3-5 years).

### Valuation judgment

**A forward P/E of 44x for a company in an undersupplied market with book-to-bill above 4x already reflects most of the growth expectations.** Coherent's issue is that it's not a pure InP company — it also has industrial lasers and materials businesses with lower growth that drag on overall margins. If the data-center business could be valued standalone, it would be more attractive. The current price is more "the right direction, at a rich price."

### Risk factors

1. **Silicon-photonics substitution risk**: The biggest medium-to-long-term threat
2. **Capacity-ramp risk**: Unstable yields on 6-inch InP lines could affect delivery
3. **Overvaluation risk**: A 44x forward P/E already reflects an optimistic outlook; growth falling short would compress the valuation
4. **Drag from non-AI businesses**: Slower-growing segments like industrial lasers pull down overall margins

### One-line takeaway

**A core supplier of optical interconnects for AI data centers — the undersupply picture is real, but a 44x forward P/E shows the market already sees it. Not cheap, but the direction is right.**

## 8. ASML (ASML) — EUV lithography, no substitute

### Company overview and bottleneck position

ASML is the sole supplier of EUV (extreme ultraviolet) lithography machines. 100% of the world's EUV lithography machines come from ASML alone. Without EUV, there are no sub-7nm advanced chips — no AI GPUs, no HBM, no product of any advanced process node. It's not one monopolist among several — it is, literally, the only one.

A single EUV lithography machine (NXE series) sells for roughly $180-200 million; the latest High-NA EUV (EXE series) sells for $350-400 million. ASML's order backlog reached €38.8 billion at the end of 2025 (source: ASML Q4 2025 6-K), with a record €13.2 billion in orders in Q4 2025 alone. SK Hynix has committed to purchasing about 30 EUV units through December 2027, and Samsung plans to buy 20 units for its Pyeongtaek P5 fab (source: ASML statistics).

### Key financial data

| Metric | Data | Source |
|------|------|------|
| Full-year 2025 revenue | €32.7 billion | ASML 6-K |
| 2025 EUV revenue | €11.6 billion (+39%) | ASML 6-K |
| Q1 2026 revenue | €8.8 billion | ASML Q1 2026 |
| Full-year 2026 guidance | €36.0-40.0 billion | ASML guidance |
| Gross margin guidance | 51-53% | ASML guidance |
| Order backlog (end of 2025) | €38.8 billion | ASML 6-K |
| Market cap (6/2026) | ~$621.6 billion | Analyst data |
| P/E (TTM) | ~57-59x | Multiple sources |
| Forward P/E | ~40-46x | GuruFocus |

### Irreplaceability analysis

**No substitute exists, literally.** The EUV lithography supply chain is itself an unrepeatable miracle: Zeiss supplies the optical systems, Trumpf supplies the laser source, and Berliner Glas supplies mirrors — each link is a sole supplier. A complete EUV lithography machine has more than 100,000 parts and over 40 subsystems, and no second company can build one.

China has been completely cut off from EUV supply (US export controls), Japan's Nikon has no mass-production solution in EUV, and Canon is exploring nanoimprint lithography (NIL) but it remains far from mature. ASML's monopoly position will not be broken for the foreseeable future (10+ years).

### Valuation judgment

**A forward P/E of 40-46x is a monopoly premium, but it isn't cheap.** ASML's issue is not competition but growth rate — EUV lithography-machine capacity expansion is constrained by supply-chain bottlenecks (Zeiss's optical-system output capacity is limited), and annual unit-shipment growth may run only 15-20%. The €38.8 billion order backlog provides extremely high revenue visibility, but it also means near-term growth is capped by the capacity ceiling.

For investors seeking certainty, ASML is an excellent choice (monopoly + order backlog + irreplaceability). For investors seeking value, a 40-46x forward P/E needs 5-7 years of earnings growth to "digest."

### Risk factors

1. **Valuation risk**: A 40-46x forward P/E requires sustained high growth to be justified
2. **Geopolitics**: Expanded export controls could shrink the addressable market
3. **Lithography technology roadmap**: Alternative technologies like nanoimprint lithography (NIL) and directed self-assembly (DSA) remain far from mature, but cannot be entirely ruled out
4. **Cyclicality**: Semiconductor capex is cyclical; ASML orders could drop sharply in a downturn (as in 2023)

### One-line takeaway

**The single most irreplaceable equipment company on Earth, but a 40-46x forward P/E shows the market fully knows it. Whether paying a premium for certainty is worth it depends on your investment horizon.**

## 9. AMD (AMD) — The only general-purpose GPU challenger

### Company overview and bottleneck position

AMD is the only company that can challenge Nvidia in the general-purpose AI GPU market. Its MI300/MI350-series accelerators have already won large-scale customer endorsement: OpenAI chose AMD to build 6GW of compute (MI450 deployment starting in the second half of 2026), Meta has committed to a multi-year contract (MI450, valued at roughly $60 billion), and AMD's data-center revenue reached a record $16.6 billion in 2025 (source: AMD 8-K, January 2026).

AMD's value doesn't lie in "beating Nvidia" — unrealistic in the near-to-medium term — but in giving customers a "second choice." When Nvidia's GPUs are undersupplied and aggressively priced, AMD offers an alternative at 80-90% of the performance for a lower price. For inference workloads (which don't require top-tier training performance), AMD's value-for-money advantage is even more pronounced.

### Key financial data

| Metric | Data | Source |
|------|------|------|
| 2025 data-center revenue | $16.6 billion (+32%) | AMD 8-K |
| Q1 2026 data-center revenue | $5.8 billion (+57%) | AMD Q1 2026 8-K |
| Q1 2026 data-center share of total revenue | >50% | AMD earnings |
| 3-5 year data-center AI revenue CAGR target | 80%+ | AMD Analyst Day, 11/2025 |
| Market cap (6/2026) | ~$350-400 billion (estimate) | Multiple sources |
| Forward P/E | ~45-69x (wide variance across sources) | GuruFocus / multiple sources |

### Irreplaceability analysis

**Moderate.** AMD's GPUs are narrowing the hardware-performance gap with Nvidia (MI350 competes with the B200, MI450 with the next generation), but its software ecosystem (ROCm vs. CUDA) is the biggest shortfall. ROCm's compatibility and stability still lag CUDA, with frequent bug reports in large-scale model training. AMD is investing heavily in ROCm development, but catching up on the software ecosystem will take 3-5 years.

AMD's true value lies in the EPYC server CPU — it already holds over 30% share in the cloud-server market, and AI inference is increasingly running on CPUs. The CPU+GPU combination is what differentiates AMD from pure-play GPU suppliers.

### Valuation judgment

**Complicated.** Forward P/E ranges between 45-69x (the variance reflects differing earnings forecasts), corresponding to an 80%+ data-center AI revenue growth target. If AMD can deliver on this growth rate, the current valuation has support; if growth falls short (say, delayed delivery on OpenAI's and Meta's orders), the valuation would face significant compression.

Compared with Nvidia's 22-25x forward P/E, AMD's valuation is actually more expensive (a higher P/E for a weaker market position). This reflects the market pricing in a "challenger premium" for AMD — a bet on share-growth optionality rather than absolute earnings certainty.

### Risk factors

1. **Software-ecosystem gap**: The ROCm-vs-CUDA gap could prove harder to close than the hardware gap
2. **Order-fulfillment risk**: Whether OpenAI's and Meta's massive orders can be delivered on schedule is uncertain
3. **Rich valuation**: Relative to Nvidia, AMD carries a higher P/E despite a weaker market position
4. **Custom-ASIC erosion**: AMD faces not only Nvidia competition but also substitution from Broadcom/Marvell custom ASICs

### One-line takeaway

**The only general-purpose GPU challenger — endorsements from OpenAI and Meta carry real weight, but the valuation is rich relative to Nvidia's. This is a bet on share growth, not on monopoly earnings.**

## 10. Synopsys (SNPS) — Chip design's "water and electricity"

### Company overview and bottleneck position

Synopsys and Cadence together control roughly 85% of the global EDA (electronic design automation) market (source: HeyGoTrade analysis). EDA software is the foundational tool of chip design — from logic synthesis and place-and-route to timing analysis and physical verification, no chip can be designed without EDA software. Every Nvidia GPU, AMD CPU, Broadcom ASIC, and Apple chip in the world was designed using Synopsys or Cadence tools.

In July 2025, Synopsys completed its $35 billion acquisition of Ansys (source: multiple media reports), extending its business from EDA into simulation and analysis, forming a full-chain coverage of "design + verification + simulation." Post-merger, Synopsys holds about 46% of the EDA market.

### Key financial data

| Metric | Data | Source |
|------|------|------|
| Q2 FY2026 revenue | $2.276 billion (vs. $1.604 billion in Q2 FY2025) | Synopsys 8-K |
| Full-year FY2026 guidance | $9.56-9.66 billion | Company guidance |
| Order backlog | $11.4 billion | Analyst reports |
| Market cap (6/2026) | ~$97.3 billion | Yahoo Finance |
| P/E (TTM) | ~116x | MacroTrends |
| Forward P/E | ~35x | FullRatio |

### Irreplaceability analysis

**Extremely strong.** EDA is a classic duopoly market — Synopsys and Cadence have traded the lead back and forth for decades, but no third company has ever truly threatened them. The reason is simple: chip design flows depend heavily on the completeness and reliability of the tool chain, and switching EDA vendors means re-validating the entire design flow at extremely high cost. Synopsys's customer stickiness is reflected in its $11.4 billion order backlog and high renewal rates.

AI's impact on EDA is twofold: (1) the complexity of AI-chip design is surging (a single B200 has over 200 billion transistors), requiring more, and more expensive, EDA tools; and (2) AI itself is being used to accelerate chip design (Synopsys's AI-driven synthesis tools), which could increase tool pricing power.

### Valuation judgment

**A 35x forward P/E is within a reasonable range for a company with steady growth (15-20%) that is nearly irreplaceable.** But two caveats: (1) the 116x trailing P/E reflects one-time costs from the Ansys acquisition and is not a meaningful reference; (2) integration risk from the $35 billion Ansys acquisition remains — if synergies fall short, margins could come under pressure.

Compared with Cadence (also growing around 17%, in a similar valuation range), Synopsys adds the incremental Ansys simulation business, but also adds integration risk. Between the two, Synopsys carries somewhat better odds.

### Risk factors

1. **Ansys integration risk**: A $35 billion mega-acquisition; whether cultural integration and cross-selling meet expectations is uncertain
2. **Growth ceiling**: The total EDA market is roughly $15.0-18.0 billion; growth is constrained by the global number of new chip-design starts
3. **Open-source EDA threat**: Google-backed open-source EDA tools like OpenROAD have made some inroads at the low end, though they pose no near-term threat at the high end
4. **Not a cheap valuation**: A 35x forward P/E for 15-20% growth implies a PEG of roughly 1.8-2.3x — not undervalued

### One-line takeaway

**Chip design's "water and electricity" — the duopoly position is solid, and 35x forward P/E is reasonable but not cheap. Whether the Ansys integration succeeds is the key variable over the next two years.**

| 9 | Coherent | ~44x | Right direction, but valuation already reflects expectations | ★★ |
| 10 | AMD | 45-69x | Challenger premium — priced richer than Nvidia despite a much weaker position | ★★ |

### The three most worthwhile names for deeper research

**#1: SK Hynix.** Buying the 62%-share HBM leader at 7x P/E, with a 72% operating margin exceeding Nvidia's, and capacity sold out through 2028. The market is pricing it like a traditional cyclical memory stock, but AI's demand for HBM is structural, not cyclical. Even if margins fall from 72% to 50%, the current valuation still carries a significant margin of safety. This is the clearest mispricing anywhere in the chip layer.

**#2: Nvidia.** A $5 trillion market cap looks intimidating, but a 22-25x forward P/E for 40%+ growth implies a PEG of roughly 0.5-0.6x — cheaper than most tech stocks. The CUDA ecosystem's moat, the execution of the full-stack strategy, and $193.7 billion in data-center revenue make Nvidia the most certain beneficiary in the AI value chain. The risk lies in the timing of the growth inflection, not the valuation itself.

**#3: TSMC.** A dual monopoly in advanced process and CoWoS, a 22x forward P/E, and $52.0-56.0 billion in capex paving the way for future growth. Geopolitics is the only genuine risk — but it is also precisely why TSMC cannot be replaced: no other company on Earth can replicate TSMC's manufacturing capability within 2-3 years.

### The bottom line

**The investment logic of the chip layer is simple: find the bottlenecks in the physical world that cannot be quickly replaced by software, capital, or time, then check whether the market is correctly pricing that irreplaceability. SK Hynix at 7x P/E is a clear mispricing; Nvidia and TSMC at 22-25x P/E are reasonable good prices; the remaining seven either have valuations that already fully reflect expectations, or their irreplaceability isn't quite as strong.**
