# A single picture to understand the AI industry chain: the five-layer cake, and where the money flows

Earlier this year, at Davos, Jensen Huang sat down with BlackRock CEO Larry Fink and sketched out a five-layer cake.

He said AI isn't a product — it's an entire industrial system running from power plants all the way to phone screens. Every layer is its own business, and every layer has its own bottleneck.

I think the most valuable thing about this framework is that it answers a very practical question: in 2026, the four big cloud providers will spend over $630 billion on AI — where does all that money actually go?

Walking through the five layers one by one makes the flow of money very clear.

---

## What the five layers look like

```
Layer 5  Application layer   What you can see:    AI assistants, autonomous driving, robots
Layer 4  Model layer         What you can use:    Large models, multimodality
Layer 3  Infrastructure layer What you can't see: Data centers, networking, liquid cooling
Layer 2  Chip layer          What everyone's fighting over: GPUs, memory, packaging
Layer 1  Energy layer        What everyone forgets: Power, transformers, nuclear
```

There's a line from Jensen Huang that stuck with me: "In the future, every company will have two factories — one that makes products, and one that's an AI factory. The AI factory does just one thing every day: produce tokens."

The metric he uses to measure an AI factory's efficiency is: how many tokens produced per watt.

In other words, the AI business ultimately comes down to the same thing as manufacturing — how much intelligence you can squeeze out of each unit of electricity. So the bottom layer of the cake isn't chips, it's energy. Without electricity, a GPU is just a piece of metal generating heat.

---

## Layer 1: Energy — the least glamorous, but the most certain

According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), global data center electricity consumption was about 485 terawatt-hours in 2025, and is projected to roughly double to around 950 terawatt-hours by 2030. Gartner's estimate is somewhat lower (448 terawatt-hours), but the trend is consistent: a doubling within five years.

The problem is, there isn't enough electricity. Nearly half of the US data centers originally slated to come online in 2026 have been delayed or canceled. Not because of a chip shortage — because of a power and transformer shortage.

The average lead time for large power transformers now exceeds two and a half years, with some high-spec models pushed out to four years or more. That means a transformer ordered today won't arrive until 2029 at the earliest. This bottleneck has no chance of easing before 2028.

The investment logic for the energy layer is simple: no matter which AI model wins, data centers still need electricity. You don't need to bet on which model company wins — you just need to know that power infrastructure supply can't keep up with demand.

Metrics worth watching: changes in transformer lead times, data center power-capacity gaps, nuclear-project approval progress.

---

## Layer 2: Chips — the most value-concentrated layer

AI accelerator chips are the single most money-dense segment of the entire industry chain. Depending on the estimate used, this market is worth roughly $150 billion in 2026.

A few key facts about the landscape:

General-purpose GPUs still dominate — about 86% share of the data center AI chip market in 2025. But that number is trending down: custom chips (ASICs) are projected to grow 45% in shipments in 2026, for the first time overtaking general-purpose GPUs' 16% growth. Major customers are all looking for alternatives — this is a structural shift.

HBM (high-bandwidth memory) is an indispensable component of AI chips, with over 60% of global share concentrated in a single company, and the supply-demand shortage is expected to persist at least through 2028.

For the most cutting-edge logic chip processes (3nm and below), over 90% of manufacturing capacity is concentrated in a single company — TSMC. Advanced packaging capacity (such as CoWoS) is similarly tight and has become a new bottleneck.

The chip layer is characterized by extremely high value density but also the widest valuation divergence. Some leaders have $5 trillion market caps but trade at forward P/Es in only the mid-20s, while some companies with market caps in the tens of billions trade at over 100x. Within the same layer, pricing differences can be huge.

Metrics worth watching: the HBM supply-demand gap, advanced-packaging capacity utilization, the substitution ratio of custom chips for general-purpose GPUs.

## Layer 3: Infrastructure — where the real money goes

This layer is the most straightforward, because there's one hard number: the four big cloud providers' 2026 capex.

| Company | 2026 capex guidance | YoY growth |
|------|------------------|---------|
| Amazon | ~$200 billion | +52% |
| Microsoft | ~$190 billion | +61% |
| Google | $180-190 billion | +97% to +108% |
| Meta | $125-145 billion | +73% to +101% |
| **Total** | **~$630-725 billion** | **~+77%** |

Note: all figures in USD, YoY growth calculated against actual 2025 capex. Each company's guidance is a range; the table takes the midpoint or confirmed value.

Not a single one is cutting back. All are raising guidance.

Roughly where this money goes: about 60%-plus into short-lived assets like chips and servers (this money flows up to the layer above), and 30%-plus into long-lived assets like data center buildings, land, and networking equipment.

The investment approach for the infrastructure layer is straightforward: follow the capex. Six to seven hundred billion dollars is going to be spent — the only question is whose pocket it lands in. And there's an interesting phenomenon in this layer — two companies doing very similar business often trade at valuations that differ by a factor of two. This kind of within-industry pricing gap is relatively easy to spot as an opportunity.

Metrics worth watching: each hyperscaler's quarterly capex guidance (raised or cut?), order backlogs for optical modules and switches, liquid-cooling penetration rates.

## Layer 4: Models — fastest growth, and the most cash-burning

Something quite extreme is happening at this layer: one AI company's annualized recurring revenue (ARR) rose from $1 billion to $47 billion in about 17 months. To be clear, annualized revenue is a forward-looking metric that multiplies the current month's revenue by 12 — the actual cash collected is less than that — but the growth rate is still unprecedented.

The second half of 2026 is a big year for AI-company IPOs. Several top players are preparing to go public, which will be the first time these sky-high valuations get tested in the public markets.

The model layer has a few defining features: astonishing growth but even more astonishing valuations — revenue is doubling repeatedly, but valuations run 40-50x annualized revenue; the winner-take-all picture hasn't settled — the gap between the top three or four players is narrowing quickly; Chinese players are an important variable — their API pricing is only a fraction, sometimes tens of times cheaper than comparable Western products, and prices keep falling.

For investors, the hard part about the model layer is that model companies themselves are priced too richly. Two adjacent angles may be more worth watching: first, companies with unique proprietary training data (models need data, and good data is a non-renewable resource); second, enterprise service companies doing private deployment (not every enterprise is comfortable handing its data to a large-model vendor).

Metrics worth watching: ARR growth and retention rates, API pricing trends, post-IPO market performance.

---

## Layer 5: Applications — easiest to understand, hardest to monetize

Everything people can actually perceive as AI — assistants, customer service, autonomous driving, robots — sits at this layer.

But the reality is that for most listed companies wearing an "AI concept" label, AI's actual contribution to revenue remains low. The companies genuinely making big money from AI can still be counted on one hand.

Valuation divergence at this layer is the most extreme of all five. At one end are "pure AI companies" growing fast but trading at 100-200x; at the other end are traditional software giants undergoing AI transformation, whose new AI products might be growing 200-300%, but because total company revenue growth is only around 10%, the market is still pricing them as "old companies."

There's an interesting mismatch here: the market sees a 10% overall growth rate and misses the 2-3x growth happening in the AI sub-business. If AI revenue as a share of the total rises from 3% to 15%, overall growth would suddenly accelerate — and at that point the valuation logic would get rewritten. Of course, this still needs time to play out.

Metrics worth watching: AI features' actual contribution to revenue (not just ARR or user counts — look at real cash), actual customer usage rates, AI's impact on overall gross margin.

## Having gone through all five layers, there's a pattern

The lower the layer, the higher the certainty and, generally, the more reasonable the valuation. The higher the layer, the bigger the imagination, but also the greater the uncertainty.

| Layer | Certainty | Explosiveness | Valuation characteristics |
|------|--------|--------|---------|
| Energy | Extremely high | Low | Often undervalued |
| Chips | High | Medium-high | Leaders reasonable, smaller companies overlooked |
| Infrastructure | High | Medium | Wide valuation gaps between peers |
| Models | Medium | Extremely high | Generally expensive |
| Applications | Low | Extremely high | Severely divergent |

If you're chasing certainty, look from the bottom up. If you're chasing explosive upside, dig from the top down.

---

## A few investment common-sense notes

First, popular doesn't mean expensive, and unpopular doesn't mean cheap. Nvidia is the single most talked-about company in the whole space, yet its forward valuation is only in the mid-20s with growth over seventy percent — by that math, it's actually not expensive. The key question isn't whether a company is popular, it's the relationship between its growth rate and its valuation.

Second, within the same track, when two companies doing similar things trade at valuations differing by a factor of two, the market will eventually correct that gap.

Third, more useful than "who's the strongest" is the question "who's the bottleneck." The strongest company is usually the most expensive; the bottleneck company is often overlooked.

Fourth, AI is a decade-scale industrial transformation — there's no need to rush and go all-in at once. Good companies eventually have moments when they're not expensive.
