## I. Charles Zhang: an honest but mediocre CEO

### 1.1 Strategic track record

Charles Zhang (Zhang Chaoyang), born in 1964 (now 62), holds a bachelor's degree in physics from Tsinghua and a PhD in physics from MIT (1993). He founded Sohu's predecessor in 1996, and the company was formally named Sohu in 1998, listing on Nasdaq in 2000.

As a first-generation Chinese internet entrepreneur, Zhang's strategic track record reads as follows:

| Internet wave | Sohu's move | Outcome | Winner |
|-----------|-----------|------|------|
| E-commerce (2003-2010) | Completely absent | — | Alibaba |
| Search (2004-2013) | Built Sogou | Sold to Tencent in 2021 | Baidu |
| Mobile social (2011-2014) | Failed to incubate an IM product | — | Tencent (WeChat) |
| Long-form video (2014-2020) | Sohu Video, went from leader to laggard | Monthly active users fell to the bottom of the industry | iQiyi/Tencent Video |
| Short-form video (2016-2020) | Completely absent | — | ByteDance/Kuaishou |
| Livestream e-commerce (2019-2024) | Zhang livestreams personally but sells nothing | Deliberately abstained | Douyin/Kuaishou |
| Large AI models (2023-2026) | **Completely absent** | — | Baidu/Alibaba/ByteDance/Zhipu/DeepSeek |

Source: public reporting, corporate development histories of the companies listed

**Over the past 20 years, Sohu has either missed or lost on every major wave in Chinese internet.** Sogou did get built, but it was ultimately sold to Tencent. Sohu Video was once a leader, but it lost the copyright war against BAT's capital firepower.

**Counter-argument**: Jack Ma never built a successful social product either, and Pony Ma never built e-commerce (though he later got WeChat Pay and Video Accounts) — it's unreasonable to expect every CEO to catch every wave. Zhang at least built Sogou, and sold it at a reasonable time — that's not total failure.

**This counter-argument has merit, but there is a key distinction**: Jack Ma and Pony Ma each found their own "main battlefield" (e-commerce and social, respectively) and built an unassailable moat there. Zhang's problem isn't that he "missed every wave" — it's that **he never held a single main battlefield**: the portal was disrupted by algorithmic recommendation, video was crushed by giants, search was dominated by Baidu, and gaming survives on the fumes of a single 2007 IP.

### 1.2 Integrity assessment

On this dimension, Zhang's record is clean:

- No SEC investigation record
- No financial fraud scandal
- Minority shareholders received a 68% premium in the Changyou take-private
- The $150 million buyback in 2025 was genuinely completed
- A relatively low-profile personal life

**Integrity is Zhang's biggest strength.**

### 1.3 Capital allocation ability

This is one of the most critical dimensions for evaluating a CEO. Zhang's capital allocation record:

| Action | Assessment |
|------|------|
| Sogou sale (2021, $1.18 billion) | **Adequate** — timing was reasonable, the search business was already under siege |
| Changyou take-private (2020, $579 million) | **Excellent** — bought back the most profitable subsidiary at a fair premium |
| Share buybacks (2020-2026, roughly $150 million cumulative) | **Adequate** — has begun returning cash to shareholders |
| Use of the $1.2 billion cash on the balance sheet | **Failing** — mostly dormant, no meaningful reinvestment |
| Continued investment in media/video businesses | **Failing** — kept funding losses knowingly |
| AI / new business positioning | **Zero** — completely absent |

**Summary**: Zhang has performed adequately on "selling assets" and "buybacks," but failed badly at "using the money." $1.2 billion in cash has sat idle for years, with neither a large buyback/dividend program nor any meaningful new business investment.

**Counter-argument**: Conservative management isn't necessarily bad in an uncertain environment. Sohu hasn't gone the way of LeEco with reckless investment leading to bankruptcy — at least the cash is still there.

**This counter-argument holds, but only to a point**: not spending recklessly is indeed better than spending recklessly, but holding a large cash pile for years without returning it to shareholders or deploying it effectively is, at its core, a waste of shareholder capital. As Buffett has said: "If management can't deploy capital effectively, it should return the money to shareholders." Zhang did not do this before 2025.

## II. Take-private: the single most important catalyst

### 2.1 Financial feasibility of taking Sohu private

| Item | Amount |
|------|------|
| Current market cap | $360 million |
| Acquisition price at a 100% premium | $720 million |
| Zhang's stake, roughly 35.8% (via Photon Group) | No need to buy back his own shares |
| External shares that would need to be acquired (64.2%) | Roughly $460 million |
| Company's net cash on the balance sheet | $1.15 billion |
| **Cash remaining after taking the company private** | **Roughly $690 million** |

Source: shareholding data from Sohu's 2025 annual report, Yahoo Finance

**Conclusion: Zhang could take Sohu private at a 100% premium using Sohu's own cash, and still have $690 million left over afterward.** This is essentially a risk-free "negative-price acquisition."

### 2.2 Precedent

Zhang has already executed a nearly identical transaction once before:

| Dimension | Changyou take-private (2020) | Sohu today |
|------|-----------------|---------|
| Net cash / market cap before going private | Roughly 1.5x | **3.2x (more extreme)** |
| Controlling shareholder stake | Sohu held 70%+ | Zhang holds roughly 35.8% |
| Premium | **68%** | Undetermined |
| Outcome | Completed successfully | — |

Sina also completed a take-private delisting in 2020 (an MBO led by Charles Chao, at roughly an 18% premium). There is now a well-established playbook for taking Chinese portal companies private.

### 2.3 Will Zhang take the company private?

**Factors favoring take-private**:
- Market cap has long sat far below cash, so the listing has no further financing value
- Maintaining the listing costs $5-10 million a year (audit, compliance, investor relations)
- Zhang has already done this once successfully with Changyou
- Rising US-China uncertainty adds to the appeal of delisting
- After going private, there's no more quarterly-earnings pressure, letting him focus on the IP business

**Factors against take-private**:
- Zhang may **enjoy the social status that comes with being a public-company CEO**
- Part of the "physics-livestream celebrity CEO" brand depends on the "public-company founder" label
- Taking the company private means dealing with minority-shareholder lawsuits and regulatory approval
- Zhang may simply feel "no rush" — with cash in hand, slow buybacks are also an option

**Judgment**: The probability of a take-private is moderate-to-high, but the timing is uncertain. It could happen within 3-5 years, or it could drag on much longer.

**The biggest risk is not "no take-private" — it's "a take-private at a very low premium."** Referencing Sina's take-private (only an 18% premium), if Zhang were to take Sohu private at a 20-30% premium (implying $17-18/ADS), retail shareholders would book a profit but far below intrinsic value. The 68% premium in the Changyou deal is a good precedent, but there's no guarantee Sohu shareholders get the same treatment.

## III. Three core risks

### Risk 1: Value trap — the discount never closes

This is the biggest risk. Sohu could end up like many "net-cash discount stocks," where **market cap stays below cash indefinitely but the value is never released.**

| Comparable case | Net cash / market cap | Outcome |
|---------|-----------|------|
| Esprit Holdings (330.HK) | 1.5-2.0x | Long-term value trap, continuously burning cash |
| Japan's "zombie company" cohort | Generally >1.0x | Chronically undervalued; culture discourages buybacks/liquidation |
| Yahoo (final years) | >1.0x | Eventually acquired by Verizon |

**There is a non-trivial probability Sohu becomes a long-term "walking dead" stock.** If Zhang neither takes the company private, nor buys back/pays dividends at scale, and the business keeps losing money, investors can only watch the discount persist without getting any return.

**Mitigating factor**: The $150 million buyback in 2025 is a positive signal. But its durability still needs to be proven.

### Risk 2: TLBB IP aging — the revenue base could collapse

The Tian Long Ba Bu (TLBB) franchise contributes 60% of Sohu's total revenue. This 2007-vintage IP carries a clear lifespan risk:

- Mobile-game MAU fell **-27%** year over year in 2025 (source: 2025 annual report)
- Q1 2026 gaming revenue rose only +3% quarter over quarter, as the TLBB Return revival dividend fades
- Sohu has not disclosed any major new game pipeline
- Changyou's scale in the Chinese gaming market (roughly $500 million) is a rounding error next to Tencent's (roughly $27.4 billion)

**If TLBB revenue declines 50% over the next 3-5 years, Changyou's operating profit will shrink sharply, accelerating cash burn.** This is the primary path by which Sohu's "net-cash cushion" gets eroded.

**Counter-argument**: NetEase's Fantasy Westward Journey (launched in 2003) is still a cash-printing machine. Classic MMO IPs may have longer lifespans than expected.

**Counter to the counter**: Fantasy Westward Journey has the support of NetEase's entire gaming ecosystem behind it, while TLBB has only Changyou's single team. The resource gap determines the gap in how long each IP can be extended.

### Risk 3: Founder risk — 62 years old, no successor

Zhang is 62, wears the Chairman, CEO, and lead-livestreamer hats simultaneously, and Sohu's brand recognition depends almost entirely on him personally.

- Should Zhang exit due to health, retirement, or any other reason, Sohu's stock could fall another **30-50%**
- Sohu has no publicly disclosed succession plan
- The physics-livestream IP is Zhang's personal asset, not the company's

**This is a textbook case of "key man risk."**

## IV. Investment judgment: two entirely different frameworks

Judging Sohu depends on which investment framework you apply:

### Framework A: Good business + good company + good price

This is the framework of late-career Buffett, Duan Yongping, and Li Lu. By this standard:

| Dimension | Assessment | Verdict |
|------|------|------|
| Good business? | Portal/video are structurally dying; gaming rides a single aging IP | **No** |
| Good company? | No moat, no growth, no successor | **No** |
| Good price? | Extremely cheap | **Yes** |
| **Overall** | **1 of 3 criteria met** | **Don't buy** |

**Under this framework, Sohu is a textbook "value trap" — cheap but not good.**

### Framework B: Graham cigar-butt stock

This is the framework of Benjamin Graham and early-career Buffett (the 1950s). By this standard:

| Cigar-butt criterion | Sohu | Met? |
|-----------|------|-------|
| Market cap < net current assets | $360 million < $1.15 billion | **Emphatically met** |
| Cash > 50% of total assets | Roughly 70%+ | **Met** |
| Interest-bearing debt very low | $36 million | **Met** |
| Core business cash flow positive or manageable | Gaming is profitable; overall a slight loss | **Met** |
| Management is buying back stock | $150 million in 2025 | **Met** |
| A catalyst exists | Buybacks + take-private optionality | **Met** |

**Under this framework, Sohu is an A-grade cigar-butt stock.**

### Where the two frameworks diverge

**This is what makes Sohu so interesting: the exact same set of facts produces opposite conclusions depending on which investment framework you apply.**

- If you believe in "good business + good company + good price" — Sohu is a value trap; avoid it
- If you believe in "buying a dollar of assets for 40 cents" — Sohu is a textbook opportunity

**Which framework you believe determines your judgment. This series does not choose for you.**

## VI. Who should pay attention, and who should avoid it?

| Investor type | Recommendation | Reason |
|-----------|------|------|
| **Experienced deep-value / event-driven investors** | May take a small position | Understands cigar-butt logic and can tolerate the psychological pressure of a long hold |

**If you do decide to pay attention, a few rules**:
- Keep the position strictly under **3-5%**
- Set a holding period of **18-36 months**
- Watch the core catalysts: new buyback announcements, take-private announcements, TLBB revenue trends
- If net cash is being consumed quickly (more than $100 million a year), reassess

## VII. Sohu's ultimate question

The four parts of this series essentially all answer the same question:

> **Is Sohu "a liquidation asset sitting on a pile of gold," or "something that looks cheap but where the value can never actually be captured — a value trap"?**

The honest answer is: **either is possible, and it depends on what Zhang Chaoyang does next.**

Before 2025, the answer leaned toward "value trap" — management wasn't buying back stock, wasn't paying dividends, wasn't taking the company private, and the discount just sat there indefinitely.

After 2025, the $150 million buyback changed the narrative — management has started returning cash, though the scale and durability of that both still need to be proven.

**In the end, Sohu's investment value doesn't depend on its business (the business has no future) — it depends on one person's decision: what Zhang Chaoyang chooses to do with $1.2 billion in cash.**

- If he keeps buying back stock and paying dividends, the discount gradually narrows and investors get a reasonable return
- If he initiates a take-private, investors get a one-time cash-out
- If he does nothing, the discount persists forever and investors are left simply waiting

This is not a story about a "good business." It's a story about "how an honest but mediocre founder chooses to handle $1.2 billion in cash."
