## First, a fundamental question: is AGIBOT a good business today?

10,000 units in three years, the world's top shipment volume, all-in bets from Tencent, Hillhouse, and Sequoia — the first three articles laid these facts out clearly. Now comes the hardest, most important question in this series: **is this business worth putting money into?**

Don't rush to say "of course it is" — because net profit hasn't been disclosed, gross margin hasn't been disclosed, and cash flow hasn't been disclosed. Judging whether a business is good while missing all three of those is like groping an elephant in a dark room.

Measured against value-investing standards, a "good business" typically needs to satisfy several conditions:

| Standard | AGIBOT's current state | Assessment |
|------|-----------|------|
| Sustained profitability | **Unknown — net profit has never been disclosed, and losses are ongoing** | Not met |
| Predictable free cash flow | **Unknown — cash flow has never been disclosed** | Not met |
| A moat that's hard for competitors to replicate | Has first-mover and scale advantages, but switching costs are low and the technical barrier is narrowing | Partially met |
| Customers pay for value, not for discounts | **Insufficient evidence — many customers appear to be buying for "strategic positioning" rather than a clear return on investment** | Not met |
| Trustworthy management | Star-studded team, strong execution, but capital operations are overly aggressive | Partially met |

**Honest answer: by traditional "good business" standards, AGIBOT currently is not one.**

It is a company at a very early stage, burning cash to expand, that has yet to prove its business model works. That's not a slight — Amazon wasn't a "good business" in 2001 either, and neither was Tesla in 2018. The question is: how confident are you that AGIBOT will become the company that eventually "proves itself"?

## Six major risks

### Risk 1: Volume without profit — leading in shipments but possibly loss-making growth

This is the bears' central argument.

Unitree earned **600 million yuan** in net profit on **1.7 billion yuan** of 2025 revenue — a **35%** net margin; AGIBOT's profit picture on 1.05 billion yuan of revenue is unknown but very likely a loss.

**Why does one company make money in the same race while the other doesn't?** The most likely reasons:
1. Unitree has a high-margin quadruped robot business to "feed" itself; AGIBOT doesn't
2. Unitree was founded earlier (2016 vs. 2023), so its R&D spending has already passed through the steepest part of the ramp
3. AGIBOT's product lineup is too scattered (Yuanzheng + Lingxi + Jingling + Qiyuan) — every line needs investment, but none has broken out

**Counterargument**: loss-making growth is normal for early-stage tech companies. JD.com lost money for 12 years before turning a profit; Tesla lost money for 17 years. What matters is whether the losses are buying a valuable market position and technology accumulation.

**Counter-counterargument**: while JD.com was losing money, its GMV and user count were growing exponentially, and its moat was deepening. AGIBOT's moat today is shallow — switching costs are low, technical barriers are being closed by rivals, and the brand is tied to one individual. "Also losing money" doesn't mean "also building a moat."

### Risk 2: Suspicious related-party orders

Several of AGIBOT's major customers have equity ties to the company:

| Customer | Relationship | Order size |
|------|------|---------|
| Longcheer Technology | Invested in AGIBOT first, then placed orders | Reportedly "several hundred million yuan" |
| BYD | Both a shareholder and a customer | Amount undisclosed |
| Joyson Electronics | Supply-chain partner | Undisclosed |

**The question**: when a customer is also a shareholder, is the order really "driven by genuine demand" or "driven by the investment relationship"?

Longcheer Technology's case is especially worth watching — it invested first, then ordered nearly a thousand units. If the real driver behind these orders is "maintaining the investment relationship" rather than "the robots genuinely work well," the orders could vanish once the investment is exited.

**Counterargument**: pairing industrial-capital investment with ordering is a common playbook in China's hard-tech sector, and doesn't necessarily mean anything is wrong with the orders. BYD investing in AGIBOT while also deploying AGIBOT robots on its own factory floors is itself a way of "validating the product with real action."

**The truth can't be determined right now.** It requires the IPO prospectus to disclose related-party transaction details and customer repeat-purchase data.

### Risk 3: Valuation bubble

| Valuation basis | Amount | P/S on 2025 revenue |
|---------|------|---------------------|
| Series B (private market) | 15 billion yuan | **14.3x** |
| IPO target valuation (midpoint) | ~40.9 billion yuan (HK$45 billion) | **39x** |
| Unitree's IPO valuation | 42 billion yuan | **24.6x** |
| UBTECH's market cap | ~HK$51.4 billion | **~23x** |

**14x P/S (Series B valuation)** is acceptable for a high-growth sector. But **39x P/S (IPO target valuation)** requires the market to believe:

- 2026 revenue reaches at least **2.5-3 billion yuan** (growth of 140-186%), enough to bring the P/S back down to comparable-company levels
- Or the company turns profitable by 2028, with net profit reaching **800 million yuan+** (at a 50x P/E)

These assumptions aren't impossible, but for a company only 3 years old that has never disclosed its profitability, **the level of confidence is extremely low**.

**Counterargument**: Figure AI is valued at $39 billion (about 280 billion yuan), with shipment volume and revenue near zero. By comparison, AGIBOT's HK$45 billion valuation is at least backed by 1.05 billion yuan of revenue and shipments in the thousands of units — which looks far more "grounded" than Figure AI's.

**But "cheaper than Figure AI" doesn't mean "cheap."** That logic is like saying "a 500-yuan cup of coffee is cheaper than a 5,000-yuan cup" — both are expensive.

### Risk 4: Overly aggressive capital operations

Between 2024 and 2025, beyond products and fundraising, AGIBOT carried out a striking number of capital-market moves:

- Invested in **15** startups
- Formed **17** joint ventures
- Spent **2.1 billion yuan** acquiring Shangwei New Materials (688585.SH), gaining an A-share shell

**The skeptics' concern**: for a company with 1.05 billion yuan in annual revenue that is still losing money, is it wise to run capital operations at this scale within a single year — shouldn't the energy and funding be more focused on products and customers?

The 2.1-billion-yuan acquisition is especially controversial. Shangwei New Materials is a new-materials company with limited synergy with the humanoid robotics business. This looks more like a "reverse-merger" maneuver — securing an A-share capital-markets platform — than industrial integration.

**Counterargument**: Deng Taihua is a 27-year Huawei veteran, and Huawei's playbook has always been to "build broad alliances, build an ecosystem." A supply-chain alliance formed with 40+ listed-company partners is one of AGIBOT's differentiating advantages. Acquiring Shangwei New Materials for an A-share shell keeps future capital-market options open.

**Both readings have merit — the outcome is the real test.** If 2026 revenue really delivers "multiple-fold growth" and the supply-chain alliance pays off, these moves will look like "planning ahead"; if growth falls short, they'll look like "overextension."

### Risk 5: Key-person risk

Zhihui Jun is AGIBOT's single biggest IP asset. If he were to leave, the company would simultaneously lose:
1. **Brand influence** — the free traffic and public attention driven by his 2.84 million Bilibili followers
2. **Technical leadership** — as CTO and President, he directly oversees the X-Lab advanced-research unit
3. **Fundraising appeal** — a good share of investors put money in specifically because of Zhihui Jun personally

There's currently no clear sign of any "de-Zhihui-Jun-ification" effort underway. The addition of Chief Scientist Luo Jianlan (with a Google DeepMind background) has diversified the technical dependency to some degree, but the dependency on brand and fundraising can't be resolved in the short term.

### Risk 6: The sector's timeline could run far longer than expected

| Technology revolution | Tech demo → mass commercialization | Years waited |
|---------|---------------------|---------|
| Internet | 1969 (ARPANET) → 1995 | 26 years |
| Autonomous driving | 2004 (DARPA Challenge) → **still not achieved** | 20+ years, and counting |
| Humanoid robots | 2023 (year of mass production) → ? | **To be determined** |

Humanoid robots today most closely resemble **autonomous driving circa 2016** — exciting tech demos, capital pouring in feverishly, top companies' valuations soaring. But autonomous driving has had another 10 years since 2016, and large-scale commercial deployment at L4 level still hasn't arrived.

**If humanoid robotics' commercialization timeline also needs 10-20 years, then every company's current valuation in this sector is seriously overpriced.**

## Historical analogy: DJI or Royole?

What will AGIBOT ultimately become? Consider two extreme cases side by side:

| Analogy | Similarities | Outcome |
|------|--------|------|
| **DJI (best-case outcome)** | Chinese hard tech, technology leadership, a founder with strong personal appeal, rapid mass production | Global drone hegemon, 80%+ market share |
| **Royole (worst-case outcome)** | Flashy tech concept, feverish capital chase, soaring valuation | Commercialization failure, bankruptcy liquidation in 2023 |
| **NIO (middle-ground scenario)** | First mover in a new sector, premium positioning, persistent losses | Survived a near-death experience, but still unprofitable |

**Will AGIBOT become DJI?** Possible, but not highly likely — DJI won by achieving a brand monopoly where "drone = DJI"; the humanoid robotics race is far more crowded (Tesla + Unitree + 140 Chinese companies).

**Will AGIBOT become Royole?** Also not very likely — AGIBOT has real shipments in the thousands and 1.05 billion yuan of revenue, far more substantial than Royole's "slide deck plus concept." Its team's execution is also clearly stronger.

**Most likely scenario**: AGIBOT follows NIO's path — a stretch of rapid growth, persistent losses, round after round of fundraising to stay alive, and eventually becoming one of the surviving top players once the sector matures. But that road will be very painful along the way, with possible "near-death moments" in between.

> AGIBOT is a company "worth watching closely but not worth betting on right now" — not because it's bad, but because **there isn't enough information**. Until the IPO prospectus discloses audited financial data, what you'd be buying isn't a company — it's an information black box.

## A checklist of questions that need first-hand verification

If you're seriously considering an investment in AGIBOT (or the humanoid robotics sector), the following questions are worth answering through on-the-ground due diligence:

1. **Visit BYD's and Longcheer Technology's factories** — are AGIBOT robots really working on the production line, or only "on display" in a corner?
2. **Talk to customers who've already bought in** — will they buy again? Why? How much does one robot save them per year?
3. **Understand product reliability** — what's the mean time between failures (MTBF)? What do repairs cost?
4. **Visit the contract manufacturers** — what are the yield rates at Junpu Intelligent and Lens Technology like? Can quality be assured at mass-production scale?
5. **Understand the real scale of losses** — through private-market investors, find out the non-public data: how much cash is burned each year? How long will the cash last?
6. **Track Tesla Optimus's real progress** — how are trial runs going at the Fremont plant? Is 500,000 units of annual capacity by 2027 realistic?
