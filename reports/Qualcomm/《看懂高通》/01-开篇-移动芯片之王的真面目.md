## A set of numbers that will make you rethink Qualcomm

The world sells roughly 1.2 billion smartphones a year. Inside the vast majority of flagship Android phones sits a Snapdragon processor; and regardless of whether that particular processor is made by Qualcomm, phone makers almost always have to pay Qualcomm an additional fee — a patent licensing fee. That's Qualcomm's business: it sells chips, and it collects a "toll."

That understanding isn't wrong, but it's far from complete. Start with a set of numbers:

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| Current share price (May 2026) | **~$209** |
| Market cap | **~$217 billion** |
| FY2025 total revenue | **$44.3 billion** (+13.7%) |
| FY2026 first-half revenue | **$22.8 billion** |
| QTL patent-licensing pre-tax margin | **over 63%** |
| FY2025 free cash flow | **$12.8 billion** |
| 5-year average ROIC | **29.0%** |
| Annualized automotive chip revenue | **over $5 billion** (already surpassed as of FY2026 Q2) |
| Automotive design-win pipeline | **$45 billion** |
| PE (TTM) | **~21x** |
| Dividend yield | **~1.8%** |
| Annual buybacks + dividends | **aggressive shareholder returns** ($20 billion new buyback program + quarterly dividend of $0.92) |

(Data sources: Qualcomm FY2025 10-K, Q1/Q2 FY2026 earnings reports, public market data)

Put these numbers together and what you see isn't a "phone chip company" — it's a tech giant **running two businesses at once, "chip design" and a "patent tollbooth,"** while extending its reach into automotive, PCs, IoT, and on-device AI.

## What exactly is Qualcomm?

The most common misreading is to equate Qualcomm with "the Snapdragon processor." Snapdragon is indeed Qualcomm's best-known product, but Qualcomm's business model is far more complex than "selling chips."

Qualcomm runs two starkly different businesses simultaneously:

### QCT: Chip technology (roughly 85% of revenue)

Designs and sells phone processors, baseband chips, RF front-ends, automotive chips, PC processors, and IoT chips. FY2025 revenue was about **$37.7 billion**. This is a capital-intensive business that requires continuous, massive R&D spending while facing fierce competition.

### QTL: Technology licensing (roughly 15% of revenue, but a huge share of profit)

Based on Qualcomm's accumulated standard-essential patents in 3G/4G/5G, it collects patent licensing fees from phone makers worldwide. FY2025 revenue was **$6.4 billion**, pre-tax profit was **$4.0 billion**, with a margin of over **63%**.

The two businesses are fundamentally different in nature:

| Dimension | QCT chips | QTL patent licensing |
|------|---------|------------|
| Nature | Selling products | Collecting a "toll" |
| Margin | Moderate (squeezed by competition) | Extremely high (>63%) |
| Moat | Moderate (requires continuous R&D leadership) | Extremely deep (standard-essential patents can't be bypassed) |
| Growth potential | Depends on opening new markets | Limited (tied to phone shipment volumes) |
| Analogy | More like Nvidia | More like a highway tollbooth |

**The key to understanding Qualcomm is that these two businesses form a flywheel.** Chip R&D continuously generates new patents that enrich the patent pool; the high profits from patent licensing provide stable funding for high-risk chip R&D. Cut off either one, and the other would be significantly damaged.

(Data source: Qualcomm FY2025 10-K)

## Three common misjudgments about Qualcomm

### Misjudgment one: "Qualcomm just sells phone chips"

Phone chips are indeed Qualcomm's largest revenue source — in FY2026 Q2, QCT's handset business had quarterly revenue of about **$6.0 billion**. But if that's all you see, you'll miss several important shifts:

- **Automotive chips**: FY2026 Q2 quarterly revenue of **$1.33 billion** (+38% year-over-year), already annualizing above **$5 billion**, with management targeting an annualized run rate of **$6 billion** by the exit of FY2026 and **$8 billion** by FY2029
- **IoT**: FY2026 Q2 quarterly revenue of **$1.73 billion** (+9% year-over-year)
- **PC processors**: the Snapdragon X2 series holds roughly **10%** share of the Windows laptop market, with more than 100 design configurations in mass production or under development
- **Patent licensing**: reliably contributes **over $4 billion** in pre-tax profit every year

The share of revenue from non-handset businesses has risen from about **30%** three years ago to about **40%** today. Qualcomm is transforming from a "phone chip company" into a "smart computing platform company" — and whether this transformation succeeds will determine its story for the next five years.

(Data sources: Qualcomm Q2 FY2026 earnings report, PCWorld reporting)

### Misjudgment two: "Apple designed its own baseband — Qualcomm is finished"

This is the most frequently raised bearish argument among investors discussing Qualcomm. Part of the fact is correct, but the conclusion needs careful math.

**Fact**: Apple's self-designed C1 baseband chip already shipped in the iPhone 16e in 2025. In fall 2026, C1 will appear in more iPhone 17 models. The Ganymede baseband, launching in 2026, will support mmWave. By 2027, Apple expects to fully stop using Qualcomm basebands.

**Magnitude of the impact**: Qualcomm earns roughly **$5.7-5.9 billion** a year in baseband chip procurement revenue from Apple (note: sources differ on the figure — the $2 billion used in earlier working drafts was a conservative estimate; the latest data shows the total, including RF components, is higher). This revenue will go to zero within 2-3 years.

**But there's a crucial "however"**: Apple's self-designed baseband does not affect patent licensing fees. For every iPhone Apple sells, it still has to pay Qualcomm's QTL division a patent licensing fee (about **$8** per unit), because 3G/4G/5G standard-essential patents can't be bypassed.

**The counter to the bear case**: automotive chip growth is currently outpacing the rate at which Apple revenue is disappearing. Automotive's annualized revenue has already surpassed $5 billion in FY2026, and the $45 billion design-win pipeline means this growth curve is far from peaking. Management is well prepared for the Apple decoupling — this isn't a "sudden stab in the back" risk, it's a "known, actively hedged" risk.

(Data sources: AppleInsider, Qualcomm Q2 FY2026 earnings call, Futurum Research)

### Misjudgment three: "Semiconductor companies are all cyclical stocks not worth holding long-term"

This judgment holds for most chip companies, but it needs to be discounted for Qualcomm. The reason lies in that patent-licensing business with over 63% margins.

Look at FY2023: that year, the global phone market went through inventory destocking, and Qualcomm's total revenue plunged **19.2%**. But QTL patent-licensing revenue only dipped slightly — because patent fees are charged based on phone shipment volume, and even if phones aren't selling well, as long as they're still being manufactured, Qualcomm still collects.

**QTL is Qualcomm's "counter-cyclical cushion."** It keeps Qualcomm from swinging as violently during industry downturns as pure chip companies (like MediaTek) do.

But this cushion isn't unlimited. QTL revenue growth has essentially plateaued (tied to global phone shipments of about 1.2 billion units/year), so the real growth engine has to come from new markets within QCT.

(Data source: Qualcomm FY2023-FY2025 annual reports)

## Understanding Qualcomm requires answering four questions

If forced to define Qualcomm in a single sentence, the closest version might be:

> **Qualcomm = a wireless-communications tollbooth with a 63% margin (QTL) + a chip-design machine racing simultaneously across phones, automotive, PCs, and IoT (QCT) + a transformation story that is losing its largest customer (Apple) while trying to fill the gap with new customers.**

To understand this company, at least four questions need answers:

**The same company: bulls say "buy a patent money-printer plus an automotive growth engine at 21x earnings"; bears say "Apple is leaving, China could blow up, and phones have peaked."**
