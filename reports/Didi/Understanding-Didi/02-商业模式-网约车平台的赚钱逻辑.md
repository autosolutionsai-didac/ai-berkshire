# The Money-Making Logic of a Ride-Hailing Platform

> *Understanding Didi* series · Part 02 · Business Model
> Reading time: about 10 minutes

---

## How does Didi actually make money?

"Didi takes a 20% cut" is one of the most widespread misconceptions in the Chinese internet world. The real number is: for every 100 yuan of fare, Didi keeps about **4 yuan**. The rest goes to drivers, subsidies, and taxes.

That 4% figure is the key to understanding Didi's entire business model — it determines where Didi's profit ceiling sits, how narrow its pricing room is, and why a company with 226.7 billion yuan in annual revenue posted a GAAP net profit of only about 990 million yuan. Let's take the machine apart.

Didi's **226.7 billion yuan** in 2025 revenue came from three segments:

| Segment | Revenue (billion yuan) | Share | Core metric |
|------|------------|------|---------|
| **China Mobility** | **201.9** | **89%** | ~40 million average daily orders, GTV of 333.8 billion |
| **International** | **14.95** | **6.6%** | Operating in 14 countries, orders +24.7% |
| **Other/Innovation** | **9.84** | 4.4% | Freight + autonomous driving + fuel/charging |

(Source: Didi 2025 20-F annual report)

**89% of revenue comes from China Mobility**. To understand Didi, you first have to understand this cash cow.

## How the money from a typical order is split

Suppose you book an Express ride and pay **100 yuan**. Here's where that 100 yuan goes:

| Item | Amount (yuan) | Note |
|------|-----------|------|
| Driver take-home | **~79** | Official figure: average commission **14-15%** |
| Government taxes + payment channel fees | ~4 | VAT + WeChat/Alipay processing fees |
| Rider subsidies/coupons | ~5-7 | User acquisition, engagement |
| Driver incentives/order bonuses | ~7 | Peak-hour and bad-weather subsidies |
| **Didi's net revenue** | **~4-4.3** | **Net take rate ~4%** |
| **Didi's EBITA** | **~1.8-2.0** | After R&D, operations, and compliance costs |

(Source: Didi 20-F, industry estimates. Note: figures vary significantly by city and vehicle type; this is a national-average approximation)

**Key findings**:

1. **The "20% commission" is a gross figure — the net take rate is only 4%**. The 16-percentage-point gap in between is all subsidies and incentives.
2. **For every 100 yuan of GTV, Didi actually pockets only 4 yuan** — this is an extremely thin-margin business.
3. **There is almost no room to raise prices** — regulators capped the industry-wide commission ceiling at **27%** starting in August 2024 (some orders had previously reached 29%), and public attention to driver income remains high.

**Counterpoint**: a 4% net take rate looks thin, but the base is **450.8 billion yuan** in GTV — 4% of that is an absolute value of roughly 18 billion yuan. Moreover, under a platform model, marginal costs are extremely low; each additional order adds almost no incremental cost.

## Two-sided network effects: Didi's core moat

Ride-hailing is a classic **two-sided network effect** business:

```
More riders → more orders → more drivers willing to drive → shorter wait times → more riders
```

The strength of this flywheel directly determines the depth of the moat. A few metrics to gauge it:

| Metric | Didi (2025) | Note |
|------|-------------|------|
| Average daily orders | **~40-45 million** | The world's largest single-market ride-hailing platform |
| Annual active users | **749 million** | Close to China's total mobile internet user base |
| Driver wait time for an order | **< 2 minutes** (tier-1 cities) | A direct result of network density |
| Rider wait time for a car | **~3-5 minutes** (tier-1 cities) | An experience-based barrier |
| ETA prediction accuracy | 12 years of accumulated data | An algorithmic barrier |

(Source: Didi 20-F, industry reports)

**The core logic of network effects**: once a platform's driver density in a given city reaches a critical threshold, latecomers can almost never subsidize their way to the top — because users can already hail a car the moment they open the app, leaving them no reason to switch.

This explains a counterintuitive phenomenon: **from 2021 to 2023, Didi's app was pulled from app stores for two years, and its market share fell from 90%+ to ~70% — but once the app was restored, its share stabilized and did not keep declining**.

**Counterpoint**: the network effect is real, but its strength is weakening. Amap's aggregation model doesn't need to build its own driver pool — it only needs to plug in small and mid-sized fleets to offer a "one tap, hail across the whole network" experience. Aggregator-platform orders grew from **7%** of the whole industry in 2019 to **31%** in 2024 (source: industry reports). If this trend continues, Didi's network effect will be "diluted."

## International business: a second growth curve, or a cash-burning pit?

Didi's international business covers **14 countries**. 2025 figures:

| Metric | 2025 | YoY |
|------|------|------|
| Revenue | **14.95 billion yuan** | +24.7% |
| Order volume | **4.505 billion orders** | +24.7% |
| GTV | **117 billion yuan** | +28.2% |
| EBITA | Profitable in the first three quarters, **a 3.44 billion yuan loss in Q4 alone** (Brazil food-delivery expansion + Mexico subsidies) | — |

(Source: Didi 2025 20-F annual report)

Core markets:

| Market | Brand | Status |
|------|------|------|
| **Brazil** | **99** | 55 million+ users, profitable since 2024, food delivery live in 30+ cities |
| **Mexico** | DiDi | "Mobility + food delivery + fintech" bundle, in a subsidy phase |
| Colombia, Chile, Peru | DiDi | Latin American expansion |
| Japan, Australia, New Zealand | DiDi | Asia-Pacific footprint |
| Egypt | DiDi | Beachhead in Africa |

**Bull case**: Brazil's 99 is Didi's most successful internationalization case — already profitable, and expanding from mobility into food delivery and fintech. If Mexico also works out, the international business could become Didi's "second leg."

**Bear case**: international EBITA lost **3.44 billion yuan** in Q4 2025 alone — full-year profit was concentrated in the first three quarters, and Q4 swung to a large loss due to Brazil's food-delivery expansion and a subsidy war in Mexico. The core question for internationalization is **whether the subsidies are sustainable** — Uber's experience in Latin America and Southeast Asia shows that local incumbents (such as 99's rival iFood) won't easily cede market share.

## Autonomous driving: a $5 billion option

Didi Autonomous Driving is a story of its own:

| Key milestone | Detail |
|---------|------|
| August 2023 | Sold its smart-vehicle + Da Vinci team to Xpeng for **7.44 billion yuan**, retaining its L4 Robotaxi business |
| March 2025 | Completed a new funding round at a valuation of about **$5 billion** |
| January 2026 | The R2 Robotaxi, developed with GAC Aion, began road testing in Guangzhou |
| March 2026 | R2 obtained a Beijing intelligent connected vehicle road-testing license |
| 2026 (planned) | First overseas Robotaxi test, in the UAE |

(Source: Didi announcements, Gasgoo, electrive.com, CNBC)

**Core logic**: Didi's unique edge in autonomous driving is that it already owns China's largest ride-hailing network. A robotaxi doesn't need to rebuild the demand side — plugging directly into the Didi app brings orders immediately. That's something Baidu Apollo, Pony.ai, and WeRide all lack.

**Counterpoint**: the commercialization timeline for L4 autonomous driving keeps slipping. The $5 billion valuation is a private-market funding-round price, and the liquidity discount is enormous. And even if robotaxi technology matures, it could **disrupt Didi's own business model** — if drivers are no longer needed, the economics of the commission model change entirely.

## Electrification: an overlooked structural tailwind

Didi's 2025 annual report disclosed a figure that's easy to overlook:

> **5.1 million registered electric vehicles, with 68% of monthly mileage driven on EVs**

(Source: Didi 2025 20-F annual report)

What does this mean?

- An EV's **energy cost** is **one-third to one-quarter** that of a gasoline car
- For drivers, lower cost per kilometer → higher net income → greater willingness to stay on the platform
- For Didi, lower driver costs → less subsidy pressure → room for the net take rate to rise

**This is a structural shift happening quietly**: China's ride-hailing electrification rate far outpaces every other market globally, and Didi is the biggest beneficiary.

**Counterpoint**: the electrification tailwind is shared across the whole industry, not unique to Didi. The small and mid-sized fleet operators on Amap's aggregation platform are electrifying too.

## Overall verdict on the business model

Pulling the breakdown above together:

### Strengths (the moat)

| Dimension | Strength | Note |
|------|------|------|
| Two-sided network effects | **Strong** | A density barrier from 40 million daily orders |
| Brand as a verb | **Strong** | "Didi yixia" ("Didi it") = hail a ride |
| 12 years of data + algorithms | **Medium-strong** | ETA, dispatching, pricing models |
| First-mover in electrification | **Medium** | 68% of mileage electrified |
| Shareholder structure | **Medium** | Backed jointly by Tencent, Alibaba, SoftBank, and Uber |

### Weaknesses (fragile points)

| Dimension | Severity | Note |
|------|-------|------|
| Net take rate of only 4% | **High** | Thin margins, almost no room to raise prices |
| Erosion from aggregators | **Medium-high** | Amap's share of orders rose from 7% to 31% |
| Regulatory sensitivity | **High** | Data security is the "heart condition" |
| Unstable profitability of international business | **Medium** | Q4 loss of 3.44 billion yuan |

**One-line summary**: Didi's business model is a combination of **"strong network effects + weak pricing power"** — the moat sits on the demand side (user habits + driver density), while the fragile point sits on the supply side (commissions are squeezed, subsidies can't stop). It isn't a Pinduoduo-style "algorithmic advertising machine" (60%+ gross margin) — it's closer to Uber: a matching machine of massive scale but very thin margins.
