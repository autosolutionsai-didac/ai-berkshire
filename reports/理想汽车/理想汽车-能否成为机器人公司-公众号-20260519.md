# Can Li Auto Become a Robotics Company?

On May 18, Li Auto's Hong Kong-listed shares plunged 14%. But what interests me more than the crash itself is the humanoid robotics team Li Auto quietly formed back in January.

Li Xiang made a bold claim: to become "one of no more than 3 companies globally that simultaneously build foundation models, chips, operating systems, and embodied intelligence."

Is that statement strategic foresight, or just talk? I did a full study.

---

## First, a technical question to settle: what does Li Auto's chip actually do?

Li Auto released a chip called M100 this year, using an "orchestrated dataflow architecture" aimed at edge AI inference.

What does that mean?

A traditional chip (von Neumann architecture) is like a worker executing tasks in sequence — no matter whether the data is ready or not, it has to wait in line to be processed. A dataflow architecture works completely differently — computation happens the moment data is ready, with no need to queue.

An analogy: the traditional architecture is a cafeteria line, one window serving people one at a time; the dataflow architecture is a buffet, where you grab whatever dish is ready.

The advantage of this architecture in AI inference is significant. Groq's dataflow chip has an inference energy efficiency 35 times that of Nvidia's H100, and NextSilicon claims 4 times the energy efficiency of Nvidia's Blackwell.

By building the M100, Li Auto is signaling that it doesn't just want to make cars, and doesn't just want to make robots — it wants to build the entire AI computing stack starting from the underlying chip. The ambition is genuinely large.

But on the other hand, making chips and making robots are two different things. Chips can be developed efficiently with a small team; robots require large-scale systems-integration capability. Making a good chip doesn't mean you can make a good robot.

---

## What does it actually take to build a humanoid robot?

After studying a large number of industry reports and case studies, I've organized the sufficient conditions for humanoid robotics into four tiers:

**Tier 1: missing any one means failure**
- AI foundation-model / embodied-intelligence capability
- Real deployment scenarios with paying customers
- Ample and sustained funding

**Tier 2: determines who wins**
- Data flywheel (deployment → data → training → stronger models → more deployment)
- Hardware systems integration
- Motion control + perception systems

**Tier 3: determines the speed of scaling**
- Supply chain and mass production
- Battery life (currently 2-4 hours; commercialization needs 8-12 hours)
- Cost control (from $100,000-500,000 down to $20,000-50,000)

In one sentence: **the sufficient condition for making a humanoid robot = AI capability x scenario deployment x funding endurance x data flywheel.**

Hardware, supply chain, and mass production are important, but they're gradually becoming things that "can be outsourced or borrowed." AI capability and scenario data are the moat that's genuinely hard to replicate.

This also explains why Tesla (FSD data + manufacturing scale + massive funding) is seen as the most competitive player — it has accumulated strength at every single tier.

## Li Auto vs. three rivals: who has met how many conditions?

I picked four companies for a side-by-side comparison: Li Auto, Xpeng, Xiaomi, and Unitree.

The conclusion first:

| Company | Sufficient conditions met | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Unitree | 5/9 | Leading in commercialization |
| Xpeng | 5/9 | Most comprehensive technology, mass production imminent |
| Xiaomi | 2/9 | Deep reserves but slow to deploy |
| **Li Auto** | **1/9** | **Clear resolve but execution just beginning** |

The only condition Li Auto has met is **funding** — a cash reserve of RMB 101.2 billion, the most ample of the four.

### Unitree: the only one actually making money

2025 figures: revenue of RMB 1.7 billion (up 335%), net profit of RMB 600 million (up 674%), 5,500 humanoid robots shipped, 32.4% global market share.

Its in-house M107 motor has a torque density of 120 Nm/kg, at only one-fifth to one-tenth the cost of comparable overseas products. The G1 sells for RMB 85,000 and the R1 for RMB 39,900. It has already proven that humanoid robots aren't just demos — they can genuinely be sold for money.

Its weak points are that its dexterous hand effort has only just started, its AI foundation model relies on external partnerships, and its IPO raise of RMB 4.2 billion gives it limited ammunition compared with automakers.

### Xpeng: the automaker with the strongest full technology stack

The IRON robot has 82 degrees of freedom, 3 in-house Turing chips (2,250 TOPS combined), a 72-billion-parameter physical-world foundation model, a 10,000-GPU computing cluster, all-solid-state batteries, and a dexterous hand that replicates the human hand 1:1.

Among automakers, Xpeng is the only one that has truly achieved full in-house development across chips + foundation model + actuators + a data factory. Mass production at scale by the end of 2026 and its first partnership with Baosteel are the key validation milestones to watch.

Its weak points are that it hasn't yet begun mass-production delivery, and its RMB 47.6 billion in cash is less ample than Li Auto's or Xiaomi's.

### Xiaomi: the one with the most potential but the slowest pace

CyberOne launched in 2022; four years on, the third generation still hasn't formally debuted. In March 2025, the company itself denied reports of mass production.

But its technical reserves shouldn't be underestimated: MiMo-Embodied is reportedly the industry's first cross-embodiment foundation model unifying autonomous driving and embodied intelligence, achieving SOTA on 29 benchmarks. Its dexterous hand was developed by engineers poached from Tesla's Optimus team, with volume reduced by 60% and having passed 150,000 grasping tests.

Its five-year R&D budget is RMB 200 billion, with RMB 60 billion earmarked for AI over three years. Money isn't the problem — speed is. Lei Jun has said there will be new progress in 2026, but the market has already been waiting a long time.

### Li Auto: plenty of money, but almost nothing else

The humanoid robotics team was only formed in January 2026. It has planned a two-wheeled robot (expected to launch mid-year) and a bipedal humanoid robot (timeline unclear).

**Across ten capability comparisons, Li Auto scores an X on 6, a triangle (partial credit) on 3, and a checkmark on only 1.**

## The core contradiction

Li Auto's problem isn't a lack of money — it's a lack of time.

Motor design requires repeated iteration and testing; you can't accelerate it just by throwing money at it. Motion-control algorithms need to be fed with real-world environment data; simulation can only solve part of the problem. A dexterous hand's precision and reliability need tens of thousands of physical tests to validate. Mass production requires stepping through countless pitfalls from unit one to unit one thousand to establish standards.

Unitree has already walked through these pitfalls — the experience behind shipping 5,500 humanoid robots isn't something RMB 101.2 billion in cash can simply buy.

Xpeng has also had at least a two-year head start — from project kickoff to the IRON launch to mass-production planning. Li Auto has just started hiring.

**The gap is at least two years.**

And the competitive landscape two years from now won't wait for Li Auto. Unitree is already sprinting toward an IPO, Xpeng is mass-producing by year-end, and Xiaomi may finally break its silence. Two years from now, Li Auto won't be facing today's rivals — it will be facing rivals that have already completed the first turn of the data flywheel.

---

## So what about the M100 chip?

The M100 uses an orchestrated dataflow architecture for edge AI inference, and that direction is correct. The energy-efficiency advantage of dataflow architectures in AI inference has already been validated by companies like Groq and NextSilicon.

But a chip is only one component of a robotics system. Having a good chip doesn't mean having a good robot, just as having a good engine doesn't mean having a good car — you still need the chassis, suspension, transmission, body, and vehicle tuning.

For Li Auto, the M100's greater value probably lies in autonomous driving and in-car AI scenarios, not robotics. In robotics, what it needs to solve first is "can it actually be built," not "which chip to use."

---

## Conclusion

**Can Li Auto become a robotics company? Yes — but not now, and not easily.**

It holds three cards:
1. **RMB 101.2 billion in cash** — enough to afford to make many mistakes
2. **Accumulated autonomous-driving AI** — perception, simulation, and foundation-model capabilities that are transferable
3. **The M100 chip** — a technical reserve for edge AI inference

It's missing three things:
1. **Time** — at least two years behind Xpeng, three years behind Unitree
2. **Hardware capability** — motors, dexterous hands, and motion control all starting from zero
3. **Scenario data** — no real-world deployment, so the flywheel hasn't started turning yet

If you ask me whether Li Auto can build a humanoid robot five years from now? Very likely yes. It has enough money, sufficiently firm strategic resolve, and can lean on the automotive supply chain.

But if you ask me whether Li Auto can become a leader in the humanoid-robotics race? That's very hard. This isn't a track where being a "late mover" is an advantage — the data flywheel means early movers keep accelerating. Li Auto would need to cover, within two years, ground that others took three to five years to cover, just to have a chance of catching up.

RMB 101.2 billion in cash can buy a window of time, but it can't buy an exception to the laws of physics.
