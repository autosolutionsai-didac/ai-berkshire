## Investment Logic Chain

```
AI model scale keeps expanding (training + inference demand surge)
    → GPU/AI chip demand explodes (NVIDIA data center revenue reaches $197.3B in FY26)
        → Advanced-node capacity gets tight (TSMC CoWoS at full capacity, HBM in short supply)
            → Upstream equipment/materials benefit (ASML order backlog €38.8B, SK Hynix gross margin 79%)
    → Hyperscaler capex arms race (combined 2026 capex of $660-690B)
        → The entire server/networking/cooling/power chain benefits
            → But profit distribution is extremely uneven: chip design > manufacturing > memory > networking >> server assembly
```

### Logic Chain Verification

| Link | Core hypothesis | Verifying event | Status |
|------|---------|---------|------|
| AI demand → chip demand | AI training/inference keeps expanding | NVIDIA FY26 data center revenue $197.3B (+65%); hyperscaler capex $660B+ | ✅ Strongly confirmed |
| Chip demand → capacity tightness | Advanced nodes/packaging/HBM in short supply | TSMC CoWoS booked solid through 2027; SK Hynix/Micron 2026 capacity fully sold out | ✅ Strongly confirmed |
| Capacity tightness → upstream price hikes | Chokepoint segments hold pricing power | TSMC raising prices 5-10% in 2026; HBM prices up 20%/year; ASML EUV units $200-400M each | ✅ Strongly confirmed |
| Hyperscaler arms race → entire chain benefits | Capex keeps growing | The big six combined at $660-690B in 2026; Goldman projects a cumulative $1.15T for 2025-2027 | ✅ Strongly confirmed |
| In-house chips → eating into NVIDIA | Long-term de-NVIDIA-ization trend | Google TPU v7, AWS Trainium 1.4 million units, 2026 custom ASIC shipments up 44.6% vs GPU up 16.1% | ⚠️ Trend confirmed but progressing slowly |

### I. Upstream of Upstream — Design Tools & IP (the industry chain's "toll collector")

#### 1.1 EDA Tools: One of the World's Strongest Oligopolies

**Core conclusion: EDA may be the segment with the deepest moat and the least substitutability in the entire semiconductor value chain.**

| Company | Market share | Gross margin | Operating margin | Pricing power |
|------|---------|--------|-----------|--------|
| Synopsys (SNPS) | 31% | 76-80% | ~35% | Very strong |
| Cadence (CDNS) | 30% | **86%** | 35% | Very strong |
| Siemens EDA | 13% | N/A (within group) | N/A | Strong |
| **Combined (three firms)** | **74-85%+** | - | - | - |

**Why it's irreplaceable (substitutability: 1/5):**
- Customer retention runs close to **100%** — the EDA industry has almost no such thing as customer churn
- 80-85% **recurring revenue** (subscription model)
- Switching EDA platforms means: enormous cost, a collapse in productivity, a data-migration nightmare, and the risk of chip-design failure
- Large customers typically **buy from both** Synopsys and Cadence at once — even customers can't choose between the two
- The more complex the chip, the deeper the dependence on EDA — the AI chip boom is a direct tailwind for EDA

**Domestic Chinese EDA (Empyrean Technology and others):** Domestic-EDA penetration exceeds 30% for analog-chip EDA, but is under 15% for digital-chip EDA, and high-end segments (hardware emulation, formal verification, timing analysis) are nearly blank.

**Duan Yongping's question — is this a good business?**
> An excellent business. 86% gross margin, 100% customer retention, 85% recurring revenue, an oligopoly, and the more complex chips get, the more indispensable they become. The only issue is the valuation has always been rich (P/E typically 40-60x), but good businesses are never cheap.

#### 1.2 ARM: A "Toll Collector" Model with 97% Gross Margin

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| Mobile chip IP share | >99% |
| Cloud computing CPU IP share | Up from 9% to **20%**, projected to reach **90%** of AI ASICs by 2029 |
| FY2025 revenue | $4.671B (licensing $1.839B + royalties $2.168B) |
| Gross margin | **~97.5%** |
| Operating margin | Over 45% |

**Source of pricing power:** Armv9 architecture royalty rates run roughly **2x** those of Armv8; CSS (Compute Subsystems) licensing carries royalty rates above **10% per chip**. Whether it's AWS Graviton, Google Axion, Microsoft Cobalt, or NVIDIA Grace — all are built on ARM architecture, so ARM "collects royalties no matter who wins."

**RISC-V threat assessment:** Limited threat in the short term (2-3 years), concentrated in low-end MCU/IoT. China is pushing RISC-V hard to reduce ARM dependence, but it's unlikely to displace ARM in data-center scenarios within 5 years.

- Pricing power is very strong: CoWoS prices rose **15-20%** in 2025

### IV. Midstream — HBM, Servers, Networking

#### 4.1 HBM (High Bandwidth Memory): The Dark-Horse Profit Center

**Why HBM is the lifeline of AI chips:** Conventional DDR5 bandwidth is around 50-60 GB/s, while a single HBM3E chip delivers **1.2 TB/s** (a 20x gap). Without HBM, a GPU's real-world performance would be drastically diminished.

| Company | HBM share | Gross margin | Pricing power | 2026 outlook |
|------|---------|--------|--------|---------|
| **SK Hynix** | **62%** | Full-year **79%** | Very strong, 2026 price hikes of 20% | Capacity fully sold out |
| **Micron** | **21%** | Above the company average | Strong | Capacity fully sold out |
| **Samsung** | ~17% | Below SK Hynix | Weak (HBM3E qualification delayed 18 months) | Primary supplier for Google TPU (60%+) |

**Key data:**
- A single HBM3E chip sells for $60-100, versus just $5-10 for equivalent-capacity DDR5 (a **10-20x** premium)
- HBM accounts for **23%** of global DRAM wafer capacity
- Market size: $35B in 2025 → $100B in 2028 (CAGR ~40%)

**Substitutability: 1/5 | Pricing power: Very strong**

**Duan Yongping's question:** How durable is SK Hynix's HBM position?
> Very durable for at least 3-5 years. HBM requires TSV (through-silicon via) technology plus CoWoS advanced-packaging capability, and the capacity-expansion cycle is long (18-24 months) — it took Samsung 18 months just to pass NVIDIA's qualification. But HBM4 could reshuffle the field, and it's worth watching.

**Optical modules:** Chinese vendors lead globally — Innolight (23-30% share) and Eoptolink (25-30%). Market growth exceeds 60%, but gross margins are on the low side (25-30%).

#### 4.4 Cooling/Liquid Cooling & Power

**Liquid cooling has gone from "optional" to "mandatory":** The GB200's single-rack draw is 132kW, versus a traditional air-cooling ceiling of 30-40kW. Vertiv and Schneider Electric form a duopoly, and their order backlogs provide years of visibility.

**Power demand:** Data center electricity consumption is set to go from 55GW in 2025 to 122GW in 2030 (doubling). Microsoft has signed a deal to restart the Three Mile Island nuclear plant. Eaton's data center orders are up 200%.

### Key Insights

**1. The "pick-and-shovel sellers" won — and the shovels themselves vary enormously**

Profit is extremely concentrated in the upstream segments that hold monopoly positions. NVIDIA, TSMC, ASML, and SK Hynix together may capture more than **40 cents** of profit out of every $1 spent on AI compute.

**2. Gross margin is the ultimate litmus test for a "good business"**

- 97% gross margin (ARM) and 86% gross margin (Cadence) = "money-printing machines"
- 73% gross margin (NVIDIA) and 79% gross margin (SK Hynix) = "super businesses"
- 56% gross margin (TSMC) = "a good business, but capital-intensive"
- 4-20% gross margin (server OEMs) = "hard-earned money" — no matter how fast it grows, it's not a good business

**3. Oracle is the cautionary counter-example**

Oracle's AI cloud gross margin is just **14%** — it's selling compute on NVIDIA's behalf, with virtually all the profit captured upstream. This is a warning to investors: not every "AI concept stock" actually makes money.

**4. Profit is starting to migrate from upstream to downstream**

Anthropic's inference gross margin has risen from 38% to **70%+**; as inference costs fall and agentic AI takes off, the economics of downstream application companies hit an inflection point at the end of 2025. Whether this inflection holds is still something to watch.

### Historical Analogy

**The closest analogy: the 1990s internet-infrastructure investment boom**

| Dimension | 1990s internet | 2024-2026 AI |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| Infrastructure arms race | Cisco/Nortel/fiber-optic companies | NVIDIA/TSMC/hyperscale cloud |
| Ultimate winner | The application layer (Google/Amazon/Facebook) | TBD |
| Infrastructure companies' fate | Cisco survives but never returned to its 2000 market cap; most fiber-optic companies went bankrupt | ? |
| Takeaway | "Selling shovels" offers high short-term certainty, but the biggest long-term winners may sit at the application layer | Today's "shovel sellers" are already priced for a great deal of optimism |

**The key difference:** NVIDIA/TSMC/ASML's degree of monopoly far exceeds Cisco's back in the day — Cisco's switches had substitutes, but EUV lithography, CoWoS packaging, and the CUDA ecosystem currently have none.

## Part Five: Civilizational Trend Judgment (Li Lu's Framework)

**Question: Is AI compute a "civilizational paradigm shift" or a "cyclical fad"?**

**Judgment: A civilizational paradigm shift.** Reasons:
1. AI is changing the very nature of knowledge work — analogous to the Industrial Revolution's impact on physical labor
2. The world's five largest companies (Apple, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Google, Amazon) are all deeply committed to AI
3. The $1.15 trillion in capex planned for 2025-2027 is not speculation — it reflects commercially validated enterprise demand
4. Anthropic's gross margin has risen from 38% to 70%+; the economics of AI applications hit an inflection point at the end of 2025

**The 10-20 year endgame:**
- **Segments most likely to see winner-take-all outcomes:** Chip design (NVIDIA or its successor), wafer foundry (TSMC)
- **Segments most likely to be disrupted:** Server OEMs (least differentiated), GPU rental brokers (dependent on NVIDIA's pricing)
- **The biggest wild card:** Where the balance point lands between in-house chips and general-purpose GPUs

### Simulated Commentary from the Four Masters

> **Buffett:** "In this AI compute chain, the two I understand best are TSMC and ASML — their moats are plainly visible, and they'll almost certainly still be around in ten years. NVIDIA is a great company, but I'm not 100% sure the CUDA ecosystem can lock in customers forever. I wouldn't touch server assemblers — a 4% gross-margin business is not a good business no matter how fast it grows."

> **Munger:** "Why wouldn't a smart person buy NVIDIA? Because a 30x P/E means the market has already priced in at least 3-5 years of high growth, while the in-house-chip trend is accelerating — Google's TPU delivers 4x the price/performance of H100, and AWS has already deployed 1.4 million Trainium chips. I'm not saying NVIDIA will lose, but its share falling from 80% to 60% is entirely possible. What does that mean? It means the current valuation leaves no room for error."

> **Duan Yongping:** "Looking at this chain, the best business models are EDA and ARM — 86% and 97% gross margins, 100% customer retention, recurring revenue; you'd have a hard time finding businesses like these even with a lantern in daylight. The problem is that both companies' P/Es are too high — you have to wait until the market mistakenly beats them down for some reason before they're worth buying. Investing isn't about finding good companies; it's about finding good companies at good prices."

> **Li Lu:** "AI really is a civilizational-scale paradigm shift — it's doing to knowledge work what the Industrial Revolution did to physical labor. But history tells us that in the early stages of a technological revolution, the 'shovel sellers' (infrastructure providers) profit first, while the ultimate winners tend to sit at the application layer. Investing in Cisco in the 1990s was very profitable, but over the long run Google and Amazon created far more value. Today's NVIDIA is the Cisco of its era — but with one crucial difference: NVIDIA's degree of monopoly far exceeds what Cisco ever had. That makes it safer, but it also makes its valuation richer."
