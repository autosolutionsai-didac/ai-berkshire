## $90 to $736 and back to $597 — Same Company, Three Different Stories

In November 2022, Meta fell to **$90**. There was no fresh bad news — the market simply converged on a single narrative overnight: this was a machine burning cash to build a metaverse that nobody would ever use. At the time, Wall Street's language was almost unanimous — "a social media company that has lost its way."

Two years later, the stock broke above **$600**, and the same crowd swapped in a new label: king of AI advertising. It touched an all-time high of **$736** in February 2025, then slid back to around **$597** by May 2026, as the narrative flipped once again to "AI capex spiraling out of control." What actually happened in between? In early 2023 Zuckerberg declared a "Year of Efficiency" and cut **21,000 jobs** — the company didn't change lanes, and it didn't invent a new product. Three and a half years, three narrative reversals — and in hindsight, most of those reversals look like overreactions.

**That pattern alone is worth flagging: narratives flip far faster than the underlying business actually changes.**

Open the latest earnings report and Meta shows a set of numbers that demand a fresh look:

| Metric | Value | Scale |
|------|------|---------|
| Monthly active users (Family of Apps) | **3.98 billion** (early 2025) | Covers roughly half the world's population |
| Daily active users | **3.56 billion** (Q1 2026) | +4% year over year |
| Full-year 2025 revenue | **$201.0 billion** (+22%) | |
| Full-year 2025 net income | **$60.46 billion** | Down roughly 3% year over year (tax-rate effect) |
| Full-year 2024 net income | **$62.36 billion** (+60%) | 2024 was a breakout profit year |
| Q1 2026 revenue | **$56.31 billion** (+33%) | Growth reaccelerated |
| Q1 2026 net income | **$26.77 billion** (+61%) | Includes an $8.03 billion one-time tax benefit |
| Q1 2026 adjusted net income | **$18.7 billion** | Excluding the tax benefit, still 9.6% above expectations |
| 2026 capex guidance | **$125–145 billion** | About 54%–63% of projected revenue |
| Current market cap | **~$1.59 trillion** | |
| Current P/E (TTM) | **~22x** | |
| Forward P/E | **~18.4x** | Peer average is about 29x |
| 2025 buybacks + dividends | **$26.26 billion + $5.32 billion** | Generous shareholder returns |
| Reality Labs cumulative losses | **~$83.6 billion** | Cumulative, 2020–2025 |

Put these numbers side by side: annual revenue of $200 billion, net income of $60 billion, monthly active users covering half the world's population, and a forward P/E of just 18x. At the same time, the company is pouring more than $125 billion a year into AI, and the metaverse has burned through a cumulative $83.6 billion.

Think "growth blue-chip, cheap valuation"? You may be underestimating the uncertainty around $145 billion in capex.
Think "bottomless money pit"? You may be overlooking the 33% revenue growth rate and the 35% return on capital.

What this series sets out to do is **take Meta apart layer by layer and let you draw your own conclusions**.

## What Kind of Company Is Meta, Really?

Define Meta in one sentence:

> **Meta = the world's largest attention-monetization machine (the advertising core business) + a $145 billion AI wager (capital expenditure) + a metaverse experiment that has burned $83.6 billion with no return in sight yet (Reality Labs).**

Each of these three pieces has been analyzed on its own before, but few people weigh them together. **The market's disagreement about Meta is fundamentally a disagreement about how much weight to assign to each of these three things.**

The bulls' weighting: advertising core business 90% + AI acceleration 10% = undervalued
The bears' weighting: advertising core business 60% + AI cash-burn risk 30% + Zuckerberg's risk appetite 10% = high risk

**Both sides are looking at the same financial statements and reaching completely different conclusions.**

## Three Common Misconceptions About Meta

### Misconception One: Meta Is a Social Media Company

That label isn't technically wrong, but it will mislead you about how the business actually works.

**More than 98% of Meta's revenue comes from advertising.** It doesn't sell a social product to users — Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and Messenger are all free to use. Meta's real customers are advertisers, and its real product is **precisely targeted user attention**.

Looked at through the lens of revenue structure, Meta more closely resembles **Google** (77% of revenue from advertising) than Netflix (subscription revenue) or Apple (hardware plus services).

This distinction matters: **Meta's core competitive strength isn't "doing social" — it's "using social products to capture user attention, then using AI to sell that attention at the highest possible price."** That framing is what should determine how you judge the rationality of its AI spending.

### Misconception Two: The Metaverse Has Already Failed, So Meta Is in Trouble

Reality Labs' cumulative losses of **$83.6 billion** (source: Outlook Respawn, compiled from Meta's historical financial reports) look alarming at first glance. But a few things need to be understood:

1. **Meta's core advertising business fully covers this loss.** In 2024, the Family of Apps segment posted operating profit of more than **$70 billion** (source: Meta 2024 annual report) — Reality Labs' loss is a drag on the business, not an existential threat.
2. **Zuckerberg is already scaling back.** In March 2026, Meta shut down the Horizon Worlds VR version and cut roughly 1,500 Reality Labs jobs. Management has already stopped mentioning "the metaverse" on earnings calls.
3. **The $83.6 billion is a sunk cost.** Investment decisions should be based on future cash flows, not past losses.

Counterpoint: even while scaling back, Reality Labs still lost **$19.19 billion** in 2025 (source: Meta 2025 annual report), and management has said the 2026 loss will be "similar in magnitude." If Zuckerberg pivots to a new direction and takes another such gamble in the future, how many more of these can investors stomach?

### Misconception Three: AI Spending Is Guaranteed to Pay Off

The Q1 2026 numbers do look good — revenue growth of 33%, ad impressions up 19%, price per ad up 12%, and the AI-driven Advantage+ toolset now processing more than **$60 billion** in annualized ad spend (source: Meta Q1 2026 earnings report).

But the **$125–145 billion** 2026 capex guidance is a staggering number:

| Comparison | Data |
|--------|------|
| Meta 2023 capex | **~$28.1 billion** |
| Meta 2024 capex | **~$39.2 billion** |
| Meta 2025 capex | **~$72.2 billion** |
| Meta 2026 capex guidance | **$125–145 billion** (up 73% to 101% year over year) |

That's a near-**3x** increase in three years.

When a reporter pressed Zuckerberg on "what's the evidence that $145 billion in AI investment will generate a return," his answer was: "that's a very technical question" (source: Fortune, April 29, 2026).

That is not a reassuring answer.

**AI spending has already generated some initial returns — that's a fact. Whether $145 billion in spending is a reasonable scale — that's an open question.** The rest of this series will break it down in detail.

## A Preview: Comparing Meta With Tencent and Pinduoduo

If you've read *Understanding Tencent* and *Understanding Pinduoduo* — Meta is a third type of investment case, worth examining alongside them:

| Dimension | Tencent | Pinduoduo | Meta |
|------|------|--------|------|
| Asset quality | WeChat is irreplaceable + evergreen gaming franchise | Algorithmic ad machine + political optionality | Global social graph + AI advertising engine |
| Valuation | 14.5x P/E | 10.5x, or 6.3x ex-cash | **18.4x forward P/E** |
| Management | Highly transparent + generous buybacks | Low transparency + zero buybacks, zero dividends | Founder in absolute control + generous buybacks |
| Risk structure | Regulatory tail risk | The Temu political gamble + a trust discount | AI capex + founder's risk appetite |
| Biggest uncertainty | US-China relations | $420 billion in cash it can't access | **$145 billion in cash it's about to spend** |

Tencent's uncertainty sits outside the company (geopolitics); Pinduoduo's sits with management (whether it's willing to return cash); **Meta's uncertainty sits in capital allocation (whether the spending is worth it).**

Three companies, three completely different investment theses.
