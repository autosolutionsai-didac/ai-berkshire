# AI and the Metaverse — A $145 Billion Bet and $83.6 Billion in Sunk Costs

> "Understanding Meta" series · Part 03
> Reading time: about 12 minutes

---

## Two ledgers that will decide Meta's future

$83.6 billion. That is Meta's cumulative net loss on the metaverse, burned from 2020 through 2025, in exchange for a VR version of Horizon Worlds that has since been shut down and a Quest headset with declining sales. And it's not over yet — 2026 is expected to add roughly another $19 billion in losses.

At the same time, Zuckerberg has launched an even bigger bet: 2026 AI capex of $125-145 billion, representing 54%-63% of projected revenue — roughly five times the total annual capex from just three years ago.

Together, these two ledgers are the core reason Meta trades at a valuation discount — the advertising business's numbers are flawless, but the market's distrust of "how Zuckerberg spends money" has pushed the forward P/E down to 18.4x, versus a peer average of 29x.

This installment sets out to untangle both ledgers, one at a time.

## Ledger one: AI capex

### Where is the money going?

The $125-145 billion in capex is directed mainly at three areas:

| Direction | Description |
|------|------|
| **GPU compute** | Large-scale purchases of NVIDIA GPUs for AI model training and inference |
| **Data centers** | New builds and expansions, including land, construction, and power infrastructure |
| **In-house chips** | The MTIA line of custom AI chips, reducing long-term dependence on NVIDIA |

(Source: Meta Q1 2026 earnings call)

The growth rate of capex has been staggering:

| Year | Capex | YoY | % of revenue |
|------|---------|------|---------|
| 2023 | **~$28.1 billion** | — | 21% |
| 2024 | **~$39.2 billion** | +40% | 24% |
| 2025 | **~$72.2 billion** | +84% | 36% |
| 2026E | **$125-145 billion** | +73%~+101% | **54%-63%** |

(Source: Meta annual filings)

**In 2026, capex is projected to exceed half of revenue** — a figure with almost no precedent in the history of large tech companies.

### Has the AI investment already produced returns?

Yes, and the returns are fairly clear-cut:

1. **Advantage+ automated advertising**: processes more than **$60 billion** in ad spend on an annualized basis, directly driving improved ad efficiency and higher prices (source: Meta Q1 2026 earnings)
2. **Upgraded content recommendation**: improved AI models have increased user time spent, driving ad impressions up 19%
3. **Q1 revenue growth of 33%**: far outpacing expense growth, indicating that returns on AI investment are accelerating

Looking only at near-term data, the return on AI investment is positive — every dollar invested has generated more than a dollar of incremental value.

### So where's the problem?

The problem lies in **scale and sustainability**.

**Scale problem**: 2026 capex of $125-145 billion still equals 54%-63% of revenue even if Meta's full-year revenue reaches $230 billion (extrapolated from Q2 guidance). Free cash flow is still expected to exceed $100 billion, but the margin of comfort has been sharply compressed.

**Sustainability problem**: The AI race is an arms race. Google, Microsoft, and Amazon are all pouring huge sums into AI infrastructure. If competitors keep raising the stakes, can Meta afford to stop? Zuckerberg's track record suggests he is unlikely to voluntarily slow down.

**Transparency problem**: When asked about the return on investment for the $145 billion in spending, Zuckerberg said "that's a very technical question" (source: Fortune, April 29, 2026). Management has promised that absolute 2026 operating income will exceed 2025 levels, but has also warned that expense growth will be "notably faster" than revenue growth.

Counterargument: **"Can't clearly see the return" does not equal "no return."** Amazon couldn't prove the ROI of AWS in its early days either, yet in hindsight that turned out to be the best investment in the company's history. If AI truly is a paradigm shift comparable to the internet, over-investing early is better than under-investing.

Response: The Amazon analogy has some merit, but there's a key difference — AWS was a new business with its own independent revenue stream, so its investment and output could be measured separately. Meta's AI investment is mainly enhancing an existing advertising business, making it very hard to isolate AI's independent contribution. That makes judging "whether the investment is excessive" much harder.

## Open-sourcing Llama: giving money to rivals, or locking in an ecosystem?

The most striking element of Meta's AI strategy is **its open-source strategy for the Llama models**.

### Llama's development timeline

| Model | Release date | Characteristics |
|------|---------|------|
| Llama 1 | February 2023 | The first open-source large model, which caused a stir in the industry |
| Llama 2 | July 2023 | Commercially usable; the ecosystem began forming |
| Llama 3 | April 2024 | Major performance gains, closing the gap with GPT-4 |
| Llama 4 Scout / Maverick | April 2025 | The first native multimodal open-source models |

(Source: Meta official announcements)

The Llama family already has more than **25 major partners** (AWS, NVIDIA, Google Cloud, and others), forming an AI ecosystem built around Meta's models.

### The business logic behind open-sourcing

Why give away, for free, models that cost hundreds of millions of dollars to train?

1. **Lower Meta's own R&D costs**: outside developers help find bugs, optimize performance, and expand use cases
2. **Establish an industry standard**: once Llama becomes the "universal foundation" of the AI industry, applications and data built on Llama could ultimately flow back to Meta's ecosystem
3. **Undercut OpenAI's pricing power**: if open-source models are good enough, OpenAI's closed models will struggle to charge high prices
4. **Attract talent**: AI researchers are more willing to join a company that is friendly to open source

### A new closed-source move

Notably, Meta began a **dual-track strategy** in 2026:
- **Open source**: continuing to advance the Llama series
- **Closed source**: released its first commercial closed-source model, **Muse Spark** (source: CNBC, April 28, 2026)

This shows that Meta's open-source strategy is not "unconditional sharing," but a selective approach — open-sourcing foundation models while keeping the most lucrative application-layer models closed.

Counterargument: open-source models give competitors (including TikTok/ByteDance) free access to advanced AI technology, effectively arming rivals with Meta's own money. And while Llama's download numbers and community activity are high, none of that translates directly into Meta's revenue.

Response: This concern isn't unreasonable. But so far, the biggest beneficiaries of Llama are small and mid-sized businesses and cloud platforms, not Meta's direct competitors. ByteDance and Google have their own AI R&D capabilities and don't rely much on Llama. Meanwhile, when small and mid-sized businesses build applications with Llama, they are likely to eventually acquire customers through Meta's advertising platform — an indirect but valuable positive loop.

## $14.3 billion stake purchase in Scale AI

In 2026, Meta paid **$14.3 billion** to acquire a stake in Scale AI and hired Scale AI's CEO, Alexandr Wang, to lead Meta's superintelligence lab (source: CNBC / 247 Wall St).

The significance of this deal:
- **Data-labeling capability**: Scale AI is the world's largest AI data-labeling company, and high-quality data is the core fuel for AI models
- **Talent acquisition**: Alexandr Wang is regarded as one of the most talented young leaders in the AI industry
- **Signaling effect**: Meta is no longer content to operate only at the "application layer" of AI — it is now positioning itself in "foundational AI research"

Counterargument: $14.3 billion is a massive transaction. If the timeline to superintelligence turns out to be longer than expected, this investment could become another expensive "metaverse-style" experiment.

## Ledger two: Reality Labs's $83.6 billion

### A brief history of metaverse failure

| Time | Event |
|------|------|
| October 2021 | Facebook renames itself Meta and announces an all-in bet on the metaverse |
| 2022-2023 | Reality Labs loses more than **$13-16 billion** per year |
| 2023 | Zuckerberg declares a "Year of Efficiency," beginning layoffs and cost controls |
| 2024 | Reality Labs loses **$17.72 billion**; Quest headset sales decline 16% |
| 2025 | Reality Labs loses **$19.19 billion**, with a single Q4 loss of **$6.02 billion** |
| January 2026 | Reality Labs staff cuts |
| March 2026 | The VR version of Horizon Worlds is shut down |

(Source: Meta annual filings, CNBC)

### Cumulative loss breakdown

| Year | Reality Labs loss |
|------|------------------|
| 2020 | **$6.62 billion** |
| 2021 | **$10.19 billion** |
| 2022 | **$13.71 billion** |
| 2023 | **$16.12 billion** |
| 2024 | **$17.72 billion** |
| 2025 | **$19.19 billion** |
| **Cumulative** | **~$83.55 billion** |

(Source: compiled from Meta annual filings)

**What does $83.6 billion mean in perspective?** It's close to Intel's entire current market cap. What did Meta get for that money? A Horizon Worlds whose VR version has already been shut down, a Quest headset with declining sales, and a handful of AR glasses prototypes.

### The metaverse isn't fully dead yet

Although Meta has sharply scaled back VR/metaverse investment, Reality Labs has not been shut down entirely:
- **AR glasses**: the smart glasses made in partnership with Ray-Ban are still selling and have received positive reviews
- **2026 loss outlook**: management says the loss will be at a "similar level" to 2025 (i.e., roughly $19 billion), but has hinted this may be the peak
- **Long-term vision**: Zuckerberg has never formally abandoned the long-term vision of "merging the physical and virtual worlds" — it has simply dropped from priority one to priority three

Counterargument: continuing to lose $19 billion a year on a business with no clear commercial prospects isn't "preserving optionality" — it's "throwing money into the ocean."

Response: viewed in isolation, Reality Labs's spending does lack justification. But Meta's Family of Apps segment generated advertising operating income of more than **$70 billion** in 2025 — the $19 billion Reality Labs loss amounts to roughly 27% of that. It's a heavy but not fatal drag. The question is whether, if Reality Labs's losses start shrinking meaningfully in 2027-2028, that profit will flow straight to the bottom line.

## Zuckerberg's capital-allocation philosophy

Looking at the AI investment and the metaverse losses together reveals Zuckerberg's capital-allocation philosophy:

**Trait one: willing to make big bets**
- 2012, $1 billion for Instagram — a bet that paid off
- 2014, $19 billion for WhatsApp — a bet that paid off
- 2021, all-in on the metaverse — a bet that failed
- 2024-2026, all-in on AI — directionally correct so far, scale still to be validated

**Trait two: he'll admit mistakes, but doesn't correct course fast enough**
- The metaverse took nearly five years, from 2021 to 2026, and $83.6 billion before it was substantively scaled back
- Layoffs and adjustments only began after nearly two years of outside criticism

**Trait three: dual-class shares mean no one can stop him**
Zuckerberg holds roughly **60% of the voting power**. That means that even if every outside shareholder objects, he can still push his own decisions through.

**At its core, the investment risk in Meta is a question of "do you trust Zuckerberg."** His track record over the past two decades has been largely excellent (the Instagram and WhatsApp acquisitions rank among the best M&A deals in tech history), but the $83.6 billion metaverse loss proves he can also make major mistakes.

## The key question: where is the ceiling on AI investment?

The market's biggest concern isn't "does AI produce a return" — the Q1 numbers already prove it does. It's "will AI investment keep expanding without limit."

Some points of reference:

| Company | 2026 capex guidance | % of revenue |
|------|-------------------|---------|
| Meta | **$125-145 billion** | 54%-63% |
| Google (Alphabet) | **~$75 billion** | ~19% |
| Microsoft | **~$80 billion** | ~30% |
| Amazon | **~$100 billion** | ~15% |

(Source: company guidance; some figures are analyst estimates)

Meta's capex as a share of revenue is far higher than its peers, and this is the direct reason the market applies a valuation discount to Meta.

But it's also worth noting: Meta's AI investment is tightly coupled to its advertising business — this is not a bet on some new future business, but investment aimed at strengthening the existing core business. If advertising revenue keeps growing at 20%+, capex as a share of revenue will naturally decline.

Management has also offered some reassurance: CFO Susan Li has stated clearly that "a leaner operating model will let us move faster, while also partly offsetting infrastructure investment." In May 2026, Meta announced layoffs of **8,000 people**, partly aimed at using AI to replace labor costs.
