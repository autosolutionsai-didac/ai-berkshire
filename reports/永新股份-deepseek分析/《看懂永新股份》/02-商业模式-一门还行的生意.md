# A "Decent Enough" Business

> *Understanding Yongxin Co., Ltd.* series · Part 02 · Business Model
> Reading time: about 12 minutes

---

## What kind of money does Yongxin make

The quality of a company ultimately comes down to three questions: whose money it earns, on what basis it earns it, and for how long it can keep earning it. The answer for Yongxin Co., Ltd. is more complicated than many might imagine — it's neither a bad business nor good enough to be called a great one.

First, a simple picture of the value chain:

```
Upstream (raw materials)     →    Yongxin Co., Ltd. (midstream manufacturing)    →    Downstream (brand owners)
Plastic pellets/ink/aluminum foil       Printed & laminated composites + film          Food/household chemicals/pharma
Commodity pricing                        Processing for a margin                       Strong bargaining power
```

What Yongxin does is buy plastic pellets, ink, and aluminum foil from upstream, process them into packaging materials through printing/lamination, vacuum aluminizing, and similar techniques, and sell them to downstream brand owners.

The essence of this business is **midstream processing and manufacturing** — earning a processing fee, not a brand premium and not a platform commission.

---

## How deep is the moat: testing five dimensions one by one

Many investors, on seeing "20 consecutive years of dividends" and "ROE of 17%," conclude this is a good company. But the key to assessing a business isn't "how much it earned in the past" — it's "whether it can keep earning in the future," which comes down to the moat.

### 1. Brand moat: almost none

Yongxin runs a B2B business. When you eat a bag of potato chips, you don't flip it over to check whether "the packaging is supplied by Yongxin Co., Ltd." End consumers have no idea Yongxin exists, so the brand cannot command a premium.

**Score: extremely weak**

### 2. Switching costs: Yongxin's most important moat

Changing packaging suppliers is not a simple matter:
- Requires re-certification (food-contact material safety standards, the customer's internal supplier audit)
- Requires re-tuning production parameters (print color matching, lamination strength, heat-sealing temperature)
- Trial-production cycle of **3-6 months**
- Major brand owners typically maintain 2-3 qualified suppliers at once and don't easily drop one

This means that once a supplier is on a major customer's approved list, the relationship carries a degree of stickiness. Yongxin has worked with some well-known brands for more than 10 years.

**But the counterpoint is:** switching costs are effective for large customers but low for small and mid-size ones. And switching costs protect against "being dropped," not against being unable to "raise prices" — the customer won't leave, but it also won't accept a price increase.

**Score: moderate**

### 3. Network effects: none

Packaging manufacturing is traditional manufacturing; there is no "the more users, the better the product" network effect.

**Score: none**

### 4. Scale effects: limited

Yongxin's 2025 revenue was **RMB 3.723 billion**, ranking near the top of the printed/laminated composite packaging industry. Scale brings some purchasing bargaining power and capacity-utilization advantage.

But the industry's CR5 is only **6%**, indicating that scale effects are not pronounced — if scale effects were strong, the industry would have consolidated long ago. Amcor (the global soft-packaging giant), whose China revenue is more than 20 times Yongxin's, has not built an overwhelming cost advantage either.

**Score: weak**

### 5. Technology / patent barriers: moderate

- **Printed and laminated composite packaging**: mature technology, limited differentiation; barriers mainly lie in process experience and customer certification
- **Vacuum-aluminized film**: heavy equipment investment, demanding process requirements, relatively fewer participants, higher barriers
- **Plastic soft-packaging film**: a 30,000-ton biaxially-oriented multifunctional film project is under construction, coming online in 2026

The technical barriers for aluminized film and functional film are higher than for ordinary printed packaging, but they make up a small share of Yongxin's revenue mix (aluminized packaging is only 1.7%).

**Score: moderate**

### Moat summary

| Dimension | Strength | Notes |
|------|------|------|
| Brand | Extremely weak | B2B; end consumers unaware |
| Switching costs | **Moderate** | Certification + trial-production cycle provide stickiness |
| Network effects | None | Traditional manufacturing |
| Scale effects | Weak | Fragmented industry; scale advantage not pronounced |
| Technology barriers | Moderate | Aluminized film and functional film carry some threshold |

**Overall judgment: a moat exists but is not deep.** Switching costs and technology barriers provide some protection, but neither is particularly wide. This means Yongxin can earn money steadily but finds it hard to raise prices meaningfully.

## Testing pricing power: raise prices 10%, would customers walk?

This is the most direct question for assessing business quality.

The answer depends on the product line:
- **Ordinary printed packaging** (68% of revenue): customers would very likely walk. Competition is intense and alternative suppliers are plentiful
- **High-end aluminized film / functional film** (~24% of revenue): some customers would hesitate, but they are not irreplaceable

**The 2025 data provides hard evidence:** revenue grew 5.61% while net profit fell 6.33% — meaning Yongxin cannot even fully pass through rising costs to customers, let alone proactively raise prices.

Looking further at raw material sensitivity:

> Synthetic resin accounts for about **58.53%** of procurement costs. According to Tianfeng Securities' estimate, for every 10% rise in crude oil (transmitted through to synthetic resin), gross margin falls by roughly **1.2-1.5 percentage points**.
> *Source: Tianfeng Securities research report*

This is a very high sensitivity. It means Yongxin is a classic **"price taker"** — it can only partially absorb upstream price increases, and can only partially resist downstream demands for cost cuts. Squeezed from both sides, its profit margin depends on the bargaining outcome between upstream and downstream.

**Counterpoint:** all packaging companies face the same raw material pressure. Yongxin's gross margin of 23% is higher than most peers (industry average of roughly 18-20%), suggesting it manages costs better than most competitors. The moat is not deep, but it sits a notch above the "average level."

## The dividend puzzle: generosity or nowhere else to invest?

Yongxin's dividend record stands out remarkably among A-share companies:

| Metric | Value |
|------|------|
| Cumulative dividends paid | **RMB 1.891 billion** |
| Cumulative net profit | RMB 2.431 billion |
| Cumulative payout ratio | **77.77%** |
| 2024 dividend (pre-tax) | RMB 380 million (payout ratio 81.19%) |
| 2025 dividend (pre-tax) | RMB 337 million (payout ratio 76.89%) |
| Average dividend yield, 2017-2024 | **5.2%** |

*Data sources: Yongxin Co., Ltd. historical annual reports, Wugui Quant*

Twenty consecutive years, a cumulative payout ratio of nearly 78%. This is extremely rare among A-share manufacturing companies.

But a high payout ratio has two readings:

**Reading one (positive): management is shareholder-friendly, distributing what it earns to shareholders in real terms.** The controlling shareholder, Huangshan Yongjia (a subsidiary of the Huangshan Supply and Marketing Cooperative), holds a 33.09% stake, and dividends are an important source of income for the controlling shareholder too — interests are aligned.

**Reading two (negative): the company lacks attractive reinvestment opportunities.** If it could find expansion projects earning 20%+ ROE, retaining profit for reinvestment would create more value than paying it out. A 78% payout ratio means only 22% of profit stays within the company — perhaps not because management doesn't want to retain it, but because there's nowhere attractive to invest it.

**Which is closer to the truth?** Both are probably true to some extent. Yongxin is indeed expanding (the film project), but not quickly; the industry's growth rate is only 5-7%, leaving limited room for large-scale reinvestment. A high payout ratio is both a strength (shareholder-friendly) and a corroborating signal (limited growth room).

## DuPont analysis: what's behind the 17% ROE

ROE is the core metric for measuring profitability. Yongxin has posted an ROE above 15% for five consecutive years, which looks impressive. But break it down (DuPont analysis), and the structure is shifting:

| Year | ROE | Net margin | Asset turnover | Equity multiplier |
|------|-----|-------|-----------|---------|
| 2021 | 15.90% | 10.62% | ~1.08 | 1.39 |
| 2022 | 17.01% | 11.16% | ~0.99 | 1.54 |
| 2023 | 17.72% | 12.26% | ~0.93 | 1.56 |
| 2024 | **19.18%** | **13.37%** | ~0.87 | 1.64 |
| 2025 | **17.34%** | **11.87%** | ~0.85 | **1.71** |

*Data source: Yongxin Co., Ltd. historical annual reports*

Two trends are worth flagging:

1. **Asset turnover has been steadily declining** (from 1.08 down to 0.85) — operating efficiency is deteriorating
2. **The equity multiplier has been steadily rising** (from 1.39 up to 1.71) — leverage is increasing

In other words, ROE has been sustained partly by rising leverage rather than strengthening profitability. In 2025, net margin fell from 13.37% to 11.87% — the core variable propping up ROE is weakening. If net margin keeps falling while leverage cannot rise further, ROE will decline further.

**Counterpoint:** the debt-to-asset ratio has risen from 27.85% to 41.68%, but the absolute level remains healthy. For a manufacturing company, a debt ratio of around 40% is entirely normal and nowhere near the danger zone.

## Cash flow: the "gold content" of profit

Profit can be adjusted; cash flow is not so easy to fake. Looking at cash flow lets you verify how genuine the profit is.

| Year | Operating cash flow (RMB) | Net profit (RMB) | Operating cash flow / net profit |
|------|----------------|-------------|-----------------|
| 2021 | ~355 million | 316 million | **112%** |
| 2022 | ~545 million | 363 million | **150%** |
| 2023 | ~514 million | 408 million | **126%** |
| 2024 | ~459 million | 468 million | **98%** |
| 2025 | **343 million** | 438 million | **78%** |

*Data source: Yongxin Co., Ltd. historical annual reports*

From 2021 to 2023, cash flow quality was excellent (>100%, indicating high-quality profit). But it deteriorated in 2024-2025, reaching only 78% in 2025 — meaning that out of RMB 438 million in net profit, only RMB 343 million became actual cash.

Free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capex) is worse still: an estimated only about **RMB 165 million** in 2025, with FCF/net profit of just **38%**. This is related to capacity projects under construction (the new film line), but the direction of the trend is unfavorable.

**Counterpoint:** the cash flow decline is tied to expansion spending; once the new film capacity comes online in 2026, capex should fall back and cash flow should improve.

## How many stars does this business deserve?

Pulling all of the above together, here is an overall rating for Yongxin's business model:

| Dimension | Score | Core judgment |
|------|------|----------|
| Moat | ★★☆☆☆ | Switching costs + technology barriers provide limited protection |
| Pricing power | ★★☆☆☆ | 2025's "revenue up, profit down" is hard evidence |
| Customer value | ★★★☆☆ | Stable supply has value, but replaceability is weak |
| Dividend culture | ★★★★★ | Top-tier among A-share companies |
| Earnings quality | ★★★☆☆ | ROE is strong but on a deteriorating trend |
| Growth room | ★★☆☆☆ | Industry is mature; growth relies on volume, not price |

**One-sentence summary: this is a "decent enough" business — not a good business, but not a bad one either.** It can keep earning money steadily, pays generous dividends, and is financially sound, but it lacks pricing power and a deep moat, with profit squeezed from both upstream and downstream.

To use an analogy: Yongxin Co., Ltd. is like a **well-run restaurant in a poor location** — the chef is reliable, the books are clean, and shareholders get their dividend on time every month, but it's located on a fiercely competitive food street, where it can't raise menu prices and has no say over ingredient costs.
