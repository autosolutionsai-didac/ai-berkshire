# Three Questions You Have to Think Through

> *Understanding Lingjing Wanwei* series - Episode 04 - Finale
> Reading time: approximately 10 minutes

## What this piece is for

The conclusions from the first three pieces aren't easy to sit with: the technology dividend isn't unique to Lingjing Wanwei, its competitive position is second-tier, its pricing power is nearly zero, and 80% of its revenue goes toward paid traffic acquisition. This piece doesn't introduce new information -- instead, it **pulls all of it together to answer three judgment questions**:

1. Will Lingjing Wanwei still be around in three years?
2. Under what conditions is it worth a bet, and under what conditions is it not?
3. Is this business, at bottom, a "good business" or "a bad business in a good sector"?

---

## Question one: will Lingjing Wanwei still be around in three years?

### Four scenarios

| Scenario | Probability (estimate) | Description |
|------|-----------|------|
| A: Successful transition to a platform | **15%** | Upgrades from "content factory" to "AI comic-drama creation platform," annual revenue breaks 1 billion yuan, valuation of 5-10 billion yuan |
| B: A leading content supplier | **30%** | Stays in the "content factory" model, annual revenue of 300-800 million yuan, profitable but with a low ceiling |
| C: Survives but stays mediocre | **25%** | Annual revenue of 100-300 million yuan, struggling in the middle of the pack, dependent on continued fundraising |
| D: Knocked out | **30%** | Runs out of cash, gets crushed by big tech, or is acquired at a low price |

**Estimated 3-year survival rate: 60-70%** (scenarios A+B+C)
**Estimated 3-year success rate: 15-20%** (defined as "achieving profitability and building sustainable competitive advantage")

(Note: the probability allocation above is derived from industry analogy and first-principles reasoning; it carries a large subjective component and should be treated only as a framework-level reference)

### Key conditions for each scenario

**What scenario A requires**:
- Lingjing Wanwei successfully opens its self-developed tool chain into a cloud platform, attracting outside creators to join
- Building a "creator ecosystem" that forms network effects around content
- Opening up overseas markets, validating global viability
- **Real-world challenge**: a platform needs a traffic gateway, and traffic is controlled by ByteDance and Tencent. A platform with no traffic of its own is very hard to get off the ground

**What scenario B requires**:
- Continued production of hit titles (at least 3-5 titles per year with over 100 million views)
- Traffic-acquisition efficiency that stays consistently above the industry average
- Building a content brand in some vertical genre (such as new-Chinese-style fantasy or female-oriented content)
- Making IP-derived monetization work (sequels, merchandise, game licensing, etc.)
- **Real-world challenge**: a 0.12% hit rate means "continuously producing hits" is essentially a game of probability

**What would trigger scenario D**:
- The next funding round (post-angel or Series A) fails to close within 6-12 months
- Kling/Jimeng launch a "one-click comic-drama generation" feature, zeroing out the value of Lingjing's tool chain
- The core technical team gets poached by big tech at high salaries
- Several consecutive quarters pass with no hit titles, and the business model cannot be validated

### The biggest variable

**The pace of evolution of general-purpose AI video tools.** This single variable may carry more weight than every other factor combined.

If Kling/Jimeng achieve, within the next 12 months, the ability to go from "input script -> automatically output a commercial-grade comic drama," Lingjing Wanwei's entire technology stack becomes redundant. This probability is estimated at **30-40%**.

**Counterpoint**: Even if general-purpose tools achieve "one-click generation," industrialized mass production still requires the full-chain capability of "topic selection -> traffic acquisition -> operations." The tool only lowers the barrier to the "production" stage, but "what content to make" and "how to sell it" still require a professional team. Lingjing Wanwei's competitiveness lies not only in its tools but also in its accumulated experience in content selection and distribution operations.

## Question two: under what conditions is it worth a bet?

### First, the reasons it's not worth a bet

Viewed through a value-investing lens, Lingjing Wanwei fails to satisfy almost every criterion:

| Checklist item | Conclusion | Notes |
|--------|------|------|
| Can you understand this business? | Partially | The model is clear, but "hit-title probability" is unpredictable |
| Does it have a durable competitive advantage? | **Does not satisfy** | The moat is nearly zero, and the technical barrier is eroding fast |
| Is management trustworthy? | **Cannot be determined** | Founders' backgrounds are opaque, information is extremely limited |
| Will it be better in ten years? | **Highly uncertain** | 3-year survival rate is only 60-70% |
| Is there a margin of safety? | **Does not satisfy** | Early-stage startup funding rounds are typically priced at a premium |

**Moat analysis summary**:

| Type of moat | Does it exist? | Notes |
|-----------|--------|------|
| Brand moat | **Does not exist** | Users follow the content, not the producer's brand |
| Switching costs | **Extremely low** | Users and platforms can switch suppliers at any time |
| Network effects | **Do not exist** | The current model is a content factory, with no platform effect |
| Economies of scale | **Limited** | Higher output doesn't meaningfully lower marginal cost; every title still requires paid traffic |
| Technical barrier | **Effective short-term, doubtful long-term** | An application-layer workflow advantage, dependent on third-party base models |

**Summary: viewed through the value-investing framework, Lingjing Wanwei is not a "good business."** It sits in a fast-growing but brutally competitive sector, with no pricing power, no moat, and opaque management.

### Now, the angle from which it might be worth a bet

Shift perspective -- if you're not a value investor but a venture investor (or someone with a strategic interest in the AI content space), Lingjing Wanwei has several points worth noting:

1. **Execution has genuinely been strong.** Three funding rounds in eight months, growing from 5 people to nearly 100, and *Supermarket at the End of the World* becoming a breakout hit at the start of 2026 -- in an industry with a 90% failure rate, simply staying alive and growing says something in itself.

2. **540% revenue growth.** The base is small, but the growth rate suggests a genuine product-market fit.

3. **An asset-light model.** A team of nearly 100, extremely low production costs, and no heavy assets -- this means that even amid sharp industry swings, Lingjing Wanwei won't die quickly from fixed costs being too high.

4. **Going overseas may be a chance to differentiate.** If the domestic market is being squeezed so hard by big tech that startups can barely breathe, overseas markets could offer a startup some breathing room.

5. **If the platformization transition succeeds**, the business model would upgrade from "content lottery" to "platform rent-collector" -- and the latter is a far higher-quality business than the former.

**But every piece of good news is followed by a giant "if."** That's the nature of a startup -- full of "ifs."

## Question three: is this a good business in a good sector, or a bad business in a good sector?

This is the most fundamental question.

### The sector is good

The growth of the AI comic-drama sector is real:

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| 2025 market size | **16.8 billion yuan** |
| 2026 market size (forecast) | **22-31.4 billion yuan** (up more than 40% year-over-year) |
| 2030 market size (forecast) | **85.8 billion yuan** |
| 2026 user count (forecast) | **280 million** |
| Production cost reduction | **80-95%** |

(Sources: iiMedia Research, 36Kr, Securities Times)

AI really is changing how animated content is produced. This isn't a bubble -- the demand is real, the technological progress is real, and the market growth is real.

### But a good sector doesn't mean its participants make money

This is a distinction many people conflate. A few examples:

- **The airline industry**: transportation demand has grown for a century, but the industry's cumulative profit across its entire history is roughly zero
- **The solar industry**: installed capacity has grown exponentially, but the vast majority of panel manufacturers have lost money or gone under
- **Bike-sharing**: it solved a real need, but after burning through tens of billions of yuan, only two or three players barely survive

AI comic dramas may be heading down the same road: **the technology dividend is shared across the whole industry, and the benefit of a 95% reduction in production cost will ultimately pass through to platforms and users, rather than staying with the producers.**

Once everyone can make comic dramas with AI --

- Oversupply (an average of **470 titles launched per day** in January 2026) -> attention becomes scarce
- Attention becomes scarce -> traffic-acquisition costs rise
- Traffic-acquisition costs rise -> profit gets eaten away
- Profit gets eaten away -> 90% of companies lose money

**This is a sector that's good for AI technology but bad for AI content companies.** The ultimate winners are likely to be: the platforms that own the distribution channels (ByteDance, Kuaishou), the technology companies that own the base models (Kling, Jimeng), and the users who enjoy cheap content. **Content producers -- including Lingjing Wanwei -- may be the thinnest-margin link in this value chain.**

**Counterpoint**: This judgment overlooks the value of "creativity." In an oversupplied market, creative content can still stand out. The success of *Supermarket at the End of the World* shows that a good premise and good execution still command a premium. If Lingjing Wanwei can keep producing creative hits, it can still claim a share of the profit pool. Analogy: Hollywood is also a fiercely competitive industry, but Marvel built sustained profit through its IP matrix.

**The counter-counterpoint**: Marvel spent 20 years and hundreds of millions of dollars building its IP matrix, and ultimately monetized it through Disney's global distribution network. Lingjing Wanwei has 50 million yuan in funding and a 100-person team. The gap in scale means comparing Lingjing Wanwei to Marvel is not appropriate.

## Summary of the overall judgment

### One summary table

| Dimension | Assessment | Core evidence |
|------|------|---------|
| Sector quality | **Good** | Market size 30 billion+ yuan, growth rate 40%+, genuine demand |
| Business quality | **Poor** | No pricing power, no moat, essentially a "content lottery" |
| Team execution | **Above average** | Three funding rounds in a year, validated hits, rapid expansion |
| Management transparency | **Low** | Founders' backgrounds are opaque, cannot be deeply assessed |
| Technical barrier | **Weak** | Short-term differentiation, may be replaced by general-purpose tools long-term |
| Competitive position | **Second tier** | Scale gap versus the leaders, squeezed from both sides by big tech |
| 3-year survival rate | **60-70%** (estimate) | The asset-light model is a safety cushion, but ammunition is limited |
| 3-year success rate | **15-20%** (estimate) | Requires sustained hits + platformization + overseas expansion |

### How different types of people should view this

| Who you are | Recommendation |
|--------|------|
| Value investor | **Avoid** -- it fails to meet any of the "good business, good management, good price" three-part standard |
| Venture investor | **Proceed cautiously** -- if you can accept a 70-80% probability of loss and have a strategic view on the AI content sector, it could work as a small-position risk exposure |
| AI content industry practitioner | **Worth watching** -- Lingjing Wanwei's tool chain and full-chain playbook are one of the industry's benchmarks, valuable as a partner or as a case study |
| General reader | **Understanding the industry is enough** -- the developmental logic of the AI comic-drama industry (technology lowers cost -> oversupply -> profit shifts to the platforms) applies to many AI application sectors |

### One last word

Lingjing Wanwei is a company that has executed well while riding a hot trend -- but there are too many companies riding hot trends, and the profit from the trend itself may ultimately not belong to those flying above it.

The story of the AI comic-drama sector is still evolving rapidly. Looking back on today's judgment 12 months from now may render it completely outdated -- Lingjing Wanwei may have already closed a Series A and pushed overseas monthly revenue past 10 million yuan; or general-purpose AI tools may have already turned "self-developed tool chains" into a joke.

**The only certainty is uncertainty itself.** In an environment like this, what matters most isn't "whether the call was right," but "how much position size you're willing to use to bear that uncertainty."
