## What AI comics actually are

A technology narrative is standard equipment for a startup raising money. Lingjing Wanwei is no exception -- "DIT architecture," "neural rendering," "data flywheel": these terms show up densely in its fundraising news. But before deciding whether the technology actually constitutes a moat, we first need to be clear about what the product actually is.

**AI comics** are an emerging content format that sits between traditional animation and live-action short dramas:

- **Not** traditional animation (no frame-by-frame drawing by animators needed; production cycles shrink from months to roughly one month)
- **Not** a simple AI-generated image slideshow (it includes dynamic rendering, character motion, voiceover, and music)
- **Is** a format that uses AI to quickly convert web novels/fiction into vertical-screen animated comic dramas with visuals, voiceover, and plot
- **Episode length**: about 3-5 minutes, matched to short-video platform consumption habits
- **Quality positioning**: somewhere between "motion comics" and "Japanese TV anime"

Lingjing Wanwei claims its product quality is "close to mainstream Japanese anime standards." **This is PR language and should be discounted** -- based on published work, AI comics still lag noticeably behind traditional Japanese anime in character consistency, complex motion, and scene continuity. But for vertical-screen short-video consumption, "good enough" may matter more than "polished."

## Lingjing Wanwei's technology approach

### A full-chain AI production line

Lingjing Wanwei has built a complete AI production line running from text to video:

```
Web fiction data mining -> AI novel writing -> script storyboarding -> static image generation -> dynamic rendering -> voiceover and music -> finished-product distribution
```

AI tools are embedded at every stage. The company's self-developed AIGC production tool is based on a **DIT architecture**, described as being driven by dual cores of "neural rendering + cognitive computing."

### Production efficiency

| Metric | Lingjing Wanwei | Traditional animation | Gap |
|------|---------|---------|------|
| Cost per minute produced | **Hundreds of yuan** | 1,500-2,500 yuan | Down 70-90% |
| Production cycle per comic drama | **About 1 month** | 6-12 months | Shortened 6-12x |
| Team setup | 1 director + 5 production staff | Teams of dozens | Per-person output up 5-10x |
| Cost per production | **100,000-150,000 yuan** | Millions to tens of millions | Down 95%+ |

(Sources: 36Kr fundraising reports, Gaohu Capital press releases, Liuchuan Media)

## Three layers of scrutiny on the technology moat

### Layer one: Are these efficiency gains real?

**Very likely real, but should be discounted.**

The cost-reduction figures Lingjing Wanwei has given on different occasions are inconsistent -- sometimes "down to 1/20 of traditional cost," sometimes "reduced by 70%," sometimes "down to 3%." These figures contradict each other, suggesting the PR data isn't using a consistent methodology.

But looking at the industry as a whole, AI genuinely has pushed comic-drama production costs down from "millions per episode" to the "low hundred-thousands per episode" range. This isn't a claim unique to Lingjing Wanwei -- competitors like Jiangyou Culture, Jellyfish Intelligence, and Lingju Animation have all reported similar efficiency figures. **The efficiency gain is real, but it is not unique to Lingjing.**

### Layer two: Is the technical barrier unique to Lingjing Wanwei?

**No. This is the most important question.**

Lingjing Wanwei's tech stack can be split into two layers:

| Layer | Content | Is it proprietary? |
|------|------|---------|
| Base layer: large AI models | Video generation (Kling, Jimeng, etc.), image generation (Stable Diffusion, etc.), speech synthesis | **No** -- Lingjing uses open-source or third-party models |
| Application layer: workflow | Self-developed DIT-architecture tooling, automated script storyboarding, character-consistency management | **Short-term differentiation** -- but the barrier to entry is not high |

What Lingjing Wanwei has built is **application-layer workflow optimization**, not base-layer model capability. This distinction is critical:

- The base models are controlled by companies like ByteDance (Jimeng), Kuaishou (Kling), and SenseTime (Seko), each of which leaps a generation every few months
- As the base models get strong enough, the value of application-layer workflow optimization shrinks rapidly
- Analogy: once smartphone cameras got good enough, the point-and-shoot camera market disappeared

**Counterpoint**: The value of a workflow isn't only technical -- it also includes "experience accumulated from trial and error": which storyboard techniques boost completion rates, which genres perform best at which time slots, and how subtle differences in character design affect user payment. This experiential data isn't something model capability alone can replace. Lingjing Wanwei claims to have accumulated large amounts of production data, forming a "data flywheel."

**Counter-counterpoint**: But the barrier to accumulating this kind of experience is extremely low -- any company producing hundreds of comic dramas a month can accumulate similar experience. Jiangyou Culture (whose monthly output far exceeds Lingjing's) spins its data flywheel even faster.

### Layer three: How much value will Lingjing Wanwei's technology retain in three years?

This is the sharpest question. Consider two scenarios:

**Scenario A: General-purpose AI tools become "good enough"**

If Kling 3.0, the next generation of Jimeng, or some open-source model achieves the capability of "input a script -> automatically output a comic drama" at commercially usable quality, the value of Lingjing Wanwei's entire self-developed tool chain will **approach zero**.

This isn't science fiction. AI video tools in late 2024 could only generate clips a few seconds long; by late 2025 they could already generate coherent videos several minutes long; the pace of progress in 2026 is still accelerating. **"One-click comic-drama generation" is not a question of "whether," but of "when."**

(Sources: Kling and Jimeng product update logs, 36Kr industry reports)

**Scenario B: Progress in general-purpose tools slows down**

If large models hit a wall in video generation (for example, long-video consistency or complex narrative understanding remain unsolved for a long time), then Lingjing Wanwei's engineering capability -- assembling imperfect base models into a usable production line -- would still retain value.

**Current assessment**: Scenario A is more likely. The pace of progress in AI video generation has far exceeded expectations, and this is the **most fundamental technology risk** Lingjing Wanwei faces.

## The partnership with Zhejiang University: a plus, but not decisive

Lingjing Wanwei has jointly developed an AI novel-writing model with Zhejiang University, producing 1,000+ short and medium-length novels per month, at an efficiency **20 times** that of a traditional writer.

(Source: 36Kr fundraising report)

This is a point in Lingjing's favor -- the university partnership adds credibility to the technology narrative. But note:

- The AI writing model sits at the "raw material supply" stage, not the core competitive advantage
- Comparable AI writing tools are already widely available on the market (Tongyi Qianwen, Ernie Bot, Kimi, and others can all generate fiction)
- The actual depth of the Zhejiang University collaboration (a joint lab vs. a one-off project) is unclear

## Is the "data flywheel" a real proposition or just talk?

Like almost every AI company, Lingjing Wanwei claims to have a "data flywheel" -- the more comic dramas it produces, the more data it accumulates, the more accurate its models become, and the higher its product quality.

**Logically sound, but three problems arise in practice**:

1. **The data flywheel premise is "data scarcity"** -- if every company in the industry is mass-producing comic dramas, data is not scarce, and the flywheel effect weakens. In January 2026, the industry as a whole launched an average of **470 new comic dramas per day** -- data supply is not scarce.

2. **A comic drama's core competitiveness lies in content, not technology** -- users don't pay because "the visuals are smoother"; they pay because "the story is better." A data flywheel can optimize technology but cannot replace creativity.

3. **The platforms hold the real data** -- user behavior data (completion rate, payment conversion rate, retention rate) sits with distribution platforms like Douyin and Hongguo. What data Lingjing Wanwei can obtain is limited.

**Counterpoint**: Lingjing Wanwei's data flywheel isn't only technical data -- it also includes data on "which genres go viral, which pacing keeps users watching, which art styles convert best." This kind of topic-selection and operations data does have accumulation value and is not entirely held by the platforms.

## The plagiarism controversy: a structural hazard of the content-factory model

In January 2026, three days after *Supermarket at the End of the World* launched, it became embroiled in a plagiarism dispute.

Mantan Culture sent a "copyright risk notice," claiming that Lingjing Wanwei's *I Opened a Supermarket at the End of the World* was "highly consistent" with its own work *I Became a Boss at the End of the World* in worldview, characters, and core plot.

Lingjing Wanwei firmly denied the claim, arguing that the so-called "similarity" was nothing more than the generic template of the apocalypse genre -- apocalyptic wasteland, awakened powers, family conflict, small-group exploration -- all standard elements of the web-fiction industry.

(Sources: Huxiu, 36Kr, The Paper (Jiemian News), The Paper, and other media reports)

**The rights and wrongs of this particular dispute are hard to judge, but it exposes a structural problem**:

AI mass-produces content -> genres become highly homogeneous (apocalypse, fantasy, sweet romance all crowding together) -> copyright boundaries blur -> the probability of plagiarism disputes rises.

This isn't a problem unique to Lingjing Wanwei -- it's an inherent hazard of the entire AI content-factory model. When hundreds or thousands of comic dramas are produced per month, each one risks stepping into some existing work's "zone of similarity." This risk amplifies as output grows.

## Core judgment of this piece

Having taken apart Lingjing Wanwei's technology, three conclusions emerge:

### 1. The efficiency gain is real

AI has genuinely pushed comic-drama production from a "cottage industry" toward "industrialization." Costs down 80-95%, efficiency up 10-20x -- these figures, while inflated by PR, are directionally and roughly correct in magnitude.

### 2. But the efficiency gain is not unique to Lingjing Wanwei

This is a technology dividend shared across the whole industry, stemming from progress in base-layer AI models. Lingjing Wanwei's application-layer workflow optimization offers short-term differentiation, but the moat is shallow -- any team with engineering capability can build a similar production line.

### 3. The biggest uncertainty: will general-purpose AI tools "one-click replace" the entire production line?

The answer to this question determines whether Lingjing Wanwei's tech stack will still be worth anything in three years. If Kling, Jimeng, or some open-source tool achieves "one-click comic-drama generation," the value of Lingjing Wanwei's tool chain goes to zero. The probability is not low, and it rises with every passing quarter.

| Dimension | Assessment |
|------|------|
| Technology authenticity | Real, efficiency gains are documented |
| Technology uniqueness | **Weak** -- base models are not proprietary, application-layer barriers are low |
| Technology durability | **Highly uncertain** -- depends on the pace of progress of general-purpose AI tools |
