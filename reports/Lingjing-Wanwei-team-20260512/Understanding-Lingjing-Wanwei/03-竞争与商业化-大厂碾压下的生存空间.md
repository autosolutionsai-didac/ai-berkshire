## First, a competitive comparison table

Lingjing Wanwei's monthly revenue of 20 million yuan sounds substantial -- until you compare it with the leading companies in the same track. Jiangyou Culture's monthly revenue is 50 million yuan, 2.5 times Lingjing Wanwei's; annualized, that scale roughly equals Lingjing Wanwei's entire full-year revenue target compressed into a single month for Jiangyou Culture. This comparison table sets the baseline for any discussion of Lingjing Wanwei's competitive position.

| Company | Type | Scale metrics | Lingjing Wanwei's gap |
|------|------|---------|--------------|
| Jiangyou Culture | Leading startup | Monthly revenue **50 million yuan**, team of **1,000+**, total views **2.2 billion** | Revenue gap **10x**, headcount gap **10x** |
| Mantan Animation | Leading startup | Total views **2.8 billion**, daily ad spend over **2.19 million yuan** | Huge gap in view count |
| Lingju Animation | Leading startup | Team of **800**, monthly output of **150 titles** | Output gap of **several multiples** |
| Jellyfish Intelligence | Leading startup | **5 titles** with over 100 million views each, 1 title over **300 million** | Clear gap in hit-title count |
| **Lingjing Wanwei** | **Second tier** | Nearly 100 staff, monthly revenue about **20 million yuan** (estimate), revenue growth rate **540%** | -- |

(Sources: TMTPost, Huxiu, 36Kr industry reports, Liuchuan Media)

**Lingjing Wanwei is not a leading player in this industry.** In a content industry with a pronounced "winner effect," this gap is not linear -- scale means more chances to experiment, higher odds of producing a hit, and stronger bargaining power with platforms.

**Counterpoint**: Lingjing Wanwei's advantage lies in being "small and fast" -- a team of nearly 100 could mean per-person efficiency far higher than a thousand-person team, and a startup's decision-making speed and flexibility to experiment are things a large organization cannot match. 540% revenue growth suggests it is catching up quickly.

## Big tech enters the arena: the real existential threat

Competition among startups isn't even the biggest problem. **The real threat comes from the internet giants.**

### The triple squeeze from big tech

| Dimension | What big tech has | What Lingjing Wanwei has |
|------|---------|-----------|
| Technology (model capability) | Proprietary large models such as Kling (Kuaishou), Jimeng (ByteDance), Seko (SenseTime) | Application-layer tools built on third-party/open-source models |
| Capital | Hundreds of millions invested annually, can sustain losses long-term | Total funding raised of roughly 50-100 million yuan |
| Distribution (traffic gateways) | Platforms like Douyin, Kuaishou, and Hongguo directly control traffic | **Zero traffic gateway of its own**, entirely dependent on platform distribution |

ByteDance's position is especially worth noting -- it simultaneously owns:
- An AI video generation tool (Jimeng)
- An AI comic-drama distribution platform (Hongguo Comics, with **24.04 million** monthly active users, ranked third in the industry)
- China's largest short-video distribution channel (Douyin)

**When a single company controls both the "production tool" and the "distribution channel," the "content producer" in the middle gets squeezed from both sides.** Lingjing Wanwei uses big tech's models to make content, then distributes that content on big tech's platforms -- both the revenue side and the cost side are controlled by someone else.

(Source: 36Kr report "Giants Enter the Ring, AI Comic Dramas Explode")

### Will big tech simply crush the startups?

**The bull case**: Big tech's advantages are too comprehensive -- technology, capital, and traffic all at once. With Tencent launching its "Firehound Comics" app (February 2026), Baidu launching its "Youmanju" app (December 2025), and Kuaishou rolling out "Dream Maker Expert 2.0," independent producers will have less and less room.

**The bear case**: The logic of running a platform is different from the logic of producing content. A platform needs a large volume of content supply and cannot produce all of it in-house. Just as YouTube hasn't replaced all of its creators, Douyin won't replace all short-drama producers either. **Big tech builds the infrastructure, startups make the content -- the two can coexist.** Analogy: China Literature (Yuewen Group) controls the web-fiction platform, but a large volume of web fiction is still written by independent authors.

**The counter-counterpoint**: But creators on YouTube don't need YouTube's own tools to create. If ByteDance's Jimeng tool becomes good enough, anyone could make an AI comic drama -- and then Lingjing Wanwei's dual identity of "tool + content" would come apart: the tool layer gets replaced by big tech, and the content layer faces competition from a flood of individual creators.

## Dissecting the business model: 80% of revenue spent on user acquisition

Lingjing Wanwei's revenue comes from two sources:

| Model | Description | Share (estimate) |
|------|------|-----------|
| Pay-to-unlock | Users pay to watch subsequent episodes | Primary |
| Ad monetization | Users watch ads to unlock content | Secondary |

Regardless of the model, distribution is heavily dependent on **paid traffic acquisition (buying traffic)** -- spending money on platforms like Douyin and Hongguo to push the work in front of users.

There's an open secret in the industry here: **more than 80% of AI comic-drama industry revenue goes toward paid traffic acquisition.**

(Source: TMTPost, "Behind the 10-Billion-Yuan AI Comic Drama Boom, 90% Are Losing Money")

What does this mean in practice?

| Scenario | Revenue | Traffic acquisition cost (80%) | Production cost | Platform take (~15%) | Profit |
|------|------|--------------|---------|--------------|------|
| Hit title (at the level of *Supermarket at the End of the World*) | 12 million yuan | ~9.6 million yuan | 150,000 yuan | ~1.8 million yuan | **~450,000 yuan** |
| Mid-tier title | 500,000 yuan | ~400,000 yuan | 150,000 yuan | ~75,000 yuan | **-125,000 yuan** |
| Ordinary title (the vast majority) | 50,000 yuan | ~40,000 yuan | 150,000 yuan | ~7,500 yuan | **-147,500 yuan** |

(Note: the figures above are a model derived from industry-average data, not Lingjing Wanwei's actual figures)

**The core problem surfaces here**: the profit in this business doesn't lie in "production," it lies in "distribution." AI has pushed production cost down (150,000 yuan per title), but the cost of traffic acquisition hasn't come down -- if anything, it keeps rising as the number of industry players explodes.

**Production costs fell 95%, but total costs may have fallen only 20-30%.** Because the real bulk of the cost -- traffic acquisition -- hasn't been disrupted by AI at all.

**Counterpoint**: Lingjing Wanwei has its own traffic-acquisition team and data-analysis capability, and its traffic efficiency may be higher than the industry average. In addition, sequels to hit titles (such as *Supermarket at the End of the World* Season 2 and Season 3, both already announced) don't require buying new traffic from scratch and can ride the long tail of the IP. This is why Lingjing Wanwei keeps emphasizing its "IP operations" capability.

## Pricing power: a fatal omission

This is the single biggest structural flaw in Lingjing Wanwei as an investment target.

| Who determines Lingjing Wanwei's revenue? | Answer |
|----------------------|------|
| Per-user payment price | **Set by the platform** (Hongguo, Douyin set the pricing tiers) |
| Traffic acquisition cost | **Set by the platform's algorithm** (bidding mechanism, Lingjing has no control) |
| Underlying AI model cost | **Set by the model vendor** (Kling, Jimeng set their own pricing) |
| Content review standards | **Set by the regulator** (new National Radio and Television Administration filing rules) |
| **What can Lingjing Wanwei actually decide?** | **Only "what content to make" and "how to make it"** |

**With the revenue side controlled by the platforms, and the cost side controlled by the model vendors and the platforms, Lingjing Wanwei has almost no pricing power anywhere along the value chain.** This is one of the most central metrics for judging whether a business is a good one.

Analogy: early Android phone assemblers also lacked pricing power -- chip prices were set by Qualcomm, and sales channels were controlled by carriers and e-commerce platforms; ultimately they could only fight price wars, with margins converging toward zero.

**Counterpoint**: Pricing power can be gradually earned through IP building. If Lingjing Wanwei can create franchise IP that "users keep coming back for" (rather than one-off hits), it can upgrade from a "content factory" to an "IP company" -- and the latter has far greater pricing power and bargaining leverage. Didn't Disney itself start out as a content producer and eventually gain pricing power through building an IP empire?

**The counter-counterpoint**: But Disney took 100 years to do it. And Disney controls its own distribution channels (theaters, streaming, theme parks). Lingjing Wanwei has neither the time nor the channels.

## The National Radio and Television Administration's new April rules: good news or bad news?

On April 1, 2026, the National Radio and Television Administration's new AI comic-drama filing rules took effect:

| Rule | Content |
|------|------|
| Clearing the backlog | All unfiled existing works must **complete supplementary review by March 31**, or be forcibly taken offline |
| New-title review | New titles must be **filed before going live** |
| Tiered review | Investment >=3 million yuan or sensitive subject matter -> reviewed by the national Radio and Television Administration; 1-3 million yuan -> provincial-level review; <1 million yuan -> platform self-review followed by provincial filing |
| Content redlines | Vulgar or suggestive content, AI reworking of classics/heroes, and unauthorized use of real people's likeness are all strictly prohibited |
| AI labeling | AI-generated content must be labeled at the opening or in a prominent position |

(Sources: 21st Century Business Herald, The Paper, Tencent News)

**Impact on Lingjing Wanwei -- a double-edged sword**:

**Upside**:
- Raises the industry's barrier to entry, weeding out a batch of non-compliant small workshops, benefiting properly run companies
- Lingjing Wanwei already has dual headquarters in Wuhan and Hangzhou and a legitimate fundraising background, giving it stronger compliance capability than fly-by-night operators
- The industry contracting from a glut of "470 titles a day" in output may improve the competitive environment

**Downside**:
- Review adds time and compliance cost, slowing down the core "rapid mass production -> rapid rollout" rhythm
- One of Lingjing Wanwei's advantages is "speed" -- the filing system could weaken that advantage
- Tighter copyright scrutiny raises compliance risk for AI mass-produced content (see the plagiarism controversy around *Supermarket at the End of the World*)

## Going overseas: a second growth curve, or just a pitch deck story?

Lingjing Wanwei has repeatedly emphasized its "going global" strategy:

- Its overseas app has already been developed, with plans to launch overseas advertising in May 2026
- Its first batch of short fiction has already achieved paid monetization on platforms like Webnovel and Dreame
- Localization is based on an AI translation and cultural-adaptation system

(Sources: 36Kr fundraising report, Liuchuan Media)

The overseas AI short-drama market is projected to reach **$650 million** (about 4.5 billion yuan) in scale in 2026, and overseas users' willingness to pay is higher than domestic users' (over half say they are willing to pay).

**The bull case**: Going overseas is a genuine increment. AI comic dramas are naturally suited to going global -- low language barriers (AI translation + dubbing), low cost of cultural adaptation (animation isn't limited by live-action actors), and high overseas willingness to pay. If Lingjing Wanwei can replicate the success of *Supermarket at the End of the World* overseas, it would open up substantial room for growth.

**The bear case**: Going overseas is easy to say and extremely hard to do. It requires local-operations capability, overseas legal compliance, payment-channel integration, and adaptation to differing cultural aesthetics -- capabilities Lingjing Wanwei has never demonstrated. And on the overseas track, leading companies like Jiangyou Culture are also positioning themselves, and the short-drama-overseas space already has first movers like ReelShort occupying the market. Lingjing Wanwei's overseas expansion looks more like a "future story" told during fundraising than a proven business model.

## Lingjing Wanwei's true position in the value chain

Putting the competition and monetization analysis together, Lingjing Wanwei's position in the industry value chain is clear:

```
Base-layer models (ByteDance Jimeng, Kuaishou Kling, SenseTime Seko)
        v  provide AI capability
Application-layer tools + content production (Lingjing Wanwei, Jiangyou Culture, Jellyfish Intelligence, etc.)
        v  provide comic-drama content
Distribution platforms (Douyin, Hongguo, Kuaishou, Tencent Firehound Comics)
        v  reach users
End users (280 million comic-drama users)
```

**Lingjing Wanwei sits in the middle layer -- upstream it depends on big tech's model capability, downstream it depends on big tech's distribution channels.** History has repeatedly shown that when both upstream and downstream are controlled by big tech, the profit margin of the middle-layer company gets squeezed continuously.

Big tech has two ways to eliminate the middle layer:

1. **Build it in-house**: ByteDance produces its own content (it already has the Xiaoyunque AI team)
2. **Open up the tools**: turn Jimeng/Kling into a comic-drama production tool "anyone can use," letting a flood of individual creators replace professional production companies

Either path shrinks Lingjing Wanwei's room to operate.

**Counterpoint**: The middle layer isn't necessarily doomed. Analogy from the phone industry -- Qualcomm makes the chips, channel distributors handle distribution, but "middle layer" brands like Xiaomi and OPPO do just fine, because they've built differentiation in product definition and supply-chain management. If Lingjing Wanwei can build differentiation in "creative content + traffic-acquisition efficiency + IP operations," it may still have room to survive.
