Recently I've had contact with some institutional investors, and I've gradually come to understand a bit about the procedures of institutional investing. It feels like sometimes investors and entrepreneurs look at things from genuinely different angles. In the past, when I was starting my business I never raised financing, and when I was doing public-market investing I never thought carefully about this difference. On one hand, I've gradually come to understand the difference between the institutional-investor perspective and the entrepreneur's perspective — this is new and unfamiliar to me. On the other hand, when I look back at my own thinking from my public-market-investing days, it seems consistent with entrepreneurship — not much different at all. It now seems that the education I received around investing and around running a business may be relatively niche, but judging by the results over time, it is, with high probability, effective. So I wanted to write down some of my past thinking on investing and on entrepreneurship. Setting the two side by side for comparison might be an interesting exercise.

## I. Investing Like Starting a Business

I understand Buffett's saying that buying stock means buying "part of the company" — you need a long-term mindset in order to look for good businesses and good partners. This is actually a lot like starting a business: on one hand, you need to focus on the business model, choosing the right one (not every model works), and you need to spend a great deal of time studying the details of that business model. On the other hand, you need to pick good "partners" — when investing, you should treat the founder or CEO as your future partner and ask yourself whether you are willing to work alongside them for the long run. If a founder's or CEO's character, or the company's culture, would make long-term collaboration uncomfortable for you, it's best not to buy on day one. It's the same in entrepreneurship — if you sense that someone isn't a good long-term fit for the team but is useful in the short term, hiring that person on often leads to regret; and if that ill-fitting person is a partner rather than a hire, it isn't just regret — it's regret beyond redemption.

Beyond focusing on a good business and a good team, investing also requires caring about whether the price is right. This may look different from entrepreneurship at first glance, but on closer reflection it's actually the same. Buffett has mentioned in many places that Charlie Munger evolved him "from an ape into a man" — it was Munger who made Buffett realize he should buy good companies at a reasonable price, rather than spend his time picking up "cigar butts" (companies that are cheap and carry some residual value, but are often not very good). This is even easier to appreciate in entrepreneurship: if a business is particularly easy to run and doesn't require paying a heavy price, it's often not the kind of good business that generates large amounts of cash flow over the long term. Good decisions are often difficult ones that require paying a painful price. A good company should put in the effort to solve — or overcome — the problems that are correct but hard, rather than scrounging for scraps everywhere (the mindset of scrounging for scraps everywhere often means you end up unable to pick up even the scraps — this is entirely different from accumulating small wins into a big win). Another price-related point in investing is looking at whether the loss, should the deal go wrong, is bearable overall. This is easier to picture from an entrepreneurial angle — when committing to a venture, you need to consider whether it could kill you; staying alive is the first priority in starting a business. At the same time, you also need to evaluate it from the other angle — whether you can win, and whether you have enough strength to commit to winning.

## II. Starting a Business Like Investing

As the saying goes, a man fears entering the wrong trade, a woman fears marrying the wrong husband. Just as investing pays special attention to the business model, the choice of industry and business model in entrepreneurship also determines a large part of the outcome. In other words, you need to spend a great deal of time studying what the right thing to do is, and only then think about how to do it correctly. Advancing steadily in the right direction is far better than sprinting in the wrong direction. Buffett has said he's seen many ordinary people make a fair amount of money in the financial industry, and he's also seen quite a few very smart, excellent people struggle painfully in bad industries. On the road of entrepreneurship, just as in investing, choice often matters more than effort. Moving forward on the right path, even slowly, is like compounding in investing — 20 consecutive years of 20% annual returns is formidable, and produces far greater returns than gaining 100% this year and losing 50% next year.

Buffett and Bill Gates, in a conversation with a group of MBA students, once asked the students a question: roughly, if you had to treat each of your classmates as a company, and had to invest all of your current money into just one of "them," taking a 5% stake, whom would you choose? Often you would not choose the smartest or the most capable one — you would choose the most trustworthy one. This analogy fits especially well when choosing a co-founder in entrepreneurship — what we need is often a trustworthy long-term partner, not someone who appears highly capable but whom you can never be sure won't stab you in the back.

When Buffett talks about investment targets, he often brings up a concept: a business's moat. If we treat every decision made in the course of starting a business as an investment decision, then we need to distinguish which of the things we exchange time and money for are assets and which are costs. Those things that, over time, deepen the business's moat tend to be "assets"; those that become more unfavorable to you the longer they persist can be regarded as costs. When it comes to acquiring assets, you're unlikely to waste much — at worst you overpay a little. But wasting money on costs is quite regrettable, and often carries negative side effects as well. In the course of starting a business, one special kind of asset is people united under a shared culture, and one kind of cost is the expense of buying labor or skills. Telling these two apart, and converting one into the other, is a fascinating exercise — and one with an especially high ROI. If all costs could be converted into appreciating assets, our CFOs and investors alike would probably be too happy to sleep. But interestingly, in the moment of actually investing, this kind of major, consequential distinction is often made carelessly, and given far too little weight.
