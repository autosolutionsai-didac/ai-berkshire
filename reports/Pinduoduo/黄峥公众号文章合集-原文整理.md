## 1. My Middle School and University

My elementary school was on the outskirts of Hangzhou, and it was, I'd say, quite ordinary. But through sheer dumb luck I got into a rather special middle school in Hangzhou: Hangzhou Foreign Languages School. According to my elementary school teacher, I was the only student from our school to get into that middle school in the nine years before and after me. Life can be a bit dramatic sometimes — I only got to take the entrance exam because I'd won a prize in a math olympiad, and my teacher told me to go for it. Before the exam, I barely knew what kind of school it was, and I did no review, no preparation, no practice tests at all. After I got the acceptance letter, I actually told my teacher I didn't want to go, because — going by the name — I thought it was a school that taught only foreign languages. I said I wanted to go to a middle school strong in math, physics, and chemistry instead. Later, the elementary school principal called me in and talked me into going. Looking back now, thank goodness I went.

Hangzhou Foreign Languages School was a good middle school. The teachers and classmates were great. Back in the early 1990s we already had foreign teachers, visiting international exchange students, and opportunities to go abroad for home stays. It was the only school in Zhejiang province at the time allowed to recruit students province-wide directly from elementary school — every other middle school admitted students by neighborhood — so it had a province-wide monopoly on picking elementary school graduates. It also ran a six-year continuous track from middle school into high school, with no new students admitted at the high school level. Its rate of students admitted directly to university without the gaokao [China's national college entrance exam] was also extremely high, over 80% every year, so getting into that middle school was practically equivalent to getting into university. Because of that, the teaching was relatively unaffected by the gaokao, and teachers often "did their own thing." I remember we even had several classes where we just watched American blockbusters (back then American blockbusters were rarely shown in Chinese theaters).

Our school took in about 160 students a year, the vast majority admitted purely by exam score. A small number got in by paying a sponsorship fee despite slightly lower scores. Among the 160, quite a few were children of officials or of wealthy families. Starting from the first year of middle school, boarding was mandatory — from the morning run to evening study hall to lights-out, we were all together every day. The interaction and mutual influence among us was far greater than at other schools, and our relationships with each other were much closer. Over those six years, from age 12 to 18, this small group of us shaped each other and formed values and a worldview somewhat different from students at other schools. Compared with other schools, we were exposed to Western culture earlier, more deeply, and more broadly. A fair number of us turned out more liberal than our peers.

After graduating from Hangzhou Foreign Languages School, I was admitted without examination into Zhejiang University's Mixed Class. The Mixed Class was the predecessor of Zhejiang University's Chu Kochen Honors College, something of an experiment in elite higher education. Each year, students admitted directly (without the gaokao) and students with relatively high gaokao scores could take a selection exam (written plus interview) for the Mixed Class. About 200 people made it into the class, and then "enjoyed" rather different "treatment" from students who went straight into a department. For the first two years, the class wasn't divided by department — everyone took the same basic science and engineering courses, using harder textbooks, taught by excellent professors. But at the end of each semester, a certain proportion of students would be cut and sent to a regular department. If you weren't cut in those two years, at the end you could choose an advisor and join a lab. Your major was then naturally determined by your advisor's specialty.

Although Zhejiang University couldn't match Tsinghua in overall resources and strength, the resources, opportunities, and freedom the Mixed Class enjoyed were still quite something. On one hand, the mentorship system gave students a good excuse to drop courses that wasted time or were boring. On the other hand, the university had certain hard-to-allocate resources and opportunities that often ended up going to the Mixed Class.

For example, in my freshman year I was fortunate enough to be selected for the Melton Foundation. The foundation was founded in the early 1990s by the founder of Verifone, the leader in point-of-sale terminals. He had already successfully sold Verifone by then, and had also invested in AOL and PayPal. When he saw the Berlin Wall fall, he realized the world was about to change dramatically, so he had an idea: select young students from a few representative emerging regions around the world at the time, let them build long-term relationships and grow together, in the hope that they might one day bring some positive change to the world together. Zhejiang University's Mixed Class was the only place in China where the Melton Foundation selected fellows. The other four regions were India, East Germany, Chile, and a Black community in the United States. Each region got five spots per year; once selected, you were given a computer and a full year of free internet access, so you could first build connections with peers from other regions over email and IM, and then each year everyone would fly to a member country and spend about ten days together at an annual meeting, discussing things and hanging out together. That yearly gathering gave me a close-up look at the daily habits of peers from other countries. One side effect was that I got used to Indian English very early on. That turned out to be a huge help later when I went to graduate school in the US, worked, took classes from Indian professors, and communicated with Indian colleagues.

This foundation had little impact on society at large, but it had a big impact on me and the other fellows alongside me. It made me deeply aware of just how different people of different races and cultures are. Their starting points, their ways of thinking about problems, and their ways of doing things were things I'd had no idea about before, and could hardly have imagined. There weren't many of us in the foundation, and the bonds we formed were correspondingly close — not only did a number of couples form among the Chinese fellows, but a number of cross-national couples formed too.

Looking back now, compared with my peers, my middle school and university experience was extremely, extremely lucky — you could even call it a bit unusual. This experience left me with some fairly deep impressions early on, about a few things:

A golden phoenix flying out of a mountain gully is a low-probability event. Most children of the wealthy, and especially of officials, are exceptionally capable.

Tian Ji's horse race [an ancient Chinese strategy story about winning through smart allocation of a weaker hand] — creating a local advantage out of an overall resource disadvantage, and thereby getting a chance to win the whole "campaign." From this, ordinary people can accomplish extraordinary things.

Money is a tool, not a goal.

My middle school and university years were, on the whole, lucky and fulfilling, but there was one significant regret: I was too goal-oriented, and in chasing being first, in working hard to be a good student, I wasted too much time and lost out on a lot of rebellion, mischief, and simply enjoying my youth. "It's a good philosophy to be content with 60 points" [i.e., not obsessing over top marks] is something I only slowly came to understand many years later.

## 2. My First Job

The first company to pay me a salary was Microsoft, though as an intern. I interned both at Microsoft Research Asia in Beijing and at Microsoft's headquarters in Redmond (the pay was very different between the two, ha). When I graduated, I didn't choose to stay at Microsoft — partly because I felt I could already see what I'd look like ten years down the road at Microsoft, and partly because when I asked my "life mentor," he said, "Google looks like a pretty awesome company, worth checking out. It'll also be good for you if you want to start a business in the future. If you go, stay at least three years, because one or two years isn't enough to really get into an important position and truly understand the company."

So, after graduating with my master's degree in 2004, I went to Silicon Valley, and Google became my first formal job — I worked as a coder and product manager. Later, when Google happened to be entering China, I was lucky enough to be among the first batch of employees who returned to China to take part in Google China's founding phase.

I stayed at Google for a full three years (though I left to start a business before all four years of stock vesting were up). Those three years were, for me, extremely worthwhile. What Google gave me far exceeded what I contributed to Google.

First, to put it plainly, Google gave me financial freedom in a very short time. I joined Google about half a year before its IPO, fresh out of school and a bit dazed, working on ad-backend system development. There weren't that many people at the company then — only a few hundred engineers. But the company went public very soon after, and its revenue, profit, and headcount all grew at breakneck speed. As I recall, when I first joined Google, the company's hourly revenue was in the low hundreds of thousands of dollars; later it became several million dollars an hour. Headcount quickly went from the thousands into the tens of thousands. The money in my bank account also suddenly grew a lot. Honestly, that feeling of a windfall from the sky wasn't very strong at the time, because it was my first job and I had no other personal experience to compare it against. It was only three or four years after leaving Google that I increasingly came to realize just how rare a company like Google is, and how rare it is to get the chance to join a company like that at a moment like that. Statistically speaking, it's fortunate enough to happen to a person once in a lifetime — at the very least, it's a once-in-ten-or-twenty-years opportunity.

Second, Google let me see the side effects of overnight wealth. As Buddhism apparently teaches, how much wealth one receives should correspond to how much merit one has accumulated — getting unexpected wealth without enough accumulated merit may not necessarily be a good thing. This was, to some extent, borne out in the trajectories of Google's early employees. Because they suddenly had far too much money, many people lost their motivation to work and went looking for new hobbies and pursuits — but often those new things weren't things they were actually good at, or even necessarily liked (flying a plane didn't necessarily work out; building telescopes didn't pan out; starting a business wasn't a fit for someone unsuited to being a boss, yet they ended up in the boss's chair anyway). And so, one way or another, they wasted a good number of years — years during which they might otherwise have achieved something even more outstanding.

Third, Google gave me the chance to closely experience and understand just how hard it is for a foreign internet company in China, and how tough the competition is with domestic Chinese internet companies. On one hand, crossing the barriers of geography and culture to get 100% trust and authorization from company headquarters is already a hard thing to achieve; without sufficient trust and authorization, dealing with the fast iteration required to keep up in the market, and with all kinds of competition — commercial and non-commercial, by-the-rules and by-the-unwritten-rules — is even harder. One extreme example: with Google's drawing power at the time, a job fair could pack a soccer stadium full of people, but the actual hiring results were a bit underwhelming relative to the hype — finding someone steady, hardworking, experienced, promising, and with good values was still extremely difficult. Contrary to what the impressive turnout might suggest, forming a combat-ready team was often far harder than for a homegrown Chinese internet company.

Beyond all this, because of Google's success, the outside world's attention to and discussion of Google also deepened my own thinking about Google and about companies in general. Of course, there are already far too many books about Google in bookstores and online — I'm unlikely to write anything more detailed or better than they have. But there are a few personal observations I still want to record here, so I can come back and look at them again later.

Google, in some ways, was actually a bit like China used to be — for instance, the "working class" [i.e., engineers] had high status, and ideology mattered a great deal. So when there was an ideological conflict, Google's response would go beyond that of an ordinary commercial company.

Google's management also had some similarities to China after reform and opening-up — encouraging grassroots innovation and bold experimentation, while keeping core power highly centralized, with the institutional advantage of being able to concentrate resources to accomplish major undertakings.

I think Google's "Don't Be Evil" is baked into Google's DNA, not just something said casually. Google genuinely puts its mission and values ahead of profit — as described in "Built to Last," for an excellent company, profit is a byproduct of doing the right things. At the same time, "Don't Be Evil" is also Google's own way of staying alert to its own capacity to do evil. The deeper you go inside Google, the more you realize just how much capability Google has to profit by doing evil — so declaring to everyone that it won't do evil, and inviting everyone to hold it accountable, is quite wise.

During my years at Google, most of Google's acquisitions were successful. Most of these successful cases were acquisitions of small companies with great teams — teams that could integrate into Google's culture and then grow into towering trees in Google's soil. That's very different from an acquisition made to eliminate a competitor.

There were also plenty of things Google never quite figured out, or wanted to change but couldn't. For instance, Google wanted to use the auction model that worked so well for its ad business for its own IPO, hoping to change, to some extent, the old and not-very-efficient stock-issuance industry — but the results were middling. Google's founders always tried to avoid Google sliding into a management style run by layers of professional managers, but it doesn't seem they escaped that either. Also, Google made huge investments and many attempts at social networking, but its fate there ended up looking a lot like Microsoft's fate in search. Some objective laws and realities can't be changed by a simple wish alone — often you need far more energy than you imagine, much the same as the fact that everyone eventually dies: an unavoidable reality you have to face.

## 3. Why Start a Business Again?

Lately a lot of people have asked me this question — why start a business again. Summing it up, I think it mainly comes down to these two points:

First, I like the current work and the team; starting a business again lets me be happier, or shall I say, more content.

Second, I still have some ambition, and some ability and energy that hasn't been released; I have a vague sense that the current opportunity might let me do something with a bigger impact, something that gives me a stronger sense of accomplishment.

At the end of the day, this second point is really the same as the first: starting a business again lets me be happier myself, and of course I also hope that happiness can radiate outward — from those close to me to more people I've never even met.

Of course, I also know I can't make everyone happy, or make everyone like me. That's not my goal either. Especially with this round of starting a business, which is far more socially engaged and touches far more people than my previous ventures — that inevitably means I'll have to face some unhappy things, and some people who don't like me very much. But staying true to my original intent, I hope that every day I can face myself with a clear conscience, feel at peace with myself, and feel I've done my duty. And if I can gradually develop the ability to selectively remember — remembering the happy things and forgetting the anxious and sad ones — that would be even better :)

A while back I was interviewed by Mr. Li Zhigang, who asked me a number of questions related to entrepreneurship. Mr. Zhigang's writing is far better than my own, so I'll "borrow" a few passages here as a further answer to why I'm starting a business again.

What do you think you're starting a business for?

Huang Zheng: At first, because of the education I received growing up, I felt starting a business was a good thing to do, so from the start I wanted to start one — starting a business meant, from the outset, wanting to build a company, a company that could make money, while I myself could become a bit more impressive. Before starting Pinhaohuo, I spent nine or ten months resting at home, and did a lot more thinking about life. I found that, in life, what matters most to me is pursuing my own happiness, and I discovered two things that bring me a deep sense of happiness:

The first is going deep, together with a group of people I like, breaking through obstacles to create something — that process really does bring me happiness. Laughing together, crying together, getting through hard times together — the feeling of a team is the same as the feeling of a family. I enjoy that process, and I hope everyone can have a happy future together. That's one side of it.

The other side, when it comes to Pinhaohuo specifically, is that I hope to do something with a bigger social impact than before — something useful to myself and useful to others too — something that, to some extent, can help good money drive out bad money, rather than the reverse. I once gave my colleagues this example: why did the melamine [milk scandal] happen? At its core, it was a process driven by consumers, where bad money drove out good — in the beginning, there must have been dairy producers who didn't use melamine, but those all died out.

I think in the whole shape of Chinese commerce — and not just China, the world is the same — there's a cycle where bad money drives out good. In doing fruit today, I think we have a bit of an opportunity: to put the fruit that's artificially ripened in cold storage and bought in bulk at a disadvantage, and to some extent let the naturally ripened fruit, the fruit grown with fewer or no pesticides, sell for a higher price instead. I think that's something with social value — it's the equivalent of me contributing to society.

How would you evaluate your team?

Huang Zheng: The analogy I use with my team is that life is a process — it's like migrant workers coming to Shanghai to work: you start out with poor skills, so you carry bricks, then later you wash dishes, after washing dishes you become a chef, and once you're good at being a chef you open a restaurant. That's the process for us — it doesn't mean my past work has nothing to do with the present; even one day, after I've opened a restaurant, I'll go back and wash dishes again, I'll go back and cook again — the whole thing strings together. So look at our team today: Pinhaohuo looks like a new company, but it's all veterans, people who've been starting businesses for many years.

What's your dream for the future?

Huang Zheng: Our team is maybe 20 years behind Alibaba's team, but I think we might have a chance, under a new distribution of traffic, new forms of user interaction, and new international conditions, to build a different kind of Alibaba. Of course, that statement might sound a bit too grand right now, but stepping forward one step at a time, it's not necessarily out of reach. It's not just a dream — I've also done the analysis: first, if you look at the whole e-commerce market, its form is actually changing a lot; the form that made Alibaba successful today may not be the form that succeeds tomorrow, but people's needs still exist, and will be further amplified, so the potential market is huge. Second, the older generation will always grow old, and the younger generation — or those of us gradually entering middle age — will always get there eventually, if not me, then someone else my age. So what I should do is approach it with an ordinary heart, do my job steadily and solidly, and strive to become the most reliable one among my generation.

## 4. Reading Russell: Happiness and the Greed for Freedom

From childhood, the education I received always pushed me to learn from advanced role models and aspire to be a useful person. So, looking back now, I started thinking about role models, life goals, and even the meaning of life very early on.

Starting in middle school, I imagined becoming a scientist, a statesman, an entrepreneur. I read biographies, thinking about who I most wanted to become. Then I started really enjoying philosophy books, pondering questions like "I think, therefore I am." In short, it feels like from the moment I learned to read, I was constantly setting goals for myself, then looking for optimized paths to achieve those goals and my understanding of life's grand goal.

It wasn't until much later, several years into starting my business, that I began to realize that achieving your goals and being happy aren't necessarily the same thing. My thinking about, understanding of, and exploration into happiness came very late.

At first, I read Tal Ben-Shahar's "Happier," said to be a famous course at Harvard. It had some short-term effect on me, but it didn't last. On this topic I went through a good while of pondering and some not-so-easy practice, and gradually I felt I was starting to understand it a bit.

Then I read Bertrand Russell's "The Conquest of Happiness" (in Chinese it's usually translated more literally as "The Road to Happiness"). The book isn't thick, but it explains things very thoroughly, and all in plain, approachable language. It said everything I'd already been thinking about on this topic, and, remarkably, the happiness problems faced by different professions and social classes a hundred years ago seem no different from today's. To borrow Charlie Munger's classic response: "I have nothing to add."

So why am I still writing this? Mainly for two reasons: one, I want to make, from memory, an "excerpt" of the book's content, for my own future review, or for comparison when I reread the book. Two, I want to write a little about what I've observed of Russell's own life in contrast with his own theory of happiness, because that's something Russell's own book basically doesn't cover. Those two points make up the title of this piece.

### I. Russell's "Happiness"

Having finished "The Conquest of Happiness," my own summary comes down to roughly these few sentences:

A) Have the courage to face common sense, use common sense to make rational judgments, and let rational intent guide your actions.

B) Shift your interest away from perfecting an infinitely ideal self, toward an interest in external, objective things.

C) Learn to let go of things that cannot be changed, that cannot possibly be conquered.

The first sentence here is the core, because the latter two are, in fact, the natural results of practicing the first. For the first sentence, the two key words are "rationality" and "common sense." Below, please bear with my rather loose "excerpting" — from the book, and embellished by my own memory — of what these two words mean.

**Common sense**

Human beings are an extremely, extremely tiny presence in the universe, and a human life is an unimaginably short span of time; what we can do and what we can change are both extremely limited. This should be a background truth we're always aware of.

A person's way of thinking is, to a great extent, determined by their background, the education they received in their youth, and their current self-interested role. So when judging an authority's view, or a commonly-held worldly opinion, or a parent's expectation, you need to be able to see the background behind it, and view it in light of that background and interested role. Once you've factored in those background facts, what you usually need is just common sense to make the judgment — what's needed more is not brilliance, but whether you have the courage, when facing the facts, to still pursue reason. (Common sense is, in fact, obvious and very easy to understand, but the various inherited biases and self-interest-driven biases we carry blind us, making us turn a blind eye to this common sense. Doesn't this sound a lot like what Buddhist scripture teaches?)

Different regions and different social circles have different values. What's non-mainstream in one place may well be mainstream somewhere else. In Russell's view, in the England of his time, mainstream American values were about competing on wealth. Whereas in England, when there was still an aristocratic class, what the aristocracy competed on was more about non-wealth things, like art.

"One of the most regrettable traits of ordinary human nature is envy, because an envious person cannot find joy in what they themselves have, but instead finds pain in what others have." Envy is a major driving force pushing toward equality between classes, nations, and genders. At the same time, hoping to achieve that kind of equality through envy will also produce the worst kind of equality — it cuts down the happiness of the fortunate, rather than increasing the happiness of the unfortunate.

"One trait of human nature is that people are always more willing to give love to those who don't need it as much, just as a bank is more willing to lend money to those who don't need it as much."

"Your motives are not always as unselfish as you think they are."

"Don't overestimate your own worth."

"Don't expect others to value you as highly as you value yourself."

"All unhappiness is caused by some kind of division or inconsistency. Discord between the conscious and unconscious mind creates a split within the self. Failing to connect yourself to society through objective interests and the power of love creates inconsistency between the self and the outside world."

**Rationality**

"It is not reason's job to stir up emotion, though part of reason's role is indeed to find ways to prevent emotions that would harm one's well-being."

At the same time, "reason has no wish to diminish any intense emotion. A rational person, on realizing they feel all or part of these emotions, will be glad to be capable of feeling them, and will not try to restrain them."

### II. The Greed for Freedom

Russell himself said, "Three passions, simple but overwhelmingly strong, have governed my life: the unbearable urge to seek out truth; unbearable compassion for human suffering; and an unbearable pursuit of love." These unbearable pursuits of his gave us not only works like "Principia Mathematica," which had a huge influence on mathematics and philosophy, but also gave us his "diverse and important" Nobel Prize-winning body of literary work, of which "The Conquest of Happiness" is one.

At the same time, these unbearable pursuits of his also brought him four marriages. What I haven't quite figured out is that his four marriages, and his "overly open" behavior at the time, must also have brought pain to some people. That pain would in turn have acted on him too. That shouldn't have been conducive to joy, or to happiness. Could these three "unbearable" pursuits also be understood as a greed for freedom?

In his own chapter "On Effort and Resignation" in "The Conquest of Happiness," Russell says, "The doctrine of the golden mean is a boring one, and yet many things prove it to be true." So, should the pursuit of knowledge and of freedom also involve some giving up? For instance, giving up the endless pursuit of whether God exists, and simply believing wholeheartedly that He does — would that make one happier? If one could willingly give up part of one's freedom, and firmly believe that staying with one person, in one country, in one place, is good — would that make one happier?

Of course, Russell's own longevity also makes me question whether my speculation about his life lacks a sufficient factual basis. After all, I find it hard to believe that either an extremely unhappy person, or someone who indulged extremely all their life, could live to ninety-eight.

## 5. Investing Like Starting a Business, and Starting a Business Like Investing

I've recently been in contact with some institutional investors, and have gradually come to understand a bit about institutional investing procedures. I feel that, sometimes, an investor's perspective and an entrepreneur's perspective really are quite different. Before, I had never raised financing when starting my own businesses, and I never thought hard about this distinction when doing secondary-market investing either. On one hand, I'm gradually coming to understand the difference in perspective between institutional investors and entrepreneurs — that's new and unfamiliar to me. On the other hand, when I look back at how I thought about secondary-market investing before, it seems consistent with entrepreneurship, without much difference. It now seems that the investing and business education I received might be relatively unusual, but judging by how things have turned out over time, it also seems to be, with high probability, effective. So I wanted to write down some of my past thinking on investing and on entrepreneurship. Comparing the two might be a somewhat interesting exercise.

### I. Investing Like Starting a Business

As I understand it, Buffett says buying stock is buying part of a company — you should have the mindset of holding for the long term, seeking out good businesses and good partners. That's actually a lot like starting a business. On one hand, you need to focus on the business model, choose the right business model (not every model works), and spend a great deal of time researching the details of that business model. On the other hand, you need to pick good "partners" — when investing, you should treat the founder and CEO as your own future partners, and ask yourself whether you're willing to be with them long-term, doing things together. If this founder's or CEO's character, or the culture they've built, would make long-term collaboration with them uncomfortable for you, then the best move is not to buy on day one. It's the same as starting a business: if you sense someone isn't a good long-term fit for the team, but is useful for the moment, hiring that person on often ends up something you regret. And if that ill-fitting person is a partner, it's not just regret — it's regret beyond remedy.

Beyond focusing on good businesses and good teams, investing also requires caring about whether the price is right. That seems, on the surface, different from starting a business, but on closer thought it's actually the same. Buffett has said in many places that Charlie Munger evolved him from an ape into a man — it was Charlie Munger who made Buffett realize he should buy good companies at a reasonable price, rather than spend his time picking up cigar butts (companies that are cheap, with some residual value, but usually not particularly good). This is even easier to grasp in entrepreneurship: if a business is especially easy to run, and doesn't require paying a big price, it's often not a good business that generates large cash flows over the long term. Good decisions are often hard ones, ones that require paying a painful price. A good company should put in the effort to solve, and overcome, problems that are correct but difficult — rather than picking up sesame seeds all over the place (the mindset of picking up sesame seeds everywhere often means you can't even manage to pick up a sesame seed — and that's a completely different thing from accumulating small wins into a big win). Another price-related point in investing is looking at whether, if the deal turns into a loss, it's something you can bear overall. Looking at this through the lens of entrepreneurship makes it more concrete: when you put yourself into a venture, you need to see whether it could destroy you — staying alive is the first priority in entrepreneurship. At the same time, you often need to evaluate it from another angle too: can you win, and do you have enough strength to invest in order to win.

### II. Starting a Business Like Investing

As the saying goes, a man fears entering the wrong trade, a woman fears marrying the wrong man. Just as investing especially requires attention to the business model, the choice of industry and business model in entrepreneurship also, in large part, determines the outcome. In other words, you need to spend a great deal of time researching what the right thing to do is, and only then think about how to do that thing correctly. Moving forward step by step in the right direction is far better than sprinting in the wrong direction. Buffett has said he's seen many ordinary people make quite a lot of money in the financial industry, and also seen some very smart, outstanding people struggle bitterly in bad industries. On the road of entrepreneurship, as in investing, choice often matters more than effort. Moving forward on the right path, even if a bit slowly, is like compounding at 20% a year for 20 years in investing — that's formidable, and yields far more than a 100% gain this year followed by a 50% loss next year.

At an MBA gathering, Buffett and Bill Gates once asked the students a question: roughly, if you treated each of your classmates as a company, and had to invest all the money you currently have into one of them for a 5% stake, which one would you choose? Often you wouldn't choose the smartest or the most capable one — you'd choose the most trustworthy one. This analogy is especially fitting when choosing a co-founder for a business: what we need is usually a trustworthy long-term partner, not someone who seems highly capable but whom you can never be sure won't stab you in the back.

When discussing investment targets, Buffett often mentions a concept: the business's moat. If you treat every decision made in the course of starting a business as an investment decision, then you have to distinguish which of the things you've traded time and money for are assets and which are costs — the things that, as time passes, help deepen the business's moat, tend to be "assets," while the things that hurt you more the longer time goes on can be seen as costs. When it comes to acquiring assets, mistaken waste isn't really much of a risk — at worst you just overpaid a bit. But wasting on costs is truly detestable, and often has negative side effects too. There's a special kind of asset in entrepreneurship: people united under a certain culture; and there's a kind of cost: the money spent purchasing labor or skills. Distinguishing between the two, and converting one into the other, is a fascinating thing to do, and one with an especially high ROI. If all costs could be turned into appreciating assets, I imagine our CFOs and investors would be laughing all the way to sleepless nights. But interestingly, in investing, this kind of major, differentiating distinction is often made carelessly, with far too little weight given to it.

## 6. The Uncertainty of Love — Buddhism, Quantum Mechanics, Logic, and AI

Since childhood I would go with my grandfather to bow before the Buddha. I can't quite say whether my grandfather counted as a Buddhist, but he firmly believed there existed bodhisattvas, and some superhuman power higher than our world, yet not fully knowable. Back then, when we burned paper ingots and offered food, it seems we would sometimes also make offerings to "White Impermanence" and "Black Impermanence" [ghostly deities of impermanence in Chinese folk religion], though I had no idea what those were. Later, in middle school, I was taught that the Buddhist saying "it is not the wind that moves, nor the flag that moves, but the mind that moves" was idealism. Then later still I read some books on Zen, which always said Buddhist teaching must be understood through enlightenment, that it cannot be put into words, and that anything explained clearly is not the true path to Buddhahood. Later still, on a visit to Fo Guang Shan, I watched a promotional film by Master Hsing Yun saying the Buddha was a person, not a god, and that everyone is a Buddha. Buddhism teaches that all is emptiness, and that one should cling to nothing. That's roughly the core of my memory of Buddhism, as a non-Buddhist who nonetheless holds it in reverence. Clearly, my understanding of Buddhism is extremely shallow and vague.

In university, there was one course I only partially took but that fascinated me even though I didn't fully understand it: quantum mechanics. Quantum mechanics says that everything has a wave-particle duality — meaning I am both a wave and a particle. There's a famous principle called the uncertainty principle: the more precisely you measure a particle's position, the greater the uncertainty in its velocity, and vice versa. Beyond these two points, I had two thoughts of my own. First, about waves: waves are fascinating things — every small local part basically embodies the overall characteristics of the whole wave, yet you can never fully observe its complete picture the way you can with a particle, because it can spread out infinitely, and the observer is immersed in it too. Any measurement we make is really an interaction with the whole wave, a response after it's been disturbed — by the time you measure it, it's no longer what it originally was, and what it originally was, you can never fully know, because the moment you measure it, it's no longer itself, and if you don't measure it, you can't perceive its existence at all. Even if you're within it, or right next to it, if there's no interaction, then it's as if it never existed. This feels a lot like the Buddhist notion of impermanence and unknowability — you can only guess, only intuit, but you can never "grasp" it. It's impermanent, it's everywhere, and you're within it; if it's a wave spreading through infinity, then maybe you're part of that wave, you yourself are the entirety of that wave's characteristics, yet you can never truly grab hold of it. Any attachment of yours, any deliberate measurement of yours, is a kind of interference on it, and after that interference, it's no longer itself — and even you are no longer your original self. What exactly is it? You have no way of knowing, and you don't need to know, because in fact, you are it.

Another thought is about the means by which we come to know the world. When we look at the various sciences through which we come to know the world, it seems, without exception, to be a science of "seeing is believing" — our measurement of the world relies mainly on light. I don't understand relativity, but I have a vague sense that in order to accommodate light (to give light a special, invariant speed), relativity had to, conceptually, make time itself change (of course, what time even is was already a question in the first place — when time is measured by light it's one thing; what if it were measured by touch, or by mental contemplation instead?). Our dependence on light is so strong that I can't help but feel that the reason the world we see looks the way it does is simply because we use light to measure it — in other words, our method of measurement created this world. This world is one we've created through our own subjective measurement; if we didn't have to rely on it, what would the world be like? But perhaps we simply can't do that, because we ourselves are some kind of broad wave — if that's the case, then all the so-called materialism of "seeing is believing" is, itself, idealism.

In 1931, the brilliant and short-lived mathematician-logician Gödel proposed the incompleteness theorem, shattering the millennia-old dream of mathematicians and philosophers alike. The basic idea is that within any self-consistent system built on a finite set of axioms, there will always be propositions that this logical system cannot prove to be true or false — that is, there will always be problems this system cannot know or decide. It tells us in no uncertain terms that trying to explain the world using a finite set of axioms plus logic is futile. Gödel proved that this is impossible.

AI is especially hot right now, and a lot of people are starting to feel AI will replace humanity. But my vague sense is that if it's built on today's kind of 0-and-1 deterministic (mechanical) judgment, computers should have enormous limitations. They run straight into the undecidability problem that Gödel's proof describes. Once we have quantum computers in the future, what will that be like? I don't know, because in theory a quantum computer might not be limited to a finite set of axioms, might not be limited to mechanical 0-1 judgments anymore.

Buddhism, quantum mechanics, and the mathematical logic of the incompleteness theorem all seem to be telling us:

The world and human life, taken as a whole, are unknowable — at the very least, they cannot be precisely measured; they are indeterminate, uncertain.

It's impossible to describe and regulate the world with a finite set of rules; things are imperfect and incomplete.

Beyond this, I have a vague sense that what you see is, to a great extent, determined by how you look — and that even though the whole is unknowable, a local part may nonetheless describe the whole.

And yet we humans always want the very thing we lack — missing an arm, we want an arm; missing a leg, we want a leg. Life will inevitably pass, yet we constantly seek eternal youth; love is never eternal, yet we pin our hopes on an unchanging stone. Perhaps the world itself is inherently indeterminate, inherently impermanent, and that's precisely why we so relentlessly pursue eternity, pursue the security of certainty. Not only do we pursue it, we also keep wanting to grab hold of it, to disturb it, in order to prove its existence and our own. Yet, little do we know, its existence may not be at all what we imagine, and it was never unchanging to begin with — the more you measure it, the more you disturb it, and the more you disturb it, the less it remains its original self. By the time you've measured it enough times to feel at ease, it's already been changed by you. Just like trust, just like love — it's indeterminate; measure it too much, and it's no longer there. Only by believing wholeheartedly, only by accepting and acknowledging an uncertain fate, can it exist, and can you be happy.

## 7. Bad Money Drives Out Good, the Movie Theater Phenomenon, and Other Reflections

It's been a long time since I last wrote a public account article, partly because I've wanted to write on this topic but my thoughts were too tangled to know how to begin. A few days ago, chatting after dinner with a good friend, we touched on a few points that made things a bit clearer to me, so I'm hurrying to write it down.

**1. Bad money drives out good, viewed from a neutral standpoint**

First, "bad money" and "good money" are neutral concepts. The phenomenon of bad money driving out good, first identified by the English financial minister Gresham, describes this: when two legal currencies with different gold content circulate in a market, and the money supply is abundant, people tend to keep the currency with higher gold content at home as a store of value, and spend the currency with lower gold content first. This phenomenon is also called Gresham's Law. It's actually quite easy to understand and consistent with human nature — as happened in the late Republic of China era, when the state desperately issued Gold Yuan notes (paper currency) to replace silver dollars. Naturally, everyone would spend the paper notes first. For the mint, Gold Yuan notes were cheap to produce and could be printed at will, which was good for the mint, but bad for ordinary people — they were the "bad money." Naturally, people voted with their feet, and the market was naturally flooded with "bad money," while "good money" rose in value — this is individual, self-protective behavior by the public, and if that individual behavior fails to organize into a collective force, then the single, more powerful mint wins — by printing Gold Yuan notes, it robbed most people of a portion of their personal wealth.

So is bad money driving out good something everyone wants to stop? Not necessarily. First, the mint wants to issue bad money; second, the proliferation of bad money is driven along by every single member of the public. When people say "bad money driving out good" is a bad thing, part of the reason is that they hope others will give them good money, while they themselves spend the bad money first. This selfishness is understandable, but the result is that the public as a whole loses out, and no individual can protect themselves either. Without solidarity, the public can't form enough collective force to counter the coercive power that dictates equal face value between bad and good money.

This phenomenon describes, from one angle, how selfish, unorganized individuals cannot resist a manager's coercive power; from another angle, it describes a phenomenon of redistributing wealth and lowering the cost of public services by exploiting the power of individual selfishness.

**2. The movie theater phenomenon**

Another phenomenon is the "movie theater phenomenon," similar to "bad money driving out good," but different. It describes a possible phenomenon of collective self-harm among a group of unorganized, selfish individuals lacking any external coercive force — namely, in a movie theater, if the people in the front row stand up, the people behind them will stand up too, and the result is that what could have been a pleasant experience of everyone sitting and watching turns into the exhausting experience of everyone standing and watching.

In my view, the movie theater phenomenon is more worth studying and addressing. Because it's a phenomenon of collective self-harm — nobody actually benefits — unlike "bad money driving out good," which is really one force defeating another and thereby driving the spread of bad money. Nowadays, "bad money drives out good" is the phrase most often quoted, but I think most of the time, what's actually being referred to is really the "movie theater phenomenon."

**3. One turd spoils the whole pot of soup**

The people who stood up first in the movie theater are like a bit of feces falling into a pot of soup — very soon the whole pot can only be thrown out. What this points to is a matter of proportion. Soup actually has some self-purifying capacity, just as polluted water flowing into a river and out to sea can be fine if the proportion is very low. But once that proportion rises past a critical point, the original purifying capacity collapses. Often we'll say a bad phenomenon isn't mainstream, so it's not a problem, and that it exists in another, otherwise healthy system too, as if there's no difference between the two sides — that's a very dangerous line of thinking. You need to realize that the critical proportion at which one turd spoils a whole pot of soup is often very low — maybe the difference between 1% and 5%. At 1% the system can still purify itself; at 5% the whole thing has to be thrown out. For example, in a group of people, if only 1% are con artists, then a presumption of innocence toward the group lets society run efficiently and well — but if that proportion rises to 5% or 8%, then a presumption of innocence would push everyone else toward becoming con artists too. In that situation, society can only shift to a presumption of guilt, and that is a difference in kind. The cultural operating efficiency of the two systems undergoes a fundamental change. But take careful note: this qualitative change from a quantitative one doesn't usually happen at 50% — it usually happens at a much lower value. In business, we know Amazon effectively determined book pricing, and thereby upended the book retail industry, while its market share in books was still far below 50%. Everyone carries cancer cells in their body, and it's normally fine — but once their replication and spread exceeds a certain value, you have cancer, and cancer doesn't need to spread to 50% of your body to kill you.

So when we talk about looking at the mainstream, or comparing two systems that aren't 100% perfect, we absolutely must pay attention to the difference between 1% and 5%; we must never assume that as long as it's a minority, the nature of the situation is the same. Very often, the qualitative change happens right in that 1%-to-5% range.

**4. Reversing collective self-harm requires enormous energy**

If a group of a hundred people has 8 con artists, and systematic research shows that the presumption-of-innocence system only works if the proportion is below 3%, then converting 5 of those 8 people won't take the effort of just 5 people — it typically takes 10 times the effort, meaning 50 people working simultaneously to convert those 5. One example is New York's crackdown on graffiti in public spaces: it's said that Grand Central Terminal in Manhattan was once covered in graffiti everywhere, and to fix this, the mayor put in enormous effort, repainting one wall every day — no matter how much someone else tainted it, he would repaint the whole thing — over and over, day after day, until the graffiti gradually diminished and then disappeared. Singapore's crackdown on spitting in public is said to have been similar — it cost many, many times more than what the offenders had "invested" in the bad behavior, before the "atmosphere" was reversed and a healthy operating system was established.

**5. Channels have value; expensive things can sell better**

One way to change public sentiment is direct, broad enforcement; another is to make use of channels. In business, there's a phenomenon worth discussing: sometimes something more expensive actually sells better. Take air conditioners, for example — they need someone to install them, someone to recommend them. It's possible that a direct-to-consumer internet-sales model like Xiaomi's, pushing the price down to the "lowest," might not do as well as Gree's approach of giving installers and channel partners enough profit margin. Even though, on the surface, going through channels raises the price of the goods, in practice, the channel and service cost may be the most economical, healthiest way to maintain a certain kind of public perception. That channel and service cost is necessary — it puts the cost of maintaining a certain order out in the open — and a system that ignores that cost may well slide toward "bad money driving out good." This kind of phenomenon, which violates what economics textbooks teach about supply-and-demand price curves, is actually extremely common. Charlie Munger has said, in his book, more than once that we can easily find such examples in consumer goods, enterprise software, hardware, and other sectors — this phenomenon, where the pricier option actually sells better, may even be the mainstream pattern in quite a few industries.

## 8. More Market or More Planning — Some Thoughts on Supply-Side Reform

A few days ago, a friend and I were chatting about the history of garment production; he said today's large-scale, mass production of clothing owes its existence to World War I, because the war triggered demand for rapid, mass-scale production of clothing, and that's how today's standardized sizing came about. Burberry's now-fashionable trench coat, he said, was also originally produced for the military. I haven't fact-checked the full history, but I have seen Burberry's trench coat ads, which do tell a moving wartime story. On first hearing my friend's account, I was a bit surprised, and also a bit skeptical, but it did activate my thinking over the past year on supply-side reform.

Supply and demand are two sides of the same coin, mutually reinforcing, somewhat like the chicken-and-egg problem — it's hard to say which comes first. But to drive reform, you always have to start from one end, and the story my friend told seemed to echo a vague yet strong feeling I already had.

To fundamentally transform the supply side, you first have to transform the demand side; the demand side is the lever that pulls the supply side into transformation.

The first category of supply-side transformation satisfies a need that has always existed but was never fully met. For example, people wanted to fly, and one day there were finally airplanes, satisfying the need to fly and also the need for fast transoceanic travel. This large category covers most upgrades on the supply side, satisfying people's pursuit of faster, more, better, and more beautiful. When one greed is satisfied, another greed appears. Of course, this greed is mostly called "pursuit" instead, most of the time.

The second category of supply-side transformation arises from the emergence of new application scenarios. For example, the outbreak of a world war. Under this sudden new application scenario, clothing and many other daily necessities were needed in batches, at scale, and in standardized form, driving enormous transformation in supply-side production organization, management, and delivery and distribution.

The third kind of supply-side transformation arises from changes in the aggregation of front-end demand, or, put another way, from a drastic change in the cost of collecting information. For example, once smartphones appeared, the cost of collecting routes, locations, and ride requests dropped drastically, making it possible to satisfy people's need for rides through a sharing model like Uber's.

There are probably other circumstances that trigger supply-side transformation that I haven't thought of, but whichever kind of supply-side transformation it is, there should always be a corresponding demand behind it. Large shifts in the scenarios of human need don't happen often (the kind that triggers the second category of transformation, like a war creating a new scenario). Upgrades to existing human needs (more, faster, better, and so on) happen quite a lot, but are all these pursuits of more and faster things worth pursuing and encouraging? That's a question worth reflecting on, though I won't expand on it here. As for the third category of transformation, extending this line of thought is quite interesting — there exists a large space of possibilities to trade the unification of time and space for greater overall efficiency and lower costs. In the abstract, there exists a possibility of a semi-market-economy in which pushing the demand-and-circulation side to adopt more "planning" drives the supply side to break through its lagging, large-scale planned-production model, and achieve small- and mid-scale batch "customized production" instead — that is:

Using a semi-"planned economy" on the demand-and-circulation side to drive a semi-"market economy" on the supply side.

Our original online e-commerce solved the problem of moving the Yiwu small-commodities market online — it made information on the circulation side highly transparent, and accelerated market competition among all kinds of vendors — but it didn't change traditional manufacturing factories very much. How much a factory should produce still requires some planning; the main factories still mostly depend on bulk orders from offline supermarket chains, planning along cycles of several months or even six months to a year. The more intense and market-driven the competition among online vendors becomes, the more severe the disconnect and contrast with offline production planning also becomes. The disconnect between planned batch international trade orders and scattered online sales orders also grows more severe. Under these conditions, the main factories still mostly depend on Walmart and Carrefour. The extreme marketization of the pure circulation side online doesn't change the lagging, highly planned nature of production on the supply side. So does there exist a model that could change the laggard, highly rigid, planned nature of the production side? I believe we still need to work on transformation from the demand side.

Suppose we could get front-end consumers to be a bit more patient and willing to coordinate with others, giving up some of the impulse for instant, what-you-see-is-what-you-get gratification, then we'd have a chance to use recommendations between people, relationships between people, and similarities in interests to group people together, consolidating each individual's personalized demand into planned demand with a certain degree of lead time. This kind of demand aggregation might not be as large as Walmart's roughly six-month bulk order, but it would still be enough to economically run a factory's production line. This way, we'd have a chance to split one big Walmart order into 50 smaller batch orders, and the back-end production could then break free of its dependence on Walmart, changing the original model of authorizing a single producer for full-volume planned production, and instead have dozens of manufacturers with production capacity compete in the market for these 50 batch orders, aggregated according to various different demands.

If we could do this, then the contradiction between highly marketized online circulation and rigid, planned production on the supply side could ease. In its place, more planned demand and a more market-driven supply side would merge together. Front-end and back-end information would be integrated more thoroughly, eliminating the mismatch between demand and production, and further helping our traditional production break free of its dependence on traditional Walmart-style supermarket chains, letting it find its own differentiation within the differentiated, batch-aggregated demand, and thereby achieve true supply-side transformation. From rigid to flexible, from homogeneous to differentiated and distinctive, from lagging planning to a semi-market model synchronized with demand.

## 9. Turning "Capitalism" Upside Down

Buffett is an admirable capitalist, a pure capitalist. His entire career can be described as tirelessly, single-mindedly, rationally moving money around to enjoy the fruits of compounding. I enjoy reading his letters to shareholders — for decades he has repeated the same simplicity, repeated a purity that is not easy to achieve. In his empire, one hand holds insurance, the other holds investing; one hand sells the ability to withstand risk and collects money for it, while the other hand puts that money into orchards with moats, orchards capable of producing compounding returns.

Originally, when I started writing this public account, I really wanted to write an article about insurance, with a working title of "Insurance, the Ultimate Expression of Capitalism." The gist would be that insurance is quite an interesting product, and very much embodies capitalism. The "rich," having capital and "plenty of money," have strong resilience against risk; the "poor," with "little money," have weak resilience against risk. So the "poor" need to buy this resilience against risk from the "rich." Although insurance really is something many people need, and does give them a more stable life, or at least a greater peace of mind, ultimately, this kind of product further accelerates the transfer of wealth from those without money to those with money — calling it the ultimate expression of capitalism is because it further amplifies the power of capital. The soft, intangible notion that "having money = greater safety" is also monetized through insurance. If this continues, and if the market is highly efficient and unobstructed, and the law protects the legitimacy of capital and its compounding, then it's quite likely that the rich will get richer and the poor will get poorer.

The reason Buffett is admirable, even worthy of being called great, I believe, is this: he is not merely a supremely talented capitalist who has played the game of capital to its very extreme; he is, more than that, a lovable person, who clearly understands that money is not the goal. On one hand, he enjoys the pleasure that the game of capital brings him; on the other hand, he wisely gave the great majority of his money to Bill Gates, younger than himself, and let Gates carry out wealth's proper redistribution with peace of mind. At the same time, he has not shied away from controversy in urging other wealthy people to donate their money too, and in advocating that the state raise taxes on the wealthy, pushing for greater redistribution of wealth at the institutional level. (Interestingly, Buffett's own father was a Republican member of Congress, yet what Buffett now advocates doesn't look much like Republican positions at all.)

In capitalist America, a remarkable Buffett was born, one who found joy in the game of insurance and compounding capital, and then gently handed the burden of that money over to Bill Gates. That's such wisdom — probably the simplest, most effortless way for a capitalist to find happiness under capitalism. Money is first accumulated, then redistributed; in that cycle, Buffett has focused mainly on the first half. In a "post-capitalist" era, suppose the effective redistribution of money mattered just as much as its accumulation. I can't help but wonder: might there be a way to use insurance and compounding — or, shall we say, an inverted version of insurance and compounding — to make the distribution of wealth a bit more even? Could there be a mechanism that lets poor people also sell "insurance" to the rich — where poor people, too, could sell some of their own "soft power," their own willingness, their own resilience against risk, to the rich, thereby achieving a more finely tuned feedback loop, a shorter-cycle flow of money back from the rich to the poor?

For example, suppose a thousand people, in the summer, already know they'll want to buy a certain style of down jacket in the winter, and they jointly sign a joint order to a manufacturer, willing to pay a 10% deposit at last year's price. In this situation, it's quite possible the factory would be willing to give them a 30% discount, because the joint order gives the factory a kind of demand certainty it wouldn't otherwise have. That certainty can be converted into the convenience of producing during a lull in the production schedule, or into confidence when procuring raw materials. The factory could even further sell that certainty to upstream and supporting manufacturers, in exchange for further reductions in the factory's own costs. In terms of the transaction format, this is like a group of people each paying 1 yuan to jointly buy a 3-yuan limited-time discount voucher, and then the factory, having sold these vouchers, can in turn go and buy similar limited-time vouchers from its upstream and supporting manufacturers — for example, spending a thousand to buy three thousand's worth of limited-time vouchers. If these thousand people have some credit history, and they jointly place an order expressing their intent but without paying a deposit, would the factory still be willing to give them a discount? I imagine it would — maybe not 30%, but would 8% work? This is essentially the factory using its own self-issued discount vouchers to buy, from ordinary consumers, an "insurance policy" guaranteeing a future purchase. Taking this further, there are actually many ways one could marketize, productize, and monetize an ordinary person's willingness and their own certainty about their future needs. Suppose the system gave each person only one chance to express their intent to buy a winter coat — that would be like giving everyone a "winter-coat intent voucher" (a voucher that person might have earned by trading in accumulated credit) — would that voucher then be worth something to the factory-owning capitalist? How would the price be set here, and what limits should apply to this bilateral transaction?

The essence here is that every person (rich or poor alike) usually understands their own intentions, their own needs and plans at some future point, far better than anyone else does. And this kind of individual planning and intent, along with an individual's grasp of the certainty of their own future behavior, is often valuable to the supply side trying to meet that need. It can reduce the uncertainty of organizing production, and can help achieve more efficient allocation of resources and capital. For this reason, I'd guess that capitalists and the wealthy would be willing to buy this reverse insurance from ordinary people. This reverse insurance would let every ordinary person's credit and intent be monetized — this reverse insurance is no longer the ordinary borrowing-and-lending model where the poor accumulate credit and borrow money from the rich, paying interest (in a lending scenario, the poor person, having borrowed money, has to pay interest, so what they buy actually costs them more than it costs the rich) — nor is it paying the rich for certainty in one's life. Instead, it's the reverse: the rich, the capitalists, pay ordinary people, the poor, for the certainty of their production-capital allocation. In the first kind of insurance and lending-and-financing product, money flows from the poor to the rich; in this reverse insurance, money flows from the rich to the poor. There ought to be a qualitative difference here.

The next question is how to productize this certainty each person (rich or poor) has about their own intentions and behavior; how to standardize it so it can circulate like a discount voucher; how to create formats to express intent; how to create products to realize the transmission of this certainty; and further, how to financialize and monetize the transmission of this certainty. Beyond that, we should consider decentralizing the productization process of this "certainty product" (because there are too many scenarios, too many situations involved), and, in this relatively decentralized process of producing and circulating "certainty products," find ways to prevent fraud, so as to form a virtuous cycle where good money drives out bad. I wonder whether blockchain was, in some sense, born just in time for this kind of "reverse insurance"...

(Having finished writing this, thinking it over, this inverted capitalism really is kind of interesting :))

## 10. Letter to Shareholders, June 30, 2018

Dear Shareholders,

Pinduoduo is not a traditional company. It was born at a time when everyone thought the e-commerce landscape was already settled, when the history books had already been written. In just three short years, it brought together more than 300 million users and over a million sellers, together building a new way of shopping. Even though its rapid growth shows it holds enormous potential and boundless future possibility.

But it is still, after all, only a three-year-old child, still carrying many obvious problems, along with plenty of dangers and challenges. So why would we still let Pinduoduo enter the uncontrollable capital markets so early? I think there are a few reasons:

We believe Pinduoduo's line of business inherently carries strong social character, so it will inevitably go public in the end, and all of its growth should also be shared with the public.

We believe Pinduoduo has enormous potential; whether it goes public in 3 years, 5 years, or a longer time from now makes no real difference. On the contrary, under public oversight, we can grow better and stronger.

We hope Pinduoduo will be a public institution. It survives by creating value for the broadest base of users. It should not be a tool for showcasing any one individual's ability, nor should it carry too much of any individual's personal color.

At the same time, as an independent public institution, it should demonstrate its own unique social value, organizational structure, and culture as an institution, and follow its own unique destiny, continuing to evolve without end.

As the founder, let me describe what I observe and imagine Pinduoduo to be, so you can more concretely understand what kind of company you're about to invest in.

**What kind of company is Pinduoduo**

Pinduoduo is dedicated to building a new "space" where the virtual internet world and the real world merge, where users can buy what they want at the best possible price, while also finding a lot of joy along the way.

Pinduoduo is a platform, and also an interdependent community made up of countless users, merchants, platform managers/operators, and platform infrastructure and service providers, all together.

Within it, every participant changes as other parties change, grows as they grow. The scale of value also shifts, among cost-effectiveness, efficiency, and the emotional and spiritual sense of gain, as all the different parties change.

The foundation of Pinduoduo's existence is creating value for users. I hope that if our team ever wakes up in the night feeling uneasy, it will never be because of stock-price fluctuations, but only because we don't understand how consumers' real needs are changing, or because consumers are dissatisfied with us, or worse, have abandoned us.

Pinduoduo will be a company brave enough to invest in the future, one grounded in the long term. It may sometimes look aggressive, and sometimes seem overly conservative, but in fact it always follows one simple, consistent logic: focusing on this institution's intrinsic value.

**Pinduoduo's values**

The core of Pinduoduo's values is "Ben Fen." This word is hard to translate into English — it roughly means "keeping to one's proper duty," and for us it carries several layers of meaning:

Be honest, and become a trustworthy person

Do your own job, no matter what others are doing

Shut out outside pressure, return to your original intent, and focus on doing what you're supposed to do well

Don't take advantage of others, even when you can

When a problem arises, first look to yourself for the cause

For Pinduoduo's management, Ben Fen means focusing on creating value for consumers. We may not always be understood, but we always act in good faith, and never do evil.

**Looking ahead**

Over the past three years, Pinduoduo has built and popularized an entirely new shopping concept and experience — "Pin" [team-buying/pooling together]. We can reasonably expect that "Pin" will evolve into many different versions. We also look forward to opening up, in the future, completely different user scenarios, just as we once pioneered "Pin."

If we close our eyes and imagine the next stage of Pinduoduo, you can picture it as a multidimensional space that tightly merges the virtual internet world with the real world.

It will be a combination of "Costco" and "Disneyland" (that is, integrating high-cost-performance products with entertainment), driven by a distributed intelligent recommendation network — rather than the currently fashionable centralized, super-brain-style AI system.

It won't just efficiently match information; it will constantly model the collective mood of the crowd throughout the entire space, and keep trying to adjust the whole space so that the group's overall experience becomes happier.

In the process of continually meeting user needs, Pinduoduo is committed to improving the efficiency and quality of the supply chain. Agricultural produce is a good example of this.

Constrained by population and land conditions, China's per-capita arable land is relatively small — unlike the US, where there are large-scale farms everywhere and agricultural production, transport, and distribution have been highly industrialized.

We've found that "Pin" can quickly aggregate consumer demand, achieving large-scale many-to-many matching, and then, by using China's low-cost logistics network, cut out layer after layer of middlemen, sending agricultural produce directly from the farm to the consumer's hands.

Beyond improving the consumer experience, this approach also achieves semi-customized batch processing of small-scale agricultural output of varying quality, type, and quantity.

It lowers the unnecessary cost of consuming agricultural produce, and also makes small-scale custom service possible. The social impact and social value of this model far exceed the achievements of our business itself, or the outside world's valuation of the company.

We're delighted by the small impact we've already achieved today, and we also believe agricultural produce is only the beginning of this trend.

Responding to this in an exclusive interview with CCTV Finance, Pinduoduo founder Huang Zheng said that, currently, Pinduoduo is indeed quite differentiated from JD — JD's biggest achievements have mainly come in the electronics category, but Pinduoduo operates in a different space, with a huge market opportunity; currently, the infrastructure for many-to-many matching, grouping people by kind, is still very underdeveloped, and if JD and Alibaba both entered this space, it would be very beneficial for pushing the whole industry forward. Huang Zheng also said that, as e-commerce predecessors, Alibaba and JD are of course companies Pinduoduo should learn a great deal from.

**"I Want to Build a Disneyland, But Right Now I Only Have Two Carousel Horses"**

Pinduoduo's slogan is "More Savings, More Fun," hoping to give users a rich variety of goods while also bringing consumers more joy. While delivering high cost-effectiveness on products, it also hopes to bring more elements of happiness into the shopping process; as people's standard of living rises, the public isn't just chasing value for money — they also want life to be more enjoyable.

In the exclusive CCTV Finance interview, Pinduoduo founder Huang Zheng said Pinduoduo is still at the early stage of "more savings," but the direction they're working toward is to blend savings and happiness together.

Looking at Pinduoduo's current interactive model, whether it's "team-buying" or its marketing approach, it's very different from traditional e-commerce. Some people feel it isn't especially fun; others feel there's a bit of fun to it. Huang Zheng said of this: "It's a gradual process — my goal, for example, is to build a Disneyland, but the current level might not be there yet; so far, I've only built two carousel horses."

**Is Pinduoduo Cheap Enough? Huang Zheng: It Can Get Even Cheaper!**

When buying products, consumers always tend to choose the one with the best value for money. Many people think Pinduoduo's users are a group chasing the absolute lowest price, but Huang Zheng denied this view.

In the CCTV Finance exclusive interview, he said he doesn't think Pinduoduo's current cost-effectiveness is high enough yet — there's still room to go further from where it stands now.

In this new era, the price, variety, and personalization of all goods should have a reasonable degree of balance, and there should be new models suited to the growth of new Chinese brands. Huang Zheng believes that, especially in the agricultural produce sector, using the old industrialized distribution channels to sell farm products no longer fits — China's land is fragmented, and under these conditions, new brands will gradually rise.

**"Social E-commerce" Is a False Concept — Low Price Isn't the Only Path to Growth**

Whether it's combining social features with e-commerce, or focusing on price, Pinduoduo wasn't the first to do either. So why, when most people thought the e-commerce landscape had already stabilized, did Pinduoduo rise so rapidly?

Huang Zheng believes "social e-commerce" is itself a false concept — you can't lump it all together under that label, and "low price" is also a relative concept; what they actually emphasize more is high cost-effectiveness. He gave an example: America's Costco — many Chinese companies admire this business model — where individual products have very high cost-effectiveness, and yet it's also a store that sells a lot of diamonds.

This is, in effect, e-commerce making use of the internet's development to aggregate homogeneous demand. Rather than calling it social e-commerce, it's better described as e-commerce organized by grouping like-minded people together — in the past, people had to go find things; now, similar people are grouped together, quickly generating demand at scale, and in a short period of time, creating a great opportunity to optimize the supply chain, giving individual products much higher cost-effectiveness.

## 17. Rising Against the Cold Winter: Unicorn Pinduoduo CEO Huang Zheng's Business Philosophy

The 2016 China Entrepreneur Leaders Annual Meeting recently concluded in Beijing. At the meeting, entrepreneurs from all walks of life gathered to jointly decode China's future business landscape. During a video debate at the conference titled "Who Leads Among University-Based Business Networks," Huang Zheng — representing the Zhejiang University business network, and now CEO of Pinhaohuo/Pinduoduo, currently the largest social e-commerce player in China — went head-to-head with an entrepreneur from Wuhan University. When the topic turned to Zhejiang University, Huang Zheng proudly said he believes that in 50 years, Zhejiang University will become a university like Stanford.

**First Meeting Huang Zheng: Grateful for the Restlessness and Luck That Passed Through Me**

Luck matters a great deal to every entrepreneur. When talking about his own unusual pre-entrepreneurship experience, Huang Zheng wore a perfectly natural expression.

After the transition from elementary to middle school, he never again went through the major exams typical of the Chinese education path — he was smoothly admitted, without examination, into Zhejiang University's Chu Kochen Honors College, the "club" for Zhejiang University's top students. He then went on to join the lab of Academician Pan Yunhe, then president of Zhejiang University.

After finishing his undergraduate degree, he applied to and was accepted at a top American university for further study. Finally, showing real foresight, he chose to join Google, a company that hadn't yet taken off. Later Google went public, and Huang Zheng was astonished to discover just how valuable the stock he was holding had become.

Huang Zheng admits candidly that if you can call him lucky, it's because, step by step along the way, he met the right people at the right time — people who gave him the best advice, letting him make the most correct choices. And, to a great extent, it's precisely because of his own ambition and "restlessness" that he went through, one after another, entrepreneurial ventures in retail, e-commerce services, and gaming — each one more successful than the last.

**The Fourth Venture: One Small Step, One Giant Leap**

Huang Zheng's original conception for Pinduoduo and Pinhaohuo came from what could be described as a "rational flash of inspiration" during his travels: in the first ten years of the new century, amid the wave of international internet entrepreneurship and innovation, Google and Facebook were undoubtedly the two most successful internet companies, and they also represented two different business models. The former was a search-type product, making money through ads, relying on massive numbers of visits from users, selling traffic to merchants for profit.

Currently, the e-commerce platform we're most familiar with, Taobao, is essentially similar to Google in nature. Huang Zheng wondered — could there also be an e-commerce platform that operates on a principle similar to Facebook's? On Facebook, users exist as independent individuals, each with countless labels. But what kind of chemical reaction would occur if people with the same labels were gathered together? Everything was still hazy, but Huang Zheng sensed this was a huge opportunity.

In Huang Zheng's view, search-based e-commerce platforms like Taobao emphasize efficiency more — the user has a clear purchasing goal, aiming to satisfy their own need in the most convenient, quickest way. What Pinduoduo wanted to build, instead, was something dedicated to transplanting offline shopping scenarios from daily life onto the internet, letting users get the greatest convenience and value with the least amount of traffic.

After that, Huang Zheng conducted extensive research and investigation, and even pulled technical staff out of his gaming team to provide support. In the end, he decided to weave the concept of communication and sharing into Pinduoduo's e-commerce operations. A complete Pinduoduo order is generated like this: the user launches a team-buying invitation, and after successfully forming a group with friends, family, coworkers, and others, they're able to buy quality goods at a lower price.

Team-buying means orders flood into participating merchants in large numbers and quickly, letting Pinduoduo negotiate discounts directly with suppliers or merchants, cutting out the middlemen, with a clear price advantage.

Discussing Pinduoduo's business model, Huang Zheng said you could think of team-buying as a group-buy, or you could think of it as several people getting together face-to-face to pool their money and haggle with a merchant. Put on the ground in the physical world, this behavior isn't really any kind of innovation. I just abstracted out this act of pooling together and haggling, and turned it into an online product.

That way, on this platform, you'll always run into someone with tastes similar to yours — if everyone happens to like the same flavor of orange, then teaming up together lets you enjoy a better price. Our fruit team-ups are basically formed by 2 people — if you're in Beijing wanting to team up with someone to buy oranges, open the storefront interface, and you'll see a stranger far away in Xinjiang, online right now, waiting to team up with you to buy oranges — both efficiency and success rate are extremely high.

Asked whether a 2-person team-up was too small a group, and whether it would do much to promote the platform, Huang Zheng candidly said Pinduoduo's team-buying concept is completely different from what others were doing with "group buying." They treated team-buying as a marketing tool — forming a group, bringing in a few new users, then having those users bring in even more new users, continuing the conversion of users along with cross-selling.

And moreover, groups of 6 or even 10 people were often, in many cases, already past their deadline before the group had even formed. So they were mainly relying on recommending the product to users' own circle of contacts, rather than using shared tastes or labels as the basis for grouping. So a lot of people, having tried it once or twice, didn't want to use it again, because it's genuinely hard to successfully form a group among your friends repeatedly.

The latest data shows that, from Pinduoduo's launch in September 2015 up through now, Pinduoduo's user base has already exceeded 100 million, with monthly GMV exceeding 2 billion yuan, and average daily orders passing 2 million. Pinhaohuo became one of the few e-commerce companies to achieve breakeven in the fresh produce category. Pinduoduo secured a $110 million Series B round of financing from investors including Tencent, GGV Capital, and IDG, becoming, since 2015, the e-commerce platform with the largest Series B financing amid the capital winter. Judged by these eye-catching results, Huang Zheng's fourth venture was undoubtedly a success too. As he himself put it, this was just one small step he took on his own entrepreneurial path, yet it unexpectedly became one giant leap for China's social e-commerce industry.

**A Promising Future, and a Long Road Ahead**

In 2016, Pinduoduo and Pinhaohuo sprinted forward, successively winning a series of heavyweight awards, including Tencent's "2016 Rapid Growth Award — Most Popular Application," the "2016 Business Model Innovation Award," the "2016 Analysys Star Award," "2016's Most Promising Growth Enterprise in China," and the 2016 Chuangyeheimahui "China's Most Promising Startup." These awards were enough to prove that their model was being increasingly recognized by the industry.

Looking to the future, Huang Zheng said that as one of the largest-volume platforms in this space, Pinduoduo and Pinhaohuo, while strengthening themselves, will also push forward the development of infrastructure for the entire industry, rather than only optimizing their own front end. The most important things on the back end — inventory, logistics — all need to be thought through and optimized to their best, bringing the whole industry forward together; there's a long road ahead, and a heavy responsibility to bear.

Finally, on the subject of entrepreneurship, Huang Zheng said outright that this might be his last venture. But for him, "starting a business is a lot like playing golf — both are contests between you and yourself. The scene you face each time may be different, but the basic motion of the swing never changes. What's needed is to keep an ordinary, steady mind, and make that motion more standard, more precise."

## 18. Pinduoduo CEO Huang Zheng: A Third of Our Employees Are Fighting Counterfeits

Huang Zheng revealed that throughout his entrepreneurial journey, Duan Yongping has continually drilled into him the importance of doing the right things first, and only then doing things right.

Where there's a spotlight, there's a shadow. As a fledgling social e-commerce platform, Pinduoduo has been dancing, so to speak, in shackles. The more praise Pinduoduo receives, the louder the controversy grows.

From the team's founding in 2015 to securing $110 million in Series B financing in 2016, this straight-line growth led the outside world to view Pinduoduo as the next Taobao, one with the potential to disrupt BAT. But this overly rapid growth also brought Pinduoduo a series of accusations — fake order volume, counterfeit sales, and more.

On April 15, 2017, founder Huang Zheng spoke candidly at a New Economy 100 CEO summit about the constant fight against people gaming order volumes. Huang Zheng confided: "Being threatened constantly, I just wanted to find somewhere to vent, to get it off my chest."

**Ran a Top-Two Outsourced Operations Business in Maternal-Infant and Food Categories**

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": In just two years, Pinduoduo's growth has already outpaced the normal speed of an e-commerce platform — what was the decisive factor behind that?

Huang Zheng: I think the core of it is innovation at the model level — this is a model that didn't exist before, one that combined social traffic and e-commerce conversion very effectively. Our team has experience in both gaming and e-commerce, and in the subsequent operational iteration, combining the two let us grow very fast. Previous e-commerce models all tried to carve out a "little Taobao" from within Taobao, whereas Pinduoduo is doing e-commerce along an entirely different dimension — there's no attempt to build a little Taobao here; it's about meeting a different set of scenario-based needs.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": Had you done e-commerce before?

Huang Zheng: Before gaming, we ran an e-commerce outsourced-operations company, one of the top two outsourced operators in the maternal-infant and food categories — doing work for brands like Nestlé, Wyeth, and Kao diapers. Even today that company remains one of the top two in its industry, with about 1,500 employees now.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": Do you have a good understanding of products — for example, what price range is more suited to the team-buying model?

Huang Zheng: At first we thought food would be the best fit, but later we found almost every category worked — nothing seemed unsuited to it.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": No breakout hit category?

Huang Zheng: There's no particular category that's the breakout hit — only individual products that become hits, like the iPhone. Actually, similar to other e-commerce platforms, Pinduoduo is also a platform catering largely to mass-market demand.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": Every platform has its own characteristics.

Huang Zheng: Our current user base is quite large — nearly 150 million, getting close to 200 million. For us, there isn't really an issue of user segmentation as such — the platform tries, within the large mass of users, to group them — for example, people on the East Third Ring Road, or people in Chaohu, Anhui — everyone's needs are different.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": On this platform, do you have any thoughts on user management?

Huang Zheng: We're moving fairly quickly to tag different users with different labels, using the team-buying relationship to bring you, your friends, and your friends' friends together, so that people with similar needs can be grouped together.

**A Different Shopping Scenario from Taobao**

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": Is there a difference between Pinduoduo's users and Taobao's users?

Huang Zheng: Actually, because Taobao is so dominant, it has basically captured almost every user in China, so of course all of our users are Taobao users too — just like every WeChat user is also a QQ user.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": When people compare you with Taobao, how do you look at that?

Huang Zheng: Taobao is primarily goal-oriented search; but actually, any given person has different scenarios — for instance, one scenario is that you're cooking at home on the weekend, and you know you need to go buy rice, soy sauce, and so on. But there's another scenario where you're bored, with nothing to do, so you grab a close friend and go wander around a Joy City or Wanda Plaza mall. What I mean is, for the very same person, Taobao and Pinduoduo represent two completely different scenarios — we're doing more of the non-goal-oriented kind, to some extent trying to make shopping itself a source of happiness.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": So it's social e-commerce centered on acquaintance relationships?

Huang Zheng: Pinduoduo's starting point was centered on acquaintance relationships, but as it developed, it gradually spread from acquaintances to acquaintances-of-acquaintances, and then to strangers — so it's a process of gradual propagation.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": What age group are your users mostly?

Huang Zheng: I've given our investors a detailed breakdown — our user base is concentrated mainly among women in their 20s to 40s.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": What value does the social relationship formed through team-buying bring to users?

Huang Zheng: The core of social e-commerce is e-commerce, not "social."

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": So isn't your platform's user growth coming from these social relationships?

Huang Zheng: Actually it still comes from the products — from their high cost-effectiveness.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": Do you shop on Pinduoduo yourself?

Huang Zheng: I buy milk, fruit, that sort of thing.

**A Team of 700, With a Third Dedicated to Fighting Fake Orders**

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": How do you control fake order volume?

Huang Zheng: We've worked with the police before to arrest two people — they were actually Peking University graduates, using software to fake orders.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": What kind of controls do you use?

Huang Zheng: It's really a matter of detection versus counter-detection — we build software to detect whether it's a bot, and for those who keep maliciously faking orders even after being banned, all we can do is work with the police to catch them.

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": We've found some hundred-yuan LV and Prada items on the platform — what do you think about that?

Huang Zheng: I haven't searched for that myself — if such items exist, it depends what the item actually is. If it's genuinely LV, that's not allowed, and the platform will take it down. If there are sales figures listed, we'll look at how many were sold. It's not that we're trying to shirk responsibility, but honestly, we can't fully control it — for a platform, it's very hard to set up a keyword list, because any item bearing that brand name that turns out different from everyone else's raises the question of where you draw the line. What if it's actually a "little raccoon" [a homophone/wordplay reference implying a knockoff or parody brand]?

Beijing Time "Cai Fang": How many people are working on this right now?

**The Person Who Influenced Me Most Is Duan Yongping**

When it comes to any specific business form — how to do logistics reliably, how to do gaming reliably — Ding Lei and Wang Wei certainly understand that better than he does.

## 19. A Conversation With Pinduoduo's Huang Zheng: They're Building Empires, Fighting Over Territory — I'm Playing a Different Game

On Taobao, an all-powerful marketplace, an item might sell for 29 yuan; on Pinduoduo, the same item might sell for 19 yuan — and if you can find five friends to buy it together with you, you might get it for 9.9 yuan, shipping included. Relying early on for a large volume of such 9.9-yuan-with-free-shipping products, Pinduoduo built itself into China's third-largest e-commerce platform by GMV, exceeding 100 billion yuan and trailing only Alibaba and JD. And it took only a bit over two years to do all this, during what people call the second half of the internet era, against a backdrop of ever more expensive traffic.

This company arose in near-total silence — people in first-tier cities may not even have heard of it — and by the time it appeared on people's radar, it was already enormous in scale. This growth trajectory isn't hard to compare to Kuaishou and Toutiao back in their day.

The traffic-driven playbook is today's mainstream internet logic; empire-building and territorial competition are today's central themes. Most of the breakout startups are, directly or indirectly, products of the tug-of-war between Alibaba and Tencent. For a startup, under China's current internet competitive environment, every seat at the table is already taken. If you still try to compete using traditional thinking, you'll never break through the encirclement of the giants.

**On Tencent**

"I Don't Think We're Part of the 'Tencent Camp'"

Caijing: Some believe Pinduoduo has already replaced JD as Tencent's primary strategic proxy in the fight against Alibaba.

Huang Zheng: Tencent supporting us? Where would that show up?

Caijing: Many of Pinduoduo's tactics are things WeChat has explicitly and repeatedly banned — for instance, inducing clicks, or directly mentioning a user's WeChat name — yet you still survive safely within the WeChat ecosystem. Is this tacit approval from Tencent?

Huang Zheng: I don't think Tencent has supported Pinduoduo — we've been blocked many times too.

Caijing: It's said Pinduoduo has been blocked by WeChat 1,000 times — what did you do to get blocked that many times?

Huang Zheng: 1,000 is an exaggeration. Didi, Alibaba, and JD have all been blocked many times too — I can't say we've done the best job, but we're certainly not the worst either. In fact, from a platform-governance standpoint, as the highest-transaction-volume organization within Tencent's ecosystem, WeChat's controls on us are actually stricter.

WeChat's policies are constantly changing — often you don't know until you try; and second, we believe some practices haven't actually caused much harm to consumers — for instance, in scenarios like a delivery notification, if you don't mention the user's name, you end up with errors. What really matters is whether it crosses the line from the user's perspective.

Caijing: In terms of judging where that line is, has Pinduoduo made good use of loopholes and gray areas in WeChat's public platform mechanisms?

Huang Zheng: If someone believes we rose by exploiting the rules, they'd also understand the rules well enough themselves — so why didn't they rise the same way?

Caijing: So you deny that Tencent gave Pinduoduo extra support and leniency.

Huang Zheng: Does Pinduoduo have any privileges on WeChat? For example, things like Mogujie or JD's Weixuan doing mini-programs within WeChat's entry points — we simply can't do that at all. Before the New Year, Tencent had us sign a mutual non-poaching agreement — if we want to poach Tencent's people, we can only do so with Tencent's consent. Would Tencent make the same demand of Toutiao?

Recently something happened that left me feeling quite wronged — suddenly a large amount of rumors claiming Pinduoduo was committing fraud spread widely through WeChat groups. I went to WeChat for help and was turned down — WeChat said if it were Alibaba asking, they could handle it. I can understand that, because if WeChat stepped in to help debunk rumors about me, the public would say it's because Tencent is our shareholder. If WeChat helps debunk rumors for Alibaba, that's seen as taking the high road.

Caijing: For Tencent, this probably isn't a big deal.

Huang Zheng: Because if I die, Tencent won't die — Tencent has thousands upon thousands of other "sons."

Caijing: Starting in the second half of 2017, Pinduoduo has been gradually guiding users from WeChat over to its own app. Some media have written that Pinduoduo is "digging into Tencent's turf."

Huang Zheng: We haven't forced the app on anyone — it's mostly users choosing it on their own initiative. Pinduoduo might be the only e-commerce app that doesn't force users to enter a phone number at login — you can just log in directly through WeChat. Right now, orders coming from our app already make up far more than 50% of the total.

Creating a sharing scenario on WeChat was an important reason behind Pinduoduo's early rise — in the early days we had no data, so we came to understand people through people, through you and your friends and what traits you shared. But WeChat isn't the only, ultimate scenario — once we fully understand our users, I can create new scenarios independent of WeChat, through our own platform, using machines instead of "friends" to make judgments, pushing the most suitable products to users. What you see today is just the early stage of Pinduoduo.

Caijing: Many of China's breakout internet startups — Didi, Ele.me, ofo, Mobike — are mostly products of the tug-of-war between Alibaba and Tencent. Is Pinduoduo's growth the same kind of story?

Huang Zheng: Toutiao wasn't — so why can't we be another exception too?

Caijing: The logic here seems obvious — Pinduoduo's existence carries huge strategic significance for Tencent, and Tencent is willing to support Pinduoduo's growth to compete with Alibaba.

Huang Zheng: I don't think Tencent operates on a strong competitive logic — Tencent is more of a businessman's logic; competition is just a byproduct of its commercial ROI calculations. Tencent invested in Pinduoduo not because we could fight Alibaba, but because their investment generates returns. Tencent invested in JD — does that really mean JD can compete with Alibaba? Alibaba will fight JD regardless, even without Tencent's investment.

Caijing: What impact would a direct clash between Tencent and Alibaba in retail have on Pinduoduo?

Huang Zheng: Alibaba and Tencent aren't really competing yet, because both of their shares in offline retail are still very low. Most of today's internet competition is a scramble for traffic — but once you set traffic aside and focus on scenarios instead, you'll find the space is huge.

Caijing: What kind of e-commerce ecosystem does Tencent hope to build?

Huang Zheng: I don't think it has any clear purpose — just as Tencent's investment in Didi doesn't mean it's building a ride-hailing ecosystem.

Caijing: Isn't using ROI alone to gauge Tencent's behavior underestimating Tencent?

Huang Zheng: What's wrong with running a business on the logic of a businessman? Ren Zhengfei once said in an interview, what does it mean for China to be strong? He said we grind tofu, they grow bean sprouts, and put together, that's a strong China.

Caijing: Suppose in 2016 you hadn't accepted Tencent's investment — would Pinduoduo still be today's Pinduoduo?

Huang Zheng: The logic should be — it's because we're today's Pinduoduo that Tencent wanted to invest. Tencent is more like an ordinary financial investor — it's neither especially good to us nor especially bad to us. I also don't think we're part of the "Tencent camp."

Caijing: But your office is full of QQ mascot figurines.

Huang Zheng: Those were all gifts from other people — Tencent didn't send that many.

**On Skepticism**

"Pinduoduo's Core Isn't Being Cheap"

Caijing: Team-buying and low prices are both tools and competitive tactics that have long existed in e-commerce — how did you turn a tactic into a strategy?

Huang Zheng: I once told someone at Tencent that Tencent's e-commerce efforts failed because they understood e-commerce as traffic × conversion rate = GMV. That traffic-based logic can't succeed today — Tencent has the largest traffic base in all of China, and it invested in a whole bunch of vertical e-commerce players; if that logic worked, Tencent would have already succeeded at e-commerce long ago.

After Pinduoduo took off, JD, Vip.com, and Mogujie all experimented with similar models — for them, team-buying was just a tool for creating incremental GMV; but for Pinduoduo, it's about the logic of people — we use team-buying to understand people, use people to recommend goods, and later we'll transition to machines recommending goods. There's almost no search function in the Pinduoduo app, and no shopping cart — you could imagine Pinduoduo as Toutiao's information feed swapped out for a feed of products.

So in the early days, everyone looked the same — low prices and team-buying — but our starting point was different, our direction was different, and once we grew up, we turned out different too.

Caijing: One investor summed it up by saying Pinduoduo succeeded by capturing the third wave of mobile internet demographic dividends — reaching people who had WeChat but not Taobao.

Huang Zheng: Only people inside Beijing's Fifth Ring Road would call this a "lower-tier" demographic. What we focus on is the broadest base of ordinary Chinese people — the same reason behind Kuaishou's and Toutiao's growth. It's just like going to Shenzhen 30 years ago — you could make money doing almost anything there, not because of anything special, but simply because you'd chosen the right direction.

Caijing: A JD executive once commented that Pinduoduo's model is simple and crude, and that its low-price strategy attracts only price-sensitive customers — meaning higher-end demographics may perhaps never shop there.

Huang Zheng: What we attract is a demographic pursuing high cost-effectiveness — they might buy a Hermès bag, and also buy a case of mangoes for 9.9 yuan — that has nothing to do with their purchasing power. Traditional companies are the ones who divide people into first-, second-, and third-tier — Pinduoduo satisfies the many different facets of a single person. It's just that we're still doing a poor job of it — we don't yet have the ability to fully satisfy a demographic like you, inside the Fifth Ring Road.

Caijing: What you understand as Pinduoduo is "high cost-effectiveness," but what many people see is "cheap and low quality" — Pinduoduo's complaint rate has reportedly ranked first in the industry, as high as 13.12%.

Huang Zheng: We also sell the iPhone X — low price is just a phase-appropriate way of acquiring users. Pinduoduo has a deeper understanding of cost-effectiveness than most platforms — always staying just beyond the consumer's expectations. Our core isn't "cheap" — it's satisfying the user's feeling of having gotten a good deal.

For example, Pinduoduo's very first product was a one-yuan bag of Lay's potato chips, selling ten thousand bags in a day. As long as half the chips were still edible, the customer would feel it was worth it. But conversely — even at just one yuan — if the chips arrived completely crushed, the consumer would feel they hadn't gotten a good deal at all, and might even curse you out.

Caijing: In January, Taobao published a white paper claiming counterfeits are shifting away from Taobao toward WeChat-based sellers and Pinduoduo — is that true?

Huang Zheng: Speaking from an engineering perspective, I find that report pretty funny. I don't think they can actually know that — how could they possibly know that?

Caijing: One of the best-selling products on Pinduoduo is a bottle of "male enhancement medicine" priced at 27.8 yuan, with a total of 4.72 million units sold. Do you think this medicine could possibly be genuine?

Huang Zheng: First, any medicine or health supplement sold on Pinduoduo will carry state-certified labeling. Second, the margins on health supplements are outrageously high to begin with, same as with facial masks — do you think a 200-yuan facial mask actually works?

Caijing: Extremely low prices require extremely low costs — how do extremely low costs guarantee quality and repeat purchases?

Huang Zheng: I admit that Pinduoduo's current control over product quality and service quality is still quite rudimentary. We're upgrading the supply chain and cracking down on counterfeits to raise product quality. There's probably no platform in all of China working harder than us at fighting counterfeits — over the past year, our repeat-purchase rate has doubled, and average order value has risen from around ten-plus yuan in the early days to forty or fifty yuan now.

Beyond meeting people's basic material needs, we've also put a lot of product design and operational work into satisfying different spiritual-level consumption needs — impulse consumption, rational consumption, cathartic consumption. This is also why, even when facing homogeneous competition, Pinduoduo can still keep drawing consumers back.

Caijing: For Pinduoduo, is satisfying people's spiritual needs just about lottery draws and grabbing red envelopes?

Huang Zheng: Lottery draws and red envelopes are the simplest forms of entertainment. It's a pretty crude approach, but we really are trying to blend consumption and entertainment together.

Caijing: Some believe Pinduoduo isn't consumption upgrading, but consumption downgrading.

Huang Zheng: Consumption upgrading isn't about letting people in Shanghai live like people in Paris — it's about letting people in Anqing, Anhui, have paper towels in their kitchen and good fruit to eat.

**On Core Competitiveness**

"Traffic Logic Is Last Era's Logic"

Caijing: As a fast-growing startup, when will Pinduoduo's accumulated management problems reach a breaking point?

Huang Zheng: Running a company doesn't have an obvious adolescence the way children do — adolescence has an obvious acceleration at the start and an obvious slowdown at the end. But a company is a gradual process of growing up. That means problems don't suddenly erupt — problems are erupting every single day.

Right now, the biggest problem is that our systems can't keep up with the pace of scale. Taobao has already established basic rules for platform governance — for instance, merchant credit systems — and we can borrow from those. But in some areas it hasn't done well either — for instance, logistics for agricultural produce — and those we need to explore ourselves.

Caijing: What's the most thorny management issue right now?

Huang Zheng: How to reduce human interference in the rules. Having people manually select and recommend products inevitably leads to corruption — in our first year of operation, an operations staff member was sent to prison for corruption, and at the time I fired a third of our operations staff. In the future, Pinduoduo will move from human product selection entirely to algorithm- and machine-driven selection.

Caijing: Pinduoduo is currently very "light" — you don't control goods, and you don't have your own warehousing and logistics system. Will you get "heavier" in the future?

Huang Zheng: We won't do procurement-and-sales ourselves, and we won't touch logistics and delivery. Alibaba has already done that really well — why would you do it too? A lot of companies try to do everything themselves because they don't trust other people.

Caijing: What's your strategic priority this year?

Huang Zheng: Upgrading the supply chain will be our strategic priority for a long time to come. Pinduoduo's ultimate model is to enable upstream batch, customized production, but right now our investment in upstream, and our ability to empower the whole industry chain, are both far too weak.

Caijing: Pinduoduo's operations staff say the only KPI they're measured on is GMV. How can a company under such enormous growth pressure balance that with a medium- and long-term strategy at the same time?

Huang Zheng: Our KPIs prioritize retention and repeat purchase first, GMV second. In fact, right now, we don't actually want to grow — I want to bring our GMV growth rate down.

Caijing: But you're spending a lot on advertising.

Huang Zheng: We advertise to tell consumers we're not scammers.

Caijing: The strategy and vision you describe seems different from how Pinduoduo employees understand the company — in their eyes, the company is still all about traffic. Why is there this disconnect?

Huang Zheng: These employees were educated for many years under the old traffic-based mindset — the company is only a bit over two years old, and we haven't yet done a great job of unifying my thinking with our employees', but we're trying to push it through from the top down. Right now I'm on one end, society and the media are on the traffic-driven end, and employees are probably somewhere in the middle.

Caijing: Pinduoduo already has 300 million users — how much longer will the traffic dividend last?

Huang Zheng: The entire population of China should be our users — I think we'll keep growing fast for the next 2 to 3 years.

Caijing: Are you spending money to buy traffic right now?

Huang Zheng: Compared to Toutiao, it's far, far less.

Caijing: How does 9.9-yuan-with-free-shipping build a business model?

Huang Zheng: Alibaba has already made this model work very well — it's mostly ads and commission cuts, but right now we only take a cut on payment processing fees.

Caijing: Pinduoduo's product categories are mostly fruit and fast-moving consumer goods — people buying fruit tend to think "I spent 10 yuan on half a box of rotten and half a box of good fruit, and I still came out ahead." If Pinduoduo wants to expand into all categories in the future, you'll face issues around brand upgrading, direction, and growth rate. How will you solve these?

Huang Zheng: Expanding into all categories is still the old traffic logic, and so is "upgrading" — upgrading is a top-down view from inside the Fifth Ring Road. I don't think Pinduoduo needs to upgrade its brand or expand into all categories — that's the wrong approach; what we're always trying to do is match — letting the right person buy the right thing in the right scenario.

Caijing: Pinduoduo grew up relying on China's broadest population base — what kind of inherent weaknesses will a company like that have?

Huang Zheng: One, you'll go through a lot of public opinion storms; two, a company like ours inevitably has to shoulder more social responsibility — our reach is even wider than Toutiao's.

The recent rumors about Pinduoduo committing fraud have made me quite anxious — if I were WeChat, or a government department, what should I do? A lot of entrepreneurs come out to do PR themselves — that's a kind of self-sacrifice. Can our generation of new entrepreneurs avoid relying on that approach? An entrepreneur will eventually grow old, but the company has to keep living on — it should rely on a mechanism with its own capacity to respond, rather than on one person.

Caijing: What's Pinduoduo's mission and values?

Huang Zheng: Our mission is "more savings, more fun" — letting consumers get more, more affordable things, and be happier along the way. Our values are Ben Fen.

Caijing: How does a company whose values are Ben Fen end up getting blocked by WeChat 1,000 times?

Huang Zheng: Ben Fen means doing what you're supposed to do in your own position. We want consumers to run more marketing activities — for a commercial organization, that's its proper duty. What's left is really about where to draw the line on how far marketing outreach should go.

Caijing: When JD was valued at $20 billion, it already had a clear core competitive advantage — its logistics moat (the user experience), full-category expansion, and having gathered the highest-quality users in first- and second-tier cities. What's Pinduoduo's core competitive advantage?

Huang Zheng: I think JD's core advantage is that courier companies can't do what e-commerce does, and e-commerce companies don't want to do delivery. Our core advantage is something people inside the Fifth Ring Road just can't understand.

**On Competition**

"They're Fighting Over Territory — What I Want Is a Different Plane"

Caijing: One of your peers said he believes Pinduoduo could take at least 40% of Taobao's order volume. Do you think that's an overestimate or an underestimate of Pinduoduo?

Huang Zheng: We're competing with Taobao on a different plane — we're both fighting for the same batch of users, but in different scenarios; being on a different plane lets us grow faster, so it's not a question of taking away some amount of Taobao's orders. Just as when Facebook grew, Google fought Facebook, but in the long run the two coexist — Facebook's rapid growth didn't hurt Google's growth, because these are two completely different scenarios.

Caijing: Wang Xing has said something similar — competing in the same direction is "jing" [race], competing head-on is "zheng" [fight] — competition isn't about knocking someone down.

Huang Zheng: What Wang Xing describes is the boundary of his empire — moving toward each other, but never saying where he'll stop. Didi and Meituan grew up in a fiercely competitive environment, but Pinduoduo's growth environment is different — we found a new commercial breakthrough in a field that looked saturated, with seemingly no opportunity left, so we don't need to waste huge resources on excessive competition.

Alibaba versus JD, Didi versus Meituan — these are empire-style competitions, with clear territorial boundaries. But I don't think our generation's thinking should work that way. Pinduoduo and Taobao are more like two different dimensions gradually merging together — Pinduoduo uses Alipay, and uses Cainiao too.

Caijing: Taobao has already launched Taobao Special Edition to counter Pinduoduo.

Huang Zheng: We see that as a compliment to us.

Caijing: An Alibaba executive commented that Pinduoduo sees itself as the second Alibaba, but they see Pinduoduo as nothing more than a low-spec version of Juhuasuan.

Huang Zheng: I haven't studied Juhuasuan. We don't want to be the second Alibaba — Pinduoduo's very existence is itself a model, and we're still in the early stage of pioneering that model. You can say I'm low-brow, say I'm crude, but you can't ignore me.

Caijing: Over the past couple of years, Taobao has been pushing a reform toward personalized recommendations, Toutiao-style distribution — that's highly overlapping with Pinduoduo's strategy. So where does the uniqueness Pinduoduo keeps emphasizing actually show up?

Huang Zheng: Pinduoduo's model is fundamentally different from Taobao's. Taobao runs on traffic logic, centered on search — users have to go find products themselves, so it needs a massive SKU count to satisfy long-tail demand. Pinduoduo represents matching — recommending products to consumers, with a limited SKU count, but needing to satisfy structural richness.

Taobao has always advocated C2B but has never gotten it off the ground, because Taobao's personalized-recommendation approach amounts to personalized search, but search itself is inherently long-tail, making reverse customization very hard to achieve. Pinduoduo, on the other hand, concentrates massive traffic onto a limited set of products, and once it has scale, it can do reverse customization, dramatically reducing costs. That's also the difference between Walmart and Costco — Pinduoduo's ultimate positioning is to be, for different demographics, a different kind of Costco.

Caijing: You seem to be underestimating your competitor. Right now, traffic from Taobao search accounts for less than 50%, and is getting lower and lower — recommendations, livestreaming, content, and activities make up the majority.

Huang Zheng: Taobao's direction is right. But as one of the most successful companies of the previous era, when it steps into a new era, it carries baggage. Its main body is still search — no matter what reform it undertakes, it inevitably means weakening search. But at Pinduoduo, search is only a very minor scenario — it's not even on the home page. This is like Baidu wanting to do what Toutiao does — they both face obstacles created by their own past success.

It's like — I'm Shenzhen, they're Shanghai. Shanghai used to be far more impressive than Shenzhen, and maybe it still is, but I'm young, I have no baggage.

Caijing: Some have said that whatever problems Taobao has run into before (counterfeits, the traffic dividend running out, government regulation), Pinduoduo will run into too.

Huang Zheng: In some respects our challenges are smaller than theirs — for that, we should thank Alibaba for the enormous contribution it made to China's e-commerce industry over the past decade; delivery, payments, and e-commerce infrastructure — we've all benefited from Alibaba.

Caijing: Are there problems Taobao won't run into, but you will?

Huang Zheng: First, being questioned as a latecomer. It's like a table full of people already eating — when Taobao rose up, the table was still empty, but by the time Pinduoduo rose up, there were already 5 people at the table; you're insisting on squeezing in from under the table to eat, but the people at the table don't want you to come up, and the people under the table don't want you to go up either — I'm bound to take hits from both directions. Second, because our growth speed is so fast, we're going to face, in a short period of time, all the problems Taobao took ten years to face.

Caijing: Why not start a new table? Or find a table with fewer people?

Huang Zheng: In China's current market competitive environment, every table, everywhere, is full.

I really like Singapore, and I've studied it deeply too. Singapore itself is a lot like a company — achieving national prosperity in a very poor surrounding environment. And precisely because it's small, it created a model where only a small portion of its assets are held domestically, with most of its assets distributed across China, the US, and elsewhere. Singapore is both a small country, and also an entity fused into every other major power.

Even if you're really impressive, and manage to build a little Alibaba, so what? From the standpoint of making your presence felt, the world won't remember you for that.

Caijing: If you replace Alibaba, people will remember you.

Huang Zheng: Even then, Alibaba was still the first one to make Alibaba.

Caijing: If Alibaba wanted to invest in you, would you accept?

Huang Zheng: That might be limited by our existing investment terms.

Caijing: It's said Pinduoduo has consistently refused to communicate with Meituan — are you considering doing service e-commerce, or worried they might enter the physical-goods team-buying space?

Huang Zheng: It's not that we refuse to talk with them — I just don't run in those circles, and I don't want to talk with other people either. Pinduoduo has no plans right now to do service e-commerce, because physical-goods e-commerce is already big enough.

Caijing: Pinduoduo can currently push products — in the future you could also push movie tickets, similar to Toutiao's logic of images-and-text first, then video.

Huang Zheng: Service e-commerce is traffic-based thinking — pouring in traffic, then consuming that traffic through different services. If we're still using a traffic-based view to do things in the post-traffic era, we'll surely fail.

Pinduoduo's emergence is precisely because it doesn't use the old way of thinking. Under a people-first mindset, you should first think about what this person needs. What we want to do is merge material consumption and spiritual consumption — spiritual consumption isn't just about service e-commerce, but about using entertainment to let users feel happy on top of their consumption.

Caijing: Toutiao is currently doing an information feed for content — do you think it will do a product feed in the future?

Huang Zheng: Let them do it if they want — can you really stop someone else from doing what you do? But that assumption is a lot like Baidu, back in the day, wanting to do what Taobao does.

Caijing: China's new generation of giants are all exploring their own boundaries. Just as some believe Alibaba shouldn't have left a gap for JD back in the day, Toutiao and Pinduoduo might well enter the same track from different directions — what's wrong with thinking ahead about this?

Huang Zheng: Jack Ma said, in an internal speech many years ago, why not leave a competitor for ourselves? He said the best landscape is Intel and AMD. Society should have multiple species existing, that then merge with each other.

**On Personal Growth**

"Most People Overthink — Most Things Are Actually Obvious, Nothing Much to Think About — You Just Need Common Sense to Judge"

Caijing: There's a rumor going around that you're Duan Yongping's godson.

Huang Zheng: Duan Yongping was introduced to me by Ding Lei. When I was at Zhejiang University, one day a stranger added me on MSN, saying he was Ding Lei, and that he was researching a technical problem — at the time I thought he was a scammer. In 2002, when I went to graduate school in the US, I met Duan Yongping, and after graduating, working at Google, I lived close to his house, and started helping him with some of his investing.

Caijing: Duan Yongping, Ding Lei, Wang Wei, and Sun Tongyu were all your angel investors — back then Pinduoduo didn't even exist yet — what do you think it was that convinced them?

Huang Zheng: Maybe it's because I don't flatter people — I said some things that were a bit interesting. I remember chatting with Wang Wei at the time, and I told him, you guys will never succeed doing e-commerce — have you ever seen a courier company succeed at e-commerce?

Caijing: Did Wang Wei believe you?

Huang Zheng: He didn't accept that he couldn't do it. So what could I do?

Caijing: What insights has Duan Yongping given you on business and life?

Huang Zheng: He said fast is slow, and slow is fast. He said things go better if you approach them with an ordinary heart. He said it's actually very hard for an ordinary person to have an ordinary heart. As for himself, he's someone who keeps improving — he's still learning, and he even reads your articles.

Caijing: Sun Tongyu is one of Alibaba's co-founders — what's the biggest thing he's helped you with?

Huang Zheng: One is values — he's a very good person; the other is that his understanding of platforms is genuinely different — very few people in China have actually run a platform, so that's been helpful. For instance, he believes a platform should think more about the evolution of the ecosystem across different stages, whereas a brand is more about a single segment's proposition for standing out.

Caijing: In 2006, Duan Yongping paid $620,000 for a lunch with Buffett, and took you along to meet him. You were 26 at the time — was that an important day for you?

Huang Zheng: What Buffett said was actually really simple — words even my mother could understand. What that lunch probably meant most to me was making me realize the power of simplicity and common sense — people's thinking is easily contaminated, and when you're judging something, you need to understand the background and the facts; once you understand them, what you need isn't brilliance, but whether you still have the courage, in the face of facts, to judge things with reason and common sense. Common sense is obvious, easy to understand, but the various biases formed by our upbringing and learning, and the pursuit of our own self-interest, blind us to it.

Duan Yongping also taught me a piece of business common sense — price fluctuates around value. Price will always fluctuate, but as long as your value keeps rising, price will eventually converge toward value. This common sense lets you feel at ease focusing on increasing the company's intrinsic value, without being overly concerned about the capital market's price fluctuations.

Caijing: When you run into an unfamiliar field, how do you go about understanding and judging it?

Huang Zheng: I usually take a fairly primitive approach — go experience and feel it myself. Most people overthink — most things are actually obvious, nothing much to think about; you just need to use common sense to judge.

Caijing: Give an example — how do you learn about fields related to e-commerce, like logistics and finance?

Huang Zheng: Why would I need to understand it? Most knowledge is useless — you solve the problem when the problem comes up. Duan Yongping taught me this — have no grand ambition; just do what's in front of you well.

I also don't plan five years or ten years out. I think about one very distant goal and one very immediate step to take. It's like falling in love — is the person you end up with really the one you planned for? Can you really decide who the next girl you meet will be?

Caijing: Do you really think falling in love and setting a company's strategy are the same thing?

Huang Zheng: Fundamentally, yes — running a company is also a journey. If a guy is genuinely ambitious about his career, running a company is a lot like falling in love, because you'll end up loving your company.

Caijing: Your employees say you basically don't manage the business, and rarely hold meetings.

Huang Zheng: You need to think clearly about one thing — are you really that impressive?

Caijing: What do you think a CEO's proper duty is?

Huang Zheng: I'm not a typical CEO — first, I'm responsible for feeding the company values and culture, and for establishing life ideals for employees. Second, I handle the problems that suddenly pop up, ones nobody's run into before, and that no one knows who to assign to.

Caijing: Your employees say that once you've said something, or made a decision, that's final. Do you think most people are pretty dumb, and that your own decisions and judgment are clearly superior to others'?

Huang Zheng: That's very different from how I see myself. In my mind, the vast majority of people, in the vast majority of ways, are better than me — I'm only better than a very few people, in very few respects.

Caijing: Which ones?

Huang Zheng: The ability to shut out external pressure and return to rational thinking rooted in first principles. Sometimes a bit of contrarian thinking too. Back when I did secondary-market investing, I found I naturally wasn't very prone to being fearful when a lot of people were fearful, and was more prone to being pessimistic when the crowd was enthusiastic.

Caijing: What have you been thinking about most lately?

Huang Zheng: Lately I've been thinking about how the cost-effectiveness of material consumption is easy to measure, but how do you measure the cost-effectiveness of spiritual consumption? I still haven't figured that out.

Caijing: Share the biggest regret from your growing-up years.

Huang Zheng: From childhood on I was always first in my class, too goal-oriented — I wasted too much time chasing being first, working hard to be a good student. I only came to that realization pretty late.

Caijing: You'd already achieved financial freedom before starting Pinduoduo — was the driving force behind this venture any different?

Huang Zheng: To put it plainly, doing this company is more about making my presence felt. You make something, and other people think it's pretty impressive, a bit different, and give you a bit of respect for it.

When Steve Jobs returned to Apple, he shot an ad that said: you can praise him, you can insult him, you can say whatever you want about him, but there's one thing you can't do — you can't ignore him.

Caijing: Why care so much about making your presence felt?

Huang Zheng: Because you're not chasing money, and you don't lack money either — what else is there to chase?

Some people pursue building the most respected company, but "most respected" is hard to measure; some pursue building the biggest company, or the most profitable company — easy to measure, but pursuing either of those is actually pretty terrifying. The best company should be incomparable — so unique that others simply can't put it on the same scale as you and compare.

Caijing: Beyond Pinduoduo, what do you most want to do, most want to achieve?

Huang Zheng: I hope in the future to transition into becoming a genuine researcher. There's a great model for this — Benjamin Franklin, printed on the hundred-dollar bill. After the age of 40, he stopped engaging in business, and devoted himself to scientific research, inventing the lightning rod — I think non-profit, wholehearted scientific research work makes a bigger contribution to humanity.

Caijing: You've always said you want to be a unique presence — why, then, did you still run an e-commerce outsourced-operations company and a gaming company in your early ventures?

Huang Zheng: Why was I still doing things that made money? Because I hadn't yet evolved to the point where I could do something entirely non-profit-driven. In the future, I hope to do non-profit research, but running a business without trying to make money — I think that's unethical; you should run things according to business logic, being a proper, dutiful merchant.

Caijing: An investor who knows you well once commented that you're a person without ambition.

Huang Zheng: In terms of running a company — if you're born a mouse, you shouldn't have ambitions to become an elephant, because that would kill the mouse; if you're born an elephant, and you say your ambition is small, that you just want to be a mouse, then the elephant will starve to death. It's hard to say whether I have ambition or not. Ambition is determined by what you're capable of doing, what you will do — by your nature.

Caijing: It's said Pinduoduo's valuation has already exceeded $15 billion, but you've never been willing to disclose the real valuation — why?

Huang Zheng: If everyone else is telling lies, and you tell one truth, the truth and the lies end up looking the same. When I first started raising money, the company was in great shape, but when I told the truth, investors thought it was a lie. No matter what number you report, they'll discount it by 20%, even 50%, against industry standard. Later, once Pinduoduo had gained a bit of a reputation, funnily enough, when I told a lie, the other side thought it was true.

It's like when I was little, my father bought a genuine watch, and everyone said it was fake; later, once I had money, my father wore a fake watch, and everyone said it was real. That's just how the world is.

## 20. CCTV Interview With Pinduoduo's Huang Zheng: If You Still Treat People as Traffic, You've Already Lost

As experts have pointed out, the era of "traffic is king" has already passed, though many internet entrepreneurs may not have realized it yet. The world will always belong to those who correctly judge the trend and bet on the right track. Standing at the threshold of the post-traffic era, the Pinduoduo phenomenon is precisely the best answer to the second half of e-commerce.

In a recent interview with CCTV Finance, Pinduoduo founder Huang Zheng said that if you always treat the consumer as traffic, treating them as an asset to be sold to merchants, that's certainly not sustainable. Pinduoduo's goal is to provide consumers with happiness and high-cost-effectiveness products.

In the first half of e-commerce, the fight for traffic was the dominant logic. What is the essence of traffic? It's information — information about merchants, information about users, information about orders, and so on — everything that can be carried and transmitted within cyberspace; that's also part of why people call the era corresponding to the internet the "information age."

It's an undisputed fact that the cost of acquiring traffic is getting higher and higher. This expense isn't just about "bit traffic" itself, but about the information carried by that "traffic" becoming ever more expensive — meaning the cost of obtaining effective information (information usable for the next step, or for direct conversion) is rising. Someone gave an example: selling hairy crabs online — going through the whole set of methods, from various e-commerce operations approaches to elevator ads — the cost of acquiring a single new customer runs as high as 345 yuan.

Expensive traffic is one reason traffic-based thinking is gradually being abandoned, but it's not the essence of the problem. "The word 'traffic' is a rather cold one — treating every living, breathing person as traffic, as a click, when in fact the person behind every click is different," Huang Zheng said. If a platform treats consumers as "data" and sells them to merchants, it will ultimately fail to align with consumers' real needs, and will be abandoned.

Huang Zheng pointed out that it's precisely because Pinduoduo starts from the logic of people that it understands consumers better than many platforms do. He gave an example: next to a highway there's a lot of traffic, but if you build a shopping mall there, that mall might not necessarily thrive. "Every time you go to Disneyland, it's packed with people. Does this place have inherent traffic? No. It's because what it offers has high cost-effectiveness, or brings you tremendous joy, and that's why people go there."

He believes this is Pinduoduo's real competitiveness: "The Pinduoduo of the future should be a combination of Costco and Disneyland, blending the real and the virtual. That is, when you go there to buy something with high cost-effectiveness, you should be able to feel really happy — you should come out happier than when you went in. That's what we hope for." Through this, Pinduoduo aims to create an entirely new commercial form.

First, Pinduoduo lowers the customer's decision-making cost. In its product selection, it focuses on standardized products — high-frequency, low-price, high-quality — and the advantage of this selection method is that it saves users time, avoiding the trap of "wall-to-wall shopping," helping users pick a product in the shortest possible time, immediately initiating a team-buy, and lowering the decision-making cost to buy the intended product at a low price.

Second, Pinduoduo deeply understands consumer psychology, with an understanding of cost-effectiveness deeper than most platforms — always staying just beyond the consumer's expectations. Cheapness isn't Pinduoduo's keyword; rather, it's about letting consumers feel they've gotten their money's worth, even more than their money's worth. In fact, over the past year, Pinduoduo's repeat-purchase rate has doubled, and its average order value has risen from around ten-plus yuan in the early days to forty or fifty yuan now.

At the same time, through social team-buying, human warmth can be added to the process. For example, an individual can launch a team-buy invitation to friends and family on WeChat; once the team-buy succeeds, the merchant ships the goods. This model weaves the act of buying into social interaction, adding interactivity and fun to what was originally just a shopping act, offering an entirely new, shared shopping experience.

Huang Zheng also revealed that, while satisfying consumers' material needs, Pinduoduo has put a lot of product design and operational work into satisfying different levels of spiritual consumption needs among people — impulse consumption, rational consumption, cathartic consumption. To some extent, this is also why, even facing homogeneous competition, Pinduoduo can still keep drawing consumers back.

From this perspective, many people's understanding of Pinduoduo as simply "social e-commerce" and "cheap" is a profound misunderstanding of Pinduoduo's business model. Everything starts from the logic of people, matching products to people, letting the right person buy the right thing in the right scenario, and buy it happily — building a shopping carnival that never ends. That is Pinduoduo's secret code.

## 21. Huang Zheng — Full Transcript of the Yunjiu Capital Interview

Reporter: How do you ensure the quality of Pinduoduo's goods?

Huang Zheng: I think, from the consumer's perspective, the first thing has to be that the goods are good — because from their perception, the goods first need to be reliable. But to ensure the goods stay reliable over the long run, what matters most is having a group of reliable people behind them — only then can you ensure the goods stay reliable on an ongoing basis.

Reporter: How do you build a reliable team?

Huang Zheng: I think, for a group of people to keep maintaining a reliable collective, a reliable company, the most important thing may be having a good culture. And that good culture, really, comes down to whether the founding members can establish good values from the very start, and then, as the team develops going forward, keep reinforcing that, treating it as the cornerstone of sustained development.

Reporter: What are your company's values?

Huang Zheng: Our company has a set of values called: integrity, and Ben Fen. Especially the latter — Ben Fen. Ben Fen means: taking responsibility for yourself. That is, if a problem comes up, first look for the problem in yourself, rather than blaming others. Second, it means that no matter what others do, you do your own proper duty. For instance, in business, if you happen to hold a certain degree of monopoly power in some local market, can you still stick to your proper duty and only take a reasonable profit? When a disagreement arises, or when a pie needs to be divided and it's hard to make a single cut perfectly fair, can you first think about doing your own proper duty, about not taking advantage of others? Establishing this kind of culture at the outset is actually pretty hard, because in many ways it goes against human nature — from the moment we're born we're grabbing for food, everyone's the same. So if you can hold to your proper duty, to some extent you'll win more friends, and more people will gather around you — others will feel that working with you is a genuinely happy thing.

Reporter: How do you create a virtuous-cycle business model?

Huang Zheng: What we want to do is see whether, through an improved way of running the business, we can create a mechanism that improves the supply chain, letting consumers choose the good things, letting good things command a premium, and thereby reverse-driving the entire production process — so that the whole commercial process ends up with good money driving out bad. If we can do that, and if it can sustain itself, keep running on its own, then over the long term, the improvement it brings to my own life and to the people around me is more lasting, with broader impact — rather than being like a single successful thing at one point in time, like setting off fireworks, which is gone once it's over. Before starting Pinhaohuo, I actually spent a long time resting at home, and during that time I spent a lot of time thinking about how to improve my own life. I found that not just myself, but the people around me too, feel a real joy in finding good things, high-quality things, and are happy to share them with others. So I think the product we're building is a kind of grassroots innovation. In fact, in our own neighborhood, we already see this kind of thing happening every day — for example, if you bring back an especially good leg of lamb from New Zealand, you'll probably share it with the people around you. I think this kind of thing exists naturally. When I calmed down and observed this, I found that it's not that you need to pay money for someone to be willing to share with you — rather, it's that the thing itself is good, and beneficial to the people around, and in today's society, as everyone grows wealthier, people are very willing to share good things — you shouldn't drive it through interest and profit alone, but instead have a mechanism that lets people discover beauty and spread beauty. I think, in today's society, as everyone grows more prosperous economically, you should create more things that give people more fulfillment and growth on a spiritual level.

Reporter: Do you enjoy the process of starting a business?

Huang Zheng: Starting a business really is something I enjoy a great deal, because it's a bit like playing a game — somewhat addictive — with a group of people you like, together heading toward a shared vision and goal. What makes starting a business even more interesting than playing a game is that you're not just making yourself happy — while you're happy yourself, you're very likely also creating impact and change in the world. That in itself gives your life a stronger sense of accomplishment. Of course, as you get older, no one can keep starting businesses forever, but I hope that during the entrepreneurial stage of my life, I can build something that, looking back later, will make me feel proud, and make me and the people who started this business with me feel proud too.

Reporter: How do you communicate more effectively with the public in this venture?

Huang Zheng: What's different about this venture compared to previous ones is that it's, to some extent, more public-facing — meaning it's a company, but it's also providing a public service. In our previous companies, doing gaming or e-commerce, that was really a purely commercial matter, so there's a piece of work we've increasingly come to realize is important, but that we previously lacked experience in — the so-called PR. Our understanding of PR isn't really about the Chinese term for "public relations" as such — it's more about how to communicate more effectively with the public. Because in previous ventures, we were dealing with purely commercial partners — I could talk to you for two hours and clearly explain my thinking. But the situation now is that people won't even finish reading an article — they just look at the headline, and after reading just the headline, they form a strong bias that's then hard to reverse. So this kind of light-touch, broad-reach public communication is something we're genuinely lacking in experience with, without a mature track record either. In this area, we're still very much rookies, and we'll definitely run into a lot of pitfalls along the way, but I think if we work a bit harder, learning it bit by bit, hopefully it won't cause us too much trouble.

Reporter: What past life experience has influenced you the most?

Huang Zheng: My first formal job, and also my only job, was at Google. I think Google's influence on me has been enormous. First: very few companies embed a non-commercial value like "do no evil" so deeply into a commercial company the way Google does — Google's success, to some extent, showed me that pursuing beautiful things, or things that look non-commercial, can, in the end, still turn out very well from a commercial standpoint. There's more than one path to making money, to commercial success, but this at least proves that one path — one that seems, in my view, more beautiful — can also work extremely well. Second: when I joined Google, the company hadn't gone public yet, so I also saw a lot of people become suddenly wealthy overnight. Observing and experiencing the people around me up close had a big influence on me, because you suddenly realize money's effect has two sides — not just letting you achieve financial and mental freedom, seemingly all positive — it also has a lot of negative sides; for a lot of people, having money can make them suddenly lose their life's goal and meaning, and for some, having money might even leave them adrift for as long as ten years, during the very best years of their life, which they might just squander away. If they'd had a little less money, their overall life wealth, including their level of happiness, might actually have been higher. This experience, to some extent, I think has let me approach business with a more ordinary state of mind, because business itself is, to a great extent, measured by wealth — but very early on, because of Google, I came to realize that this yardstick is just one of several dimensions, and that overemphasizing it can actually be harmful — to your own life, and even to the business itself.

Reporter: How do you overcome the lonely moments of entrepreneurship?

Huang Zheng: The loneliness of entrepreneurship is something a lot of entrepreneurs experience, because when you're making decisions, your position is different from everyone else's, and a lot of the time other people can't fully understand why you're doing what you're doing, or truly discuss it with you on completely equal footing, from the same position. So once you've been at it long enough, you'll find there's a bit of a paradox: when the company is doing well, you're often walking on thin ice every single day, but the day you feel very safe, very comfortable — that's often exactly when the company starts running into trouble. It really does seem to work that way. So, first, maybe it's about accepting, in your own mindset, that walking on thin ice, trembling with caution every day, is a normal state to be in. Second, it comes back to your original intent in starting the business, and what you actually want — if this venture brings you more friends, more love, then to some extent you'll feel a sense of warmth. So different people, doing the same thing, may start from different places — for me, at least, my hope in doing Pinhaohuo is that, on one hand, I can gather more and more consumers, and on the other hand, among the people I work with, including partners, at least most of them like me more over time — that way, the longer I go, the older I get, the more love I receive, and the stronger my own sense of life's happiness becomes.
