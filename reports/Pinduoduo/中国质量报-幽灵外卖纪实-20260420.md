# A Sharp Sword Cuts Down the "Ghosts," An Iron Fist Protects Livelihoods

**——A Chronicle of the State Administration for Market Regulation's Lawful Investigation and Punishment of the "Ghost Takeout" Case Series Involving Seven E-Commerce Platforms**

Source: China Quality News, April 20, 2026, Issue No. 8327, Page 1
Reporter: Xu Jianhua (with contributions from Peng Xie, He Ke, Xu Yachen, and intern reporter Wang Yiming)

---

The highest fine imposed on a single platform reached 1.5 billion yuan, with total fines and confiscated proceeds across the seven platforms reaching 3.597 billion yuan; fines against corporate legal representatives and food safety directors totaled nearly 20 million yuan; and new cake-shop listings were suspended for periods ranging from three to nine months. On April 17, the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) announced the administrative penalty results for the "ghost takeout" case series involving seven e-commerce platforms: Pinduoduo, Meituan, JD.com, Ele.me (Taobao Shan'gou), Douyin, Taobao, and Tmall.

The total fine of 3.597 billion yuan is the largest penalty since the Food Safety Law took effect. Spanning nearly ten months, covering all 31 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities nationwide, and involving every major e-commerce platform, this "largest fine in history" was the product of a winding and arduous enforcement process.

---

## I. The "Ghosts" Appear — The Case Is Escalated

On September 8, 2025, after a surprise inspection of order-transfer platforms, SAMR leadership issued instructions on the case's handling: "This case bears major significance for the public's food safety — it must be investigated and dealt with strictly and swiftly."

How it began: on July 12, 2025, the Beitaipingzhuang Market Regulation Office of the Haidian District Market Regulation Bureau in Beijing received a complaint from a consumer surnamed Liu, who reported suspected food safety problems with a birthday cake he had purchased from a shop called "Sweet Love Letter" on a certain platform.

The Haidian District market regulation authorities acted quickly and found that all 20-plus "Sweet Love Letter" outlets in the Beijing area had no physical storefronts. More shocking still, the food business licenses for all 378 chain outlets under the "Sweet Love Letter" name turned out to be forged.

Following the thread, enforcement officers uncovered a clearly delineated "ghost takeout" criminal supply chain involving e-commerce platforms, order-transfer platforms, and cake shops. On August 6, the Beijing Municipal Market Regulation Bureau reported the case to SAMR.

SAMR's Law Enforcement and Inspection Bureau determined that this "lowest bidder wins" bidding mechanism exposed the vicious nature of platform competition — platforms and "ghost shops" reaped the profits while operators who actually supplied the food were caught in a race-to-the-bottom spiral, leaving physical shops with no margin and food quality and safety unguaranteed. Given the case's broad scope and severe impact, the investigation was escalated.

SAMR's decision: expand the investigation, get to the bottom of it, trace the "ghost takeout" chain with on-site inspections, and carry out electronic forensics and evidence collection.

---

## II. Drawing the Sword — Tracing the Chain

On August 11, 2025, Lou Chao and Wang Fang, key enforcement officers from Zhejiang Province's market regulation system, led a team to Chongqing to carry out an on-site inspection of Chongqing Zhuandanbao Network Technology Co., Ltd.

A key lead uncovered in Chongqing: besides Chongqing Zhuandanbao, Anhui Xunmeng was another, even larger order-transfer platform. The two order-transfer platforms colluded with each other, forming a complete illegal supply chain that provided the technical support and trading venue for mass-listing "ghost shops" and illicitly transferring orders.

SAMR promptly directed the market regulation authorities of Zhejiang and Wuhu, Anhui, to conduct a surprise inspection of Anhui Xunmeng, obtaining a large volume of evidence.

Wang Fang, an electronic data expert, led a technical team in the data analysis. Once the Chongqing Zhuandanbao database was extracted, the team faced tens of millions of records, spread across numerous tables, with messy fields and heavy noise.

They started by examining the characteristics of the order-transfer behavior itself — order flow, account aggregation, and the relationship between order senders and receivers — gradually untangling the transfer logic. They found that behind a single order-sending account on Chongqing Zhuandanbao often sat hundreds or even thousands of order-transfer shops on the platform, with platform orders being funneled and operated under a small number of accounts.

By cross-referencing IP addresses, login logs, and recipient phone numbers, investigators progressively identified specific details for more than a dozen "ghost shops." When the Longwan District Market Regulation Bureau investigated one of these "ghost" merchants, it further uncovered collusion among intermediary agencies, forged-certificate gangs, and platform internal reviewers.

When the data from Chongqing Zhuandanbao and Anhui Xunmeng were combined, the total reached several hundred million records. Building on the successful experience with Chongqing Zhuandanbao, Wang Fang's team quickly made a breakthrough, cracking the database and obtaining key evidence.

About two weeks later, when SAMR reviewed the data analysis results, it found the scale far exceeded expectations: the two order-transfer platforms had illegally transferred as many as 3.6 million cake orders, involving seven platforms — JD.com, Meituan, Douyin, Pinduoduo, Ele.me (Taobao Shan'gou), Taobao, and Tmall.

Faced with a case of this magnitude, SAMR leadership issued directives. SAMR leaders then took charge, with the nationwide market regulation system participating jointly, formally launching a "sword-drawing" investigation into the seven leading e-commerce platforms implicated.

---

## III. The Iron Fist Strikes — A Nationwide Coordinated Operation

Zhang Jie, team leader of the Comprehensive Law Enforcement Bureau at the Nanjing Municipal Market Regulation Bureau in Jiangsu Province, recalled his feelings on receiving the "call to assemble" in Beijing: "When I got the mobilization order from the SAMR task force, I felt genuinely excited. In my day-to-day enforcement work, I had come across complaints about 'ghost takeout,' but they were always about a single shop. My family and I had also had similar suspicions ourselves when ordering takeout."

Key enforcement officers who took part in the mobilization included: Hu Chao, First-Class Chief Section Member of the Nanjing Municipal Market Regulation Bureau in Jiangsu Province (champion of the National Electronic Data Forensics Competition finals); Yu Yan, Deputy Director of the Law Enforcement and Inspection Division of the Zhejiang Provincial Market Regulation Bureau; Li Xin, Director of the Yangzhou Municipal Market Regulation Comprehensive Administrative Law Enforcement Supervision Bureau in Jiangsu Province; Zhou Qunbiao, Deputy Director of the Comprehensive Law Enforcement Bureau of the Xingtai Municipal Market Regulation Bureau in Hebei Province; Zhang Jun, Third-Class Researcher at the Law Enforcement and Inspection Bureau of the Hebei Provincial Market Regulation Bureau; Zhang Li, Deputy Director of the Jiangwan Market Regulation Office of the Hongkou District Market Regulation Bureau in Shanghai; and Yuan Xiaolong, Head of the Fifth Enforcement Team of the Comprehensive Law Enforcement Brigade of the Haidian District Market Regulation Bureau in Beijing, among others.

The team spent the 2025 National Day holiday working overtime. Two or three days before the holiday began, Li Xin had already led the Jiangsu case team on reconnaissance across multiple locations including Yangzhou and Nanjing.

On October 10, more than a hundred key enforcement officers from the national market regulation system gathered at No. 9 Madian East Road in Beijing for a one-day special training session. SAMR had prepared an enforcement manual, case-handling guidelines, and investigation checklists so that this enforcement team, drawn from all over the country, could achieve "unified action, unified goals" in the shortest possible time.

Based on the preliminary reconnaissance, SAMR formed seven special task groups plus one technical task group to provide technical support. Yu Yan, Zhang Li, Yuan Xiaolong, Li Xin, Zhang Jie, Zhou Qunbiao, Zhang Jun, and Wang Fang each served as group leaders, dividing the labor among themselves.

SAMR leadership personally directed the operation, establishing a unified coordination and command mechanism to track case progress at all times. SAMR's Law Enforcement and Inspection Bureau was specifically responsible for handling the case, with its Fourth Inspection Division coordinating the enforcement process and a dedicated task force established for the purpose.

Based on the division of labor across the different "links" of the "ghost takeout" case, distinct handling mechanisms were established: SAMR directly investigated the e-commerce platforms; the order-transfer platforms were placed under designated oversight; and "ghost takeout" shops were investigated jointly nationwide.

On October 11, 2025, the seven task groups departed from Madian in Beijing for the front lines at the seven e-commerce platforms to gather evidence on site — launching, under SAMR's full command and with the strength of the entire system mobilized, an all-out campaign against "ghost takeout" on e-commerce platforms.

---

## IV. Facing Difficulty Head-On — A Battle of Wits and Wills

As the clock struck 10 p.m., this nearly three-hour "standoff" seemed to be heading nowhere near common ground. On one side stood the task group; on the other, the liaison representatives dispatched by a certain platform company. The two sides' main point of contention was over the scope and authority of the investigation and evidence-gathering, and one particular keyword in the evidence request.

That morning at 10 a.m., the task group had moved in to the platform to conduct an on-site inspection, but the platform company adopted a "stall, delay, refuse" tai-chi-style tactic, even threatening to file complaints against the enforcement officers.

Faced with the company's non-cooperation, Wen Lingyan was so frustrated she was moved to tears, but she refused to bend or back down. Wiping away her tears, she kept working, and together with her colleagues broke through the platform company's "tai-chi tactics" with the firm stance that "without legal authorization, nothing may be done; but what the law mandates must be done." The other side finally handed over the relevant data around midnight, and the task group did not finish gathering evidence until 3 a.m. the next day.

After moving onto the platforms' premises, the task groups found that technical forensics was both the key bottleneck and the biggest challenge in the case. Zhang Yuhao, a First-Class Administrative Law Enforcement Officer at the Zhumadian Municipal Market Regulation Bureau in Henan Province, said: "The biggest challenges we faced were the sheer volume of data, and the difficulty of gathering, preserving, and verifying evidence. E-commerce platforms' business data isn't just massive in scale — it's also stored in a scattered way, generally in the cloud."

This meant that gathering evidence required the cooperation of the platform companies' technical staff to retrieve data from the cloud on site and hand it over to the task groups.

In the actual investigation, the task groups encountered layer upon layer of difficulty in obtaining data and evidence, because the platform companies mostly resorted to "soft resistance" tactics:

- Refusing to hand over data on grounds such as "system upgrade in progress," "no data authorization," or "the data isn't stored locally"
- Stalling cooperation using excuses like "trade secrets" or "insufficient network speed"
- Deploying unrelated staff to run interference repeatedly
- Appearing to "actively cooperate" on the surface while in fact providing fragmented, poorly formatted data in an attempt to bury the key information

Many times the task groups moved into a company at 10 a.m. and still had nothing to show for it by 10 p.m.; even when a platform did provide data, it was often only a third, a quarter, or even less of the total.

Faced with the platforms' various tricks, the task groups resorted to the "clumsiest" method they could think of — logging into all seven platforms and verifying information and credentials shop by shop, cake shop by cake shop, and in some cases going to check physical storefronts in person.

Since each phone number was limited by the platforms to a certain number of login views, everyday conversation among task group members turned into things like: "Brother Zhang, my number's used up for viewing — register with your number and keep checking..."

Over roughly two weeks, they burned through dozens of phone numbers and verified three to four thousand shops, gnawing through this "hard bone" with their "clumsy" method. Combined with the data from Chongqing Zhuandanbao and Anhui Xunmeng, the task groups built their own big-data database.

Once a company provided its first, "discounted" batch of data, the investigators could cross-check it against their own database, establishing a working method of "shared data patterns, data comparison, cross-verification, and tracing back to the source" to uncover the hidden tricks, and then go back to "talk" — or confront — the platform again.

Faced with the task groups' confidently held data evidence, the platform companies began "squeezing out the toothpaste" — providing 20%, then 40%, then 60% of the data... until finally handing over 100%.

Ma Zhenduo, a First-Class Section Member at the Tianjin Comprehensive Administrative Law Enforcement Corps for Market Regulation, said: "To break through the technical impasse, we repeatedly studied the platforms' data architecture, transaction processes, and algorithmic logic, and ultimately succeeded in penetrating the platforms' data barriers, achieving precise extraction, categorization, and effective preservation of key electronic data on order flow, credential filings, and order-transfer transactions."

## V. The Pinduoduo Violent Obstruction of Law Enforcement Incident (December 3-5)

At 10 a.m. on December 3, 2025, task group members from the Jiangsu and Jiangxi teams — Zhang Jie, Chen Cao, Hu Chao, Guo Hui, and others — moved onto the site to conduct their inspection, with no idea they were about to endure three turbulent days and two nights.

Under the platform's "stall, delay, refuse" tai-chi tactics, the investigation and evidence-gathering work had made no substantive progress by 11 p.m. Left with no choice, Guo Hui and several other enforcement officers used the access card the company had given them — which only worked down to the first floor — to look around the surrounding area.

### Tracking Down the Real Workplace

During the special window of the platform company's shift-change "rush hour," enforcement officers tracked the movements of platform staff and located the platform's actual workplace.

### December 3: An Enforcement Officer Suffers a Fracture

After finding every possible way to get into the office space, Guo Hui spotted a suspicious office. Even though he was wearing his enforcement uniform and had proactively identified himself, the other side still forcibly blocked his entry. **While struggling for control of the door, Guo Hui's hand was deliberately crushed by a company employee slamming the door on it, causing a fracture.** Gritting his teeth, Guo Hui held his ground until the company's person in charge finally relented and agreed to provide the data.

But the other side then played the "stalling game" — having promised to hand over the data at 3 a.m., they made the officers wait until 5 a.m., then stalled again from 5 a.m. until noon, and the data they finally handed over was heavily watered down.

### A Second Round of Evidence-Gathering, With Police Involvement

After the first day's "incident," the task group specifically coordinated with the local public security department to carry out a second round of data extraction and evidence-gathering, requesting that the company's technical director carry out the operation in person and on site.

### December 4: Security Guards Storm the Investigation Site, an Enforcement Officer Is Knocked Down and Hospitalized

At around 11 p.m. on December 4, while the task group was carrying out its investigation, **the platform's head of security suddenly lost control, and in full view of the police and the task group, led a group of people to storm the investigation site, shoving and grabbing at the enforcement officers in a violent obstruction of law enforcement.**

Guo Hui, who had kept working the day before despite his fractured hand, immediately stepped forward to intervene when he saw what was happening. As a former serviceman, he instinctively placed himself at the front line of the confrontation, **only to be shoved to the ground, his head striking the floor hard. An ambulance rushed him to the hospital.**

One might have expected that after such an egregious incident, the platform would promptly hand over the data. Instead, **after a private conversation between the company's technical director and its person in charge, the technical director suddenly collapsed to the ground on his own and was likewise taken away by ambulance**, forcing the day's evidence-gathering to end. Task group officers followed him to the hospital, where **doctors said the technical director's heart and body were both fine.**

### December 5: Eating a Sheet of A4 Paper to Destroy Evidence

After the violent obstruction incident, SAMR leadership attached great importance to it, convening a special meeting to study how to respond and instructing the Law Enforcement and Inspection Bureau to immediately send a responsible official to the site to assess the situation. On the evening of December 5, Peng Zengtian, Deputy Director of SAMR's Law Enforcement and Inspection Bureau, was dispatched to the scene to handle matters, and only then did the platform company provide the relevant data for a second time.

That same day, the task group, together with the local police and market regulation authorities, held talks with the platform regarding the violent obstruction incident. Right in the middle of the discussion, **an employee of the platform wrote words like "stay silent" and "don't talk" on a sheet of A4 paper to signal to a colleague being questioned. The task group caught them red-handed, and what followed was a shocking, movie-worthy scene — the employee crumpled up the sheet of A4 paper and, in front of everyone in the room, ate it.**

### The Enforcement Officers' Perseverance

After receiving basic bandaging and treatment, Guo Hui defied the doctors' advice and resolutely returned to the investigation site. Zhou Qunbiao, who had worked 72 hours straight, suffered a sudden cardiac episode and was rushed to the hospital for emergency treatment; lying on the emergency-room bed, his mind was still full of case file details and unfinished verification tasks. Once his condition stabilized, Zhou immediately rushed from the emergency room back to the investigation site: "The case isn't finished — I can't rest easy."

Behind these plain words lies a spirit of "exceptional capacity for hardship, exceptional capacity to fight, exceptional capacity for sacrifice" — working around the clock, seven days a week, became the norm; there were no holidays off, and minor injuries were no reason to leave the front line.

After three rounds of on-site verification, the task groups tore open a breach in the seven platforms' "iron walls," **confirming ironclad evidence on 67,604 "ghost shops"** — laying a solid foundation for the case's successful resolution.

## VI. One Penalty Per Shop — A Far-Reaching Impact

As the investigation deepened and the evidence of wrongdoing grew clearer, a new difficulty and challenge confronted the task groups: how to design the administrative penalty scheme for the platforms.

Zhang Li, Deputy Director of the Jiangwan Market Regulation Office of the Hongkou District Market Regulation Bureau in Shanghai, said: "It might look like we were handling one big case, but in reality we were facing more than 60,000 individual cases. Every shop's situation was different, so we couldn't make blanket determinations."

Drawing on the task groups' successful experience, SAMR pooled resources from all sides to form a joint case-handling effort:
- It issued more than 50,000 case-referral notices to provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities across the country, verifying platform leads uncovered in the case one by one
- The Law Enforcement and Inspection Bureau worked jointly with the General Office, the Regulations Department, the Network Monitoring Department, the Catering and Food Department, the News Department, and other bureaus and departments to resolve major aspects of the case

SAMR extended its deliberations on the applicable law beyond the system itself to legislative bodies, judicial organs, and academia, to ensure the case would stand up to both legal and historical scrutiny. SAMR leadership led delegations to legislative and judicial bodies on four separate occasions, repeatedly soliciting opinions on core issues such as determining platform liability and setting the scale of penalties, to genuinely ensure the case was handled in accordance with the law and its power exercised prudently.

SAMR organized a symposium of legal experts, inviting authorities in administrative law, civil law, e-commerce law, and related fields to hold in-depth discussions on key issues such as defining the parties liable for the violations, the boundaries of platforms' review obligations, and how to calculate illegal gains — producing written expert opinions.

SAMR leadership chaired 24 special meetings in succession, examining each platform's violations item by item and repeatedly refining the administrative penalty scheme, to ensure that every company, every finding of wrongdoing, and every penalty amount had a firm basis in law.

On the basis of fully incorporating the views of the legislature, the judiciary, and academia, a penalty scheme took shape that embodied the principle of "proportionality between violation and penalty," reflected the requirements of the "four strictests," and matched the public's expectations.

SAMR decided: in accordance with the principle of "proportionality between violation and penalty," it would lawfully impose a penalty on a "one shop, one penalty" basis for platforms' failure to fulfil their review obligations.

Wang Huowang, Director-General of SAMR's Law Enforcement and Inspection Bureau, said this case is a landmark one in SAMR's history for managing food safety and regulating platform development. The penalties reflect a regulatory orientation of "both hands firm" on promoting the sound, healthy development of the platform economy and on regulatory compliance — "promoting development and tightening regulation" together. The fundamental purpose is to compel platform companies to properly fulfil the obligations set out by law, to establish and implement a food-safety risk prevention and control mechanism covering credential review, risk monitoring, problem screening, and rapid response, to genuinely safeguard food safety, and to promote the sound, healthy development of food-delivery platforms.

---

## VII. Platform Responses and Rectification

After receiving SAMR's administrative penalty decisions, Pinduoduo, Meituan, JD.com, Taobao Shan'gou (formerly Ele.me), Douyin, Taobao, and Tmall all issued statements pledging to firmly implement the regulatory requirements and genuinely safeguard food-delivery safety.

---

Food is the first necessity for the people, and safety is the first requirement of food. Although the market regulation authorities' major campaign against "ghost takeout" has now come to a close, and every platform company has, as required by the rectification order, taken down the unreviewed "ghost shops" and ceased cooperation with the relevant order-transfer platforms on food-delivery transfers, food safety oversight is a journey with no end — the sword of enforcement remains permanently drawn and ready to strike at any time.
