# Error 1: Colin Huang donating personal shares ≠ PDD the company doing charity with its cash

## I. The previous flawed logic

> "If Colin Huang is right, the $60.4 billion in cash will ultimately return to 'shareholders in the broad sense' (human society) through another channel (research + social redistribution)."

**This statement is fundamentally wrong.**

## II. The user's precise critique

> "What Colin Huang donated was his own shares — it doesn't actually touch other shareholders' interests. If it's meant to benefit 'shareholders in the broad sense,' that would actually harm the interests of other minority shareholders."

## III. The correct logic: two things that must be kept strictly separate

| Party | Money | Right of disposal |
|---|---|---|
| **Colin Huang's personal 24% stake** ($34 billion) | **Colin Huang's own money** | Colin Huang can donate it if he wants (and already has) — this is his personal, admirable choice |
| **PDD the company's cash** $60.4 billion | **All shareholders' money** | Any use requires **shareholder authorization**, including minority shareholders |

### Key inference

If PDD used company cash for "research + social redistribution," this would in essence be:
- **Done without the consent of all shareholders**
- **Using other minority shareholders' money**
- **To do something the founder personally wants to do**

This is not "benefiting shareholders in the broad sense" at all — it is, in fact, a **forced appropriation of other minority shareholders' interests**.

## IV. The deeper problem this exposes: PDD's "no dividends" policy is actually a violation of "benfen" (proper conduct)

### Duan Yongping's original statement on benfen (one of five)
> "**Don't take advantage of others, even when we can.**"

### Applied to PDD's cash problem

- PDD not paying dividends = **forcibly retaining other shareholders' capital**
- and then using that money to realize the founder's vision ("long-term value," "ecosystem investment")
- this is precisely "taking advantage of shareholders"

### What proper conduct should actually look like

Return the money to shareholders and let shareholders decide for themselves (including shareholders donating it to charity themselves).

### Comparison with the correct approach (the Buffett model)

| Action | Buffett personally | Berkshire the company |
|---|---|---|
| Personal philanthropy | ✅ Pledged 99% of his wealth to the Gates Foundation and others | -- |
| Disposal of company wealth | -- | Buffett generates outsized returns from retained earnings through acquisitions and investments |
| Boundary | **The two are strictly separated** | **The two are strictly separated** |

Buffett would never donate Berkshire's cash to a charitable foundation, nor would he use company money to realize his personal vision. **His personal money is for his personal vision.**

Colin Huang did the second thing (donating personal shares) but refused to do the first thing (returning company cash) — this is **a logical inconsistency**.

## V. Revised version of "what Duan Yongping's bet is really on"

### Previous mistaken judgment
> "Investing in PDD = betting on the correctness of Colin Huang's philosophy, with the money ultimately returning to shareholders in the broad sense through another channel"

### Revised, accurate judgment
> **Investing in PDD = accepting the reality that "company cash is being held hostage by the founder's philosophy," and betting only that the compounding of the business itself can offset the discount from cash not being returned**

This is a **pure business bet**, not a "moral bet" or a bet on "benefiting shareholders in the broad sense."

# Error 2: Using market cap for the "dollar test" is wrong — intrinsic value should be used instead

## I. The previous flawed logic

> "PDD has cumulatively retained $42.4 billion in cash, while market cap has instead evaporated by $56.8 billion. By Buffett's 'dollar test' standard: **PDD's capital allocation has been a failure** — every $1 retained created not $1 of market value, but -$1.34 of market value."

## II. The user's precise critique

> "That approach is wrong. The correct standard is that every dollar retained should create more than one dollar of value, because **market cap is something the market assigns — PDD and its shareholders don't control it — but value is objective**."

## III. Correction: redoing the "dollar test" using intrinsic value

### The true definition of Buffett's "dollar test"

> "We test the wisdom of retaining earnings by assessing whether retention, over time, **delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained**."

Buffett's original text does indeed use "market value," but **his implicit premise** is that the market will reflect intrinsic value over the long run. In the short-to-medium term, what should actually be examined is the intrinsic value the company creates.

### PDD's change in intrinsic value (2020-2025)

| Metric | 2020 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue (RMB) | 59.5 billion | ~450 billion | **+7.5x** |
| Net profit | -7.1 billion (loss) | ~110 billion | **From loss to profit** (absolute change of +117 billion) |
| China annual active users | 788 million | 900 million+ | +14% |
| Overseas business | 0 | Temu covers 90+ countries | **From zero to the world's second-largest cross-border e-commerce platform** |
| Operating cash flow | Weak | RMB 43.7 billion/year (2024) | Continuously growing |

**From an intrinsic-value standpoint**: the value the business created with the capital PDD retained over these five years is staggering — it **easily passes the dollar test**.

### The divergence between market cap and intrinsic value

| Dimension | Performance | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Market cap** (what the market assigns) | Down ($240 billion → $143.2 billion) | Regulatory pressure + geopolitics + VIE discount + no-dividend discount |
| **Intrinsic value** (what the business creates) | Up sharply (revenue +7.5x, net profit from a loss to a profit) | Genuine business expansion |

My earlier criticism of PDD using "market cap evaporated by $56.8 billion" was a **classic case of market-cap worship** — exactly the kind of thinking Buffett and Duan Yongping oppose most strongly.

## IV. So has PDD's capital allocation actually been a "success"?

The real question after correction is: **of the $60.4 billion in cash, how much has actually gone into business investment?**

### Breaking down where PDD's cash went (estimated)

| Use | Estimated amount | Nature |

| **Total used** | **~$23-32 billion** | ✅ Created substantial intrinsic value |

### Judgment after correction

| Portion of cash | Capital allocation assessment |
|---|---|
| **The ~$28 billion already used** | ✅ **Passes the dollar test**, having created enormous business value in Temu, agriculture, and elsewhere |
| **The ~$30 billion+ sitting on the balance sheet** | ❓ **Undecided** — whether this is strategic reserve or a genuine "allocation failure" depends on how it's used over the next five years |

Possible uses:

2. **A war chest for future acquisitions**: but historically PDD has done almost no large-scale M&A
3. **Conservative redundancy**: pure risk aversion
4. **VIE structural constraints**: complex repatriation of onshore cash

**It is not "whether PDD should pay dividends" (that is a false question)** — the real question is:

### **The opportunity cost of the $30 billion+ in idle cash**

- If there is a major investment opportunity within five years (such as Temu expansion or a new business) → holding onto the cash is correct

- Neither PDD nor Colin Huang nor Lei Chen has ever made any comparable explicit commitment

**This is a fundamental lack of governance transparency** — not a difference in philosophy, but a lack of rules.

## IV. What actually happened at Berkshire (correcting another inaccuracy from before)

My earlier statement that "Buffett has Berkshire do large amounts of buybacks to return money to shareholders" was also not accurate. Here is what actually happened:

### Dividends
- **Never paid a dividend** (except for that one $0.10/share payment in 1967, about which Buffett later joked, "I must have been in the bathroom that day")
- Buffett has always opposed dividends, on the grounds that "reinvesting retained earnings is more efficient than paying a dividend and having shareholders reinvest it themselves"

### Buybacks
- **Completely opposed to buybacks in the early years** (1970s-2010s)
- **2011**: first established a conservative buyback policy (only buying back when the share price was below 1.1x book value)
- **July 2018**: the policy was loosened (buybacks allowed whenever both Buffett and Munger judged the shares to be trading "below intrinsic value")
- **Large-scale buybacks in 2020-2023**: $24.7 billion in 2020, $27.0 billion in 2021, $7.9 billion in 2022, $9.2 billion in 2023
- **Buybacks have almost stopped since 2024** (Buffett considers valuations too high)

### Key takeaway

**Berkshire not paying dividends + not buying back shares in the early years**: this was because Buffett could generate outsized returns with the cash (proven over 50 years of track record)

**PDD not paying dividends + not buying back shares**: this is because of Colin Huang's philosophy (yet to be proven)

Buffett set himself a **hard constraint** (the dollar test) and regularly acknowledges that "if I can no longer do this, the money will be returned to shareholders."

What about PDD? There is no hard constraint of any kind, no commitment about "what happens if this fails" — only the philosophical packaging of "long-term value."

# Part Four: Methodological lessons from this revision

## Lesson from Revision 1: separate "personal money" from "company money"

**The trap**: using a founder's personal charitable act to excuse the company's capital allocation decisions

**How to avoid it**:
- How much a founder donates personally = a personal choice
- How the company's cash is used = **requires the consent of all shareholders**
- These two things must never be conflated, under any circumstances

## Lesson from Revision 2: beware the "market-cap worship" trap

**The trap**: judging a company's capital allocation by short-term market-cap fluctuations

**How to avoid it**:
- Market cap = market sentiment + intrinsic value (the two cannot be separately measured)
- A company should be judged by the **growth of its intrinsic value** (revenue, profit, cash flow, users, market share)
- A short-term decline in market cap ≠ the company failing to create value
- This is the mode of thinking most emphasized by Buffett and Duan Yongping

## Overall methodological takeaway

**When doing investment analysis**:
1. **Look at the change in intrinsic value first**, then look at how the market prices it
2. **The company's money belongs to the company, personal money belongs to the individual** — any blurring of this line is an analytical trap
3. **Admitting an error is more valuable than covering it up** — the core of value investing is continually recalibrating one's understanding

# Part Five: The final characterization of PDD after revision

## Before revision
"PDD's no-dividend policy is a philosophical blind spot, a violation of benfen, and a capital allocation failure"

## After revision
**PDD is a company that is "strong at creating business value but weak on governance transparency"**:

1. **At the business level**: it passes the dollar test (measured by intrinsic value) — Temu, agriculture, and technology R&D have all created real value
2. **At the capital allocation level**: the cash already deployed has been allocated successfully, but the opportunity cost of the $30 billion+ sitting on the balance sheet **has not yet been validated**
3. **At the governance level**: it lacks Buffett-style hard constraints (such as an explicit dollar-test commitment or buyback trigger conditions); transparency is insufficient
4. **At the shareholder-relations level**: Colin Huang's benfen framework indeed has no dimension for shareholders — but this is not "a violation of benfen," it is **an incompleteness in the framework itself**

## Investment judgment

- **If you believe**: PDD's business can keep compounding at a 30%+ ROE for ten years, and the idle cash will eventually be allocated effectively → the current valuation is attractive
- **If you are concerned**: that the $30 billion+ in cash will sit idle forever and governance transparency will not improve → this should be priced in as a significant discount
- **Duan Yongping's bet**: is essentially a bet on the former, and a bet that "the market will eventually recognize the intrinsic value"

## Closing: the value of being wrong

The process of correcting these two errors is more valuable than the report itself. They remind us of:

**The two most common mistakes in value-investing analysis**:
1. Judging a company by its market cap (market-cap worship)
2. Conflating the money/responsibilities of different parties (blurred boundaries)

**True value-investing thinking**:
- Look at the objective growth of intrinsic value
- Strictly distinguish company money, personal money, and shareholder money
- Acknowledge uncertainty, acknowledge the limits of one's own understanding
- Correct mistakes rather than covering them up

**Implications for PDD**:
- PDD's business is indeed creating intrinsic value
- But governance transparency and the opportunity cost of the $30 billion+ in idle cash are the real questions
- Investment decisions should be based on the real questions, not misled by conceptualized criticism
