# Jensen Huang says AI is a five-layer cake — I dug through all 50 companies

This year, at CES and Davos, Jensen Huang has repeatedly laid out one framework: AI is a five-layer cake.

He's not just using a metaphor. He's telling everyone that AI isn't a software product — it's an entirely new industrial system running from the power plant all the way to the phone screen. Every layer has its own independent investment opportunities, and its own independent chokepoints.

I spent a week going through his framework, screening all 50 companies across the five layers one by one. There was only one screening criterion: who holds the chokepoint, and who has the market overlooked.

This piece is the overview. Five follow-up pieces will each dig deep into one layer.

## What are the five layers?

```
Layer 5  Application layer      AI agents, autonomous driving, robotics
Layer 4  Model layer            Large language models, multimodal models
Layer 3  Infrastructure layer   Data centers, networking, liquid cooling
Layer 2  Chip layer             GPUs, memory, packaging, specialty gases
Layer 1  Energy layer           Power, transformers, nuclear energy
```

In Huang's own words: "In the future, every company will have two factories — one that makes products, and one that is an AI factory. The AI factory does just one thing every day: it produces tokens."

He gave one core metric for measuring an AI factory's efficiency: tokens produced per watt. In the end, the AI business will resemble manufacturing — what matters is how much intelligence can be produced per unit of electricity.

That explains why the bottom layer of this cake is energy rather than chips — without electricity, a GPU is just a piece of metal that generates heat.

## My screening criteria

Most AI investment analysis out there focuses on "who is strongest." But the strongest companies are usually the most expensive — everyone knows Nvidia is great, but it's already a $5 trillion market cap.

I took a different angle: instead of asking who is strongest, I asked **who holds the chokepoint that the market hasn't priced in yet**.

I scored companies across four dimensions:

1. **Bottleneck purity** — what share of the company's revenue comes from the chokepoint business? Only worth a look above 30%.
2. **Irreplaceability** — is it the sole supplier or part of an oligopoly? How long before an alternative emerges?
3. **Degree of market pricing** — has the stock price already told the full bottleneck story in full? A P/E above 100x is likely already fully priced in.
4. **Catalyst timing window** — is there an event within the next six months that could validate the thesis?

After screening all 50 companies across these four dimensions, the most interesting finding was this: **the truly underpriced opportunities are not among the hottest names, but among the hidden players in the supply chain — those that "do the same thing but are valued at half the price," or that "sit atop a trillion-dollar industry chain but carry a market cap of only a few billion dollars."**

## The most notable finding at each layer

### Layer 1 — Energy: a four-year wait for transformers; the most boring layer is the most certain

Nearly half of all planned data center projects in the U.S. have already been delayed or canceled. It's not because of a GPU shortage — it's because of a shortage of power and transformers. Lead times for large power transformers run four years; high-voltage substations take three to five years. That means an order placed today won't arrive until 2030.

The biggest surprise at this layer is **AZZ Inc. (P/E of 13x)**. It does hot-dip galvanizing coatings — every transmission tower, substation steel structure, and data center steel component needs corrosion protection. Every single steel post has to pass through its production line, yet the market prices it exactly like a traditional manufacturer. A 13x P/E is practically unheard of among AI-concept stocks.

GE Vernova and Bloom Energy have the highest visibility at this layer, but they're also the most expensively valued (52x and 140x P/E, respectively). The real value for money is in midstream equipment makers and natural gas infrastructure.

### Layer 2 — Chips: the $5 trillion Nvidia isn't expensive, but the 7x P/E SK Hynix is even cheaper

Nvidia's $5 trillion market cap sounds alarming, but its forward P/E is only 26x, with FY2027 revenue projected at $370 billion, up 71% year over year. Honestly, 26x for a company growing at 70% isn't expensive.

But the real "pricing error" at the chip layer is **SK Hynix** — it holds 62% of the global HBM (high-bandwidth memory) market, with the shortage expected to persist through 2028, yet its forward P/E is only 5.8-7x. Every single AI training chip in the world needs HBM, and SK Hynix's pricing power keeps strengthening as HBM complexity increases. Buying a monopolist like this at 7x P/E is the single biggest contradiction I found after screening all 50 companies.

There are two other overlooked small-cap names at this layer: **Kanto Denka ($1.4 billion market cap)**, Japan's only supplier of NF3/WF6 specialty gases, which every wafer fabrication process needs; and **Ibiden ($4 billion market cap)**, the key bottleneck in GPU packaging substrates, which is investing 500 billion yen to expand capacity.

### Layer 3 — Infrastructure: $725 billion in real cash — hunting for valuation gaps within the same race

The four major cloud providers' combined 2026 capital expenditure totals $725 billion, double the prior year. That money is flowing into optical modules, networking, liquid cooling, and servers.

The leading companies at this layer are already expensively valued — Vertiv at 80x P/E, Arista at 60x. But there's a clear valuation gap: **nVent Electric (35x P/E) does almost the same thing as Vertiv** (data center power distribution, liquid cooling, enclosures), yet it's valued at less than half of Vertiv, while its liquid-cooling order backlog has grown 3x.

Innolight is the point where a Chinese company holds the strongest pricing power at this layer — it supplies roughly half of the world's 800G optical modules. But its stock price is no longer cheap.

### Layer 4 — Models: Anthropic has overtaken OpenAI, but the most overlooked name is Reddit

Anthropic's annualized revenue went from $1 billion to $47 billion in just 14 months, and its $965 billion valuation has now overtaken OpenAI's. Both companies plan to IPO in the second half of 2026.

But the most interesting investment logic at the model layer isn't in the model companies themselves — it's in two "shovel-selling" players:

**Reddit** — the market prices it as a social media company, but authentic human conversation data is a non-renewable resource for training large language models. Both Google and OpenAI have signed multi-year data licensing deals with it. It isn't a social media company — it's the owner of the AI training-data mine.

**Scale AI (private, valued at $29 billion)** — the data-labeling supplier behind GPT-4, Gemini, and U.S. military AI systems. Whichever model wins, it will need Scale AI to label its data.

Another overlooked valuation gap is **Cohere** — it does private, on-premises enterprise AI deployments, with ARR of $240 million and a valuation of only $7-20 billion, roughly 30x ARR, versus 40-50x for OpenAI and Anthropic. It does the same thing, at a much lower price.

### Layer 5 — Applications: few companies actually make money from AI, but valuations have diverged sharply

The brutal reality at this layer: for most "AI concept stocks," AI still accounts for a small share of revenue. Palantir is the purest play (85% growth), but trades at 160x P/E; Tesla is even more extreme (350x P/E).

The real opportunities lie at the end where valuations are being suppressed:

**Salesforce (22x P/E)** — Agentforce is growing at over 200%, but the market only sees overall revenue growth of 10% and prices it as an "old CRM company." If AI's share of the business rises from 3% to 15%, overall growth would reaccelerate, and 22x P/E would be far too low.

**Adobe (15x P/E)** — the absolute monopolist in creative software, trading at its lowest-ever valuation. The market fears AI will disrupt it, but Firefly proves AI is additive rather than a replacement — 75% of Fortune 500 companies use Firefly, and $24 billion worth of assets have already been generated with it. If that fear turns out to be wrong, 15x P/E is a textbook case of "buying amid fear."

**Tempus AI ($9 billion market cap)** — the purest publicly listed AI precision-medicine company; after falling by half from its 52-week high, its valuation has returned to a reasonable range, with 2025 revenue growth of 83% and a contract backlog exceeding $1 billion.

## A final word

After screening all 50 companies across the five layers, my strongest takeaway is this: **the best AI investment opportunities are not among the hottest names.**

Nvidia, TSMC, and Anthropic are indeed the strongest companies, but the market also knows them best. The real excess returns come from overlooked corners of the supply chain — AZZ in hot-dip galvanizing, Archrock in natural gas compression, Ibiden in packaging substrates.

Huang's "five-layer cake" offers a valuable way of thinking: the lower the layer, the more physical it is, and the harder it is to route around — the higher the investment certainty. The sexiest layers (models, applications) carry the lowest certainty; the most boring layers (energy, infrastructure) carry the highest.
