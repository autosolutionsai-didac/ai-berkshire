# The Mystery of Pinduoduo's 420 Billion Yuan Cash Pile: An Experiment in Delayed Gratification

Pinduoduo's stock price has fallen to near its 52-week low, $74. P/E ratio of 7.9x.

But do you know how much cash is on its balance sheet? 420 billion yuan, about $57.5 billion. That's 55% of its market cap.

In other words, when you spend $74 to buy one share of Pinduoduo, $40.5 of that is cash. You're actually only paying $33.5 for a business that earns roughly 100 billion yuan a year.

Is this price reasonable?

---

## If Temu disappeared tomorrow

Much of the hesitation around buying Pinduoduo comes from Temu — cash burn, tariffs, geopolitics. So let's just assume the worst case: Temu goes to zero entirely, and all overseas investment is written off.

Strip out Temu, and Pinduoduo's domestic main platform numbers look like this:

| Metric | Value |
|------|------|
| Main-platform revenue | approx. 300 billion yuan |
| Main-platform net profit | **approx. 105 billion yuan** |
| Operating margin | **approx. 40%** |

Yes, you read that right. On the consolidated statements, Pinduoduo's 2025 net profit was 99.4 billion yuan, but the main platform alone is actually higher — because Temu dragged it down with a loss of roughly 23 billion yuan.

What is the main platform? A pure platform model that never touches goods or logistics — 900 million users and 10 million merchants transact on it, and Pinduoduo collects advertising fees and commissions. Marginal cost approaches zero; nearly every extra yuan earned is pure profit.

Using main-platform profit to work out the current stock price: market cap of 760 billion yuan, minus net cash of 420 billion yuan, leaves an enterprise value of just 340 billion yuan. Divide that by main-platform net profit of 105 billion yuan — **3.3x.**

A business earning roughly 100 billion yuan a year with a 40% margin, and the market is only pricing it at 3.3x.

Even if Temu never works out, Pinduoduo is still extremely profitable. Which raises the question —

---

## Why not buy back stock?

420 billion yuan of cash on the books. The stock price has fallen to its lowest range ever. The company's own P/E of 7.9x implies a buyback would generate an implied annualized return of over 12%.

It's hard to find a better investment out there than "buying your own company at 7.9x."

And there's no procedural obstacle either. Alibaba, under an identical VIE structure, has already bought back over $30 billion cumulatively. JD.com has done more than $5 billion as well. Pinduoduo not buying back isn't a matter of being unable to — it's a choice not to.

Management's position has been unambiguous. On the Q2 2024 earnings call, Chen Lei said "now is not the right time for buybacks, and there won't be such a need in the foreseeable years ahead." The stock plunged 28% that day, wiping out $55 billion in market value.

By Q4 2025, Zhao Jiazhen's phrasing had shifted to: "Between short-term financial performance and the platform ecosystem's long-term value, the company will firmly choose the latter, and this will be the norm."

**From "not the right time" to "the norm" — the stance is hardening, not softening.**

Where is the money going? Three directions: New PDD Manufacturing (initial injection of 15 billion yuan), the 100-billion-yuan Support Program (over 100 billion yuan committed over three years), and Temu's localization pivot (shifting from direct-mail-from-China to building warehouses overseas). Management believes the long-term returns on these investments exceed what a buyback would generate.

---

## Colin Huang doesn't care about money

So what basis is there for believing management will eventually return the cash to shareholders?

Many analyses argue: Colin Huang holds more than 25% of the shares, his economic interests are aligned with shareholders', and once the stock falls far enough he will inevitably push for a buyback.

There's a problem with this reasoning — **Colin Huang genuinely may not care how much money he personally holds.**

His 25% stake is worth $26 billion at the current price. For someone who retired at 35 to do basic science research and donated $100 million to his alma mater, $26 billion versus $30 billion makes no difference — both are numbers he could never spend in a lifetime.

He personally witnessed the "side effects of overnight wealth" among his Google colleagues, and wrote on his public WeChat account that "money is a tool, not a purpose." You can't motivate someone who doesn't treat personal wealth as an end in itself by telling him "your money is shrinking."

So the chain of logic "stock falls → controlling shareholder feels the pain → pushes for a buyback" is, in Colin Huang's case, most likely broken.

---

## So why trust him at all?

Colin Huang has repeatedly laid out a philosophy centered on two words: **compounding.**

He wrote in a public essay:

> Choosing the right path matters more than effort. Moving forward on the right path, even slowly, is like compounding in investing. **A 20% annual return sustained for 20 straight years is remarkable — far more rewarding than gaining 100% this year and losing 50% next year.**

When he stepped down as chairman in 2021, he said:

> To ensure its high-quality, high-speed development **ten years from now**, some exploration needs to begin now. As the founder, I am stepping back to **feel out the stones on the road ten years ahead.**

In his 2020 letter to shareholders, he wrote:

> Any scheme to exploit others' misfortune or take advantage of loopholes for one's own benefit **looks extraordinarily foolish in the face of time.**

This isn't just talk. Look at what he's actually done:

- Retired at 35 — not for lack of ambition, but to position for what happens a decade later
- Pinduoduo went from founding to IPO in just three years, yet he never chased short-term profit maximization
- The 10-Billion-Yuan Subsidy program looked like cash burn in 2019; in hindsight, it opened the door to tier-1 and tier-2 cities
- Temu looked like a gamble in 2022; by 2025 it already covered over 100 countries

**He's not unwilling to return capital to shareholders — he believes the money invested now will come back multiplied in the future.** This is the logic of delayed gratification — giving up a certain gain today in exchange for a much larger payoff later.

---

## The price of trust, and its limits

Choosing to trust Colin Huang is, fundamentally, a bet on two things:

**First, that his judgment on direction is correct.** Compounding only works if the direction is right. If Temu and New PDD Manufacturing succeed, the payoff from delayed gratification is enormous; if the direction is wrong, delayed gratification just becomes delayed loss-cutting.

Looking at the track record — disrupting e-commerce with social group-buying, breaking into tier-1 and tier-2 cities with the 10-Billion-Yuan Subsidy program, Temu covering the globe within two years — he hasn't made a fatal misjudgment on major strategic direction yet. This is the strongest evidence for trusting him.

**Second, that the money eventually makes its way back to shareholders.** There's a VIE-structure issue here — legally, there is no mechanism that can compel a Cayman shell company to pay dividends to holders of American Depositary Receipts. This can only be a matter of trust; there's no data to verify it.

But look at it from another angle: not paying dividends still leads to shareholder returns via two possible paths —

If the money invested pays off, profit growth naturally drives the share price up, and whether or not dividends are paid becomes irrelevant.

If the money invested doesn't pay off, and there's no better use for the capital left afterward, a buyback becomes the most rational choice.

**The two paths only differ in timeline and route — the destination is the same.**

---

## Closing thoughts

Pinduoduo at $74, a P/E of 7.9x, and a core-business valuation of 3.3x once net cash is stripped out. The market's pricing implies an extremely pessimistic set of assumptions — either profits will decline sharply, or the cash will never belong to shareholders, or there's a structural risk that could wipe the company out.

Whether these assumptions are right or wrong is something everyone can judge for themselves.

But one thing is worth remembering — Colin Huang once said, "a 20% annual return sustained for 20 straight years is remarkable." He himself is putting that statement into action. The only question is: **are you willing to practice delayed gratification alongside him?**
