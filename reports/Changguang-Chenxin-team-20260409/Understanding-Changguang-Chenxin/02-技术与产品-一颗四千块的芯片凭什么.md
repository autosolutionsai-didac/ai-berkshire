# What Justifies a 4,000-Yuan Chip

> "Understanding Gpixel" series · Article 02 · Technology and products
> Reading time about 10 minutes

---

## Same image sensor, hundred-fold price gap

The CMOS image sensor (CIS) in your smartphone camera and the one in the machine that inspects chip defects on a factory production line are the same basic device. The former costs a few dozen yuan, the latter four thousand yuan — a gap of nearly three hundred times. Both are "chips that turn light into electrical signals," so why the enormous price difference?

Roughly **9 billion** CIS units ship globally each year, with a market size of over **$30 billion**, but **more than 90% of that is consumer-grade** — smartphones, automotive, security. High volume, low unit price, a relatively mature technology bar — Sony alone captures **43%** of global share, with Samsung and OmniVision (under Will Semiconductor) close behind.

(Source: Yole Group, Frost & Sullivan)

Gpixel isn't in that business.

It operates in the remaining **under 10%** — **industrial-grade and scientific-grade CIS**. This is an entirely different world:

| Dimension | Consumer CIS (mobile/auto) | Industrial/scientific CIS (Gpixel) |
|------|----------------------|-------------------------|
| Unit price | **A few to a few dozen yuan** | **Hundreds to tens of thousands of yuan** (averaging about RMB 4,000/unit) |
| Annual shipment volume | Billions of units | Millions of units |
| Global market | ~$27 billion | ~$2.5-3.3 billion |
| Key metrics | Pixel count, photo quality | Noise, dynamic range, frame rate, quantum efficiency |
| Customers | Phone makers, automakers | Industrial equipment makers, scientific instrument makers, medical device makers |
| Qualification cycle | 3-6 months | **6-18 months** |
| Cost of switching suppliers | Low | **Extremely high** (requires redesigning the entire piece of equipment) |

**An analogy**: consumer CIS is like a mass-market sedan — mature technology, high volume, thin margins. Industrial/scientific CIS is like the core component of a surgical robot — few makers, high technical barriers, and once a customer adopts it, replacement is very difficult.

This is the fundamental reason Gpixel can sell a chip for **RMB 4,000** with a **67%** gross margin: **it isn't fighting a price war in a red ocean — it's operating in a small pond with an extremely high technical barrier, doing what only a few players can do.**

## What does a 67% gross margin actually tell us?

In the chip design industry, gross margin is the most direct measure of "pricing power."

| Company | Main focus | Gross margin |
|------|---------|--------|
| **Gpixel** | **Industrial/scientific CIS** | **~67%** |
| Will Semiconductor (OmniVision) | Consumer + automotive-grade CIS | ~30% |
| SmartSens | Security + industrial CIS | ~25% |
| GalaxyCore | Consumer CIS | ~20% |
| Sony Semiconductor | Full-range CIS | ~40% |

(Source: each company's financial reports/prospectuses)

Gpixel's gross margin is **2-3 times** that of its domestic CIS peers, and even significantly higher than Sony's.

This isn't because Gpixel "controls costs well" — as a fabless company, its manufacturing costs are set by foundry quotes, leaving little room for negotiation. **A 67% gross margin has only one explanation: the product sells at a premium price, and customers are willing to pay it.**

Why are customers willing to pay it? Because in industrial/scientific-grade CIS, a slight drop in performance means:

- A machine-vision inspection line's **missed-detection rate** rises, costing a production line millions extra per year
- An astronomical telescope's **quantum efficiency** falls short, extending observation time several-fold
- A medical CT scanner's **noise** runs too high, affecting diagnostic accuracy

**In these scenarios, the sensor's price is a small share of total system cost (typically <5%), but its impact on system performance is decisive.** So customers are price-insensitive but extremely performance-sensitive.

This is the fundamental nature of the high-end CIS business: **a small share of cost, a large share of value.**

**Counter-argument**: a 67% gross margin looks great, but there's a hidden concern — gross margin fell steadily from 76% in 2022 down to 59% in 2024, then rebounded to 67% in 2025 only in 2025. This V-shaped swing suggests gross margin isn't stable, and may be heavily influenced by product mix (a higher share of scientific-grade products lifts margins) and foundry cost fluctuations. If the share of industrial-grade products (relatively lower margin) continues to rise going forward, gross margin could come under pressure again.

## Nine major product lines: what does Gpixel actually make?

Gpixel currently has **9 major product lines and 50+ standard products**, covering a wide range of applications from industrial inspection to astronomical observation.

| Line | Positioning | Core applications | Technical features |
|------|------|---------|---------|
| **GMAX** | Large-format global shutter | Machine vision, flat-panel inspection (display panels/PCBs) | Global shutter eliminates motion blur, large sensor area covers wide field of view |
| **GSPRINT** | High-speed line scan | High-speed sorting, print inspection | High frame rate, high data throughput |
| **GSENSE** | High-sensitivity area array | Astronomical observation, fluorescence microscopy, spectrometers | Extremely low noise, high quantum efficiency |
| **GLUX** | Low-power, small pixel | Drones, portable devices | Low power + miniaturization |
| **GCINE** | Cinema-grade imaging | Digital cinema cameras | Ultra-high dynamic range |
| **GTOF** | Time-of-flight 3D | 3D measurement, robot obstacle avoidance | ToF depth sensing |
| **GL/GLR** | Line-array sensors | OCT (optical coherence tomography), industrial line scanning | High near-infrared quantum efficiency |
| **GXS** | X-ray | Medical X-ray, CT detectors | Large pixels, high sensitivity |
| **GIR** | Near-infrared | Night vision, SWIR imaging | Extended spectral response |

(Source: Gpixel's official website, gpixel.com)

A few points worth highlighting:

**1. The GMAX line is the revenue mainstay.** Industrial imaging contributes **~66%** of revenue, and the GMAX line's large-format global shutter sensors address the core need in machine vision — automated inspection equipment on factory lines needs to capture every part, every panel, sharply even at high speed.

**2. The GSENSE line is the technological crown jewel.** Scientific imaging contributes **~29%** of revenue but has the highest barrier to entry. Sensors used at observatories require extremely low readout noise (<1 electron) and ultra-high quantum efficiency (>95%) — few companies worldwide can hit this level.

**3. The GLR1002BSI-S, just launched in March 2026** — a back-illuminated line-array sensor designed specifically for OCT (optical coherence tomography). OCT is a core technology in ophthalmic diagnosis and intravascular imaging, and this product targets the medical-imaging market.

**4. The GXS line (X-ray sensors)** — a core component in medical CT and industrial NDT (non-destructive testing). Its revenue share is currently only about **3%**, but the global medical-imaging CIS market is about **$0.8 billion**, making it a potential growth pole.

## What core technologies has Gpixel mastered?

The prospectus and public materials show that Gpixel has mastered the following core technologies:

| Technology | Difficulty | Competitive position |
|------|------|---------|
| **Global shutter pixel design** | High | A foundational capability for industrial CIS, on par with Sony and onsemi |
| **HDR (high dynamic range) pixels** | High | Covers both industrial and cinema-grade needs |
| **High-sensitivity/low-noise circuitry** | Extremely high | Core competitiveness for scientific grade |
| **High-speed readout circuitry** | High | Supports high frame-rate industrial inspection |
| **TDI (time delay integration) sensors** | High | Core line-scan technology, used in high-speed sorting |
| **BSI (back-side illumination) process** | Extremely high | Requires foundry cooperation, substantially raises quantum efficiency |
| **3D stacking** | Extremely high | The direction for next-generation high-performance CIS |
| **ToF 3D imaging** | High | Robotics and autonomous-driving assistance |

(Source: prospectus, company website)

The most notable items here are **BSI (back-side illumination)** and **3D stacking**.

Traditional CIS is "front-side illuminated" (FSI) — light must pass through the metal wiring layer to reach the photosensitive area, resulting in significant light loss. BSI flips the chip so light strikes the photosensitive area directly, substantially raising quantum efficiency. 3D stacking manufactures the photosensitive layer and the logic layer separately, then stacks them together to achieve higher performance.

**These two technologies form the "hard threshold" for high-end CIS.** Sony already uses them at scale in consumer grade, but in industrial/scientific grade, Gpixel is one of the few manufacturers capable of it. Multiple products in the GSENSE line use the BSI process, and the "BSI" in the name of the newly launched GLR1002BSI-S in 2026 reflects exactly this.

## The Leica partnership: gimmick or proof of capability?

On April 20, 2026 (the third day after listing), Gpixel announced a strategic partnership with Germany's **Leica Camera**, custom-developing high-performance CMOS image sensors for next-generation Leica cameras.

(Source: IT Home, company website)

The timing of this news has a "marketing" flavor to it — announcing it right after listing, with a clear effect of goosing the stock price. But setting timing aside, the signal value of the event itself is real:

**1. Leica is extremely picky about choosing suppliers.** Leica's sensors have historically come mainly from Belgium's CMOSIS (later acquired by ams) and Sony. Choosing a Chinese company to custom-design its sensors indicates that Gpixel's design capability has reached the standard demanded by a top-tier camera brand.

**2. Wang Xinyang's personal ties.** After his PhD, he worked at CMOSIS, where he led the pixel design of the CMV2000/CMV4000 — the very sensor platforms used in early Leica digital cameras. Gpixel's partnership with Leica is, in a sense, a continuation of Wang Xinyang's own accumulated technical experience.

**3. Three-site collaborative R&D.** The announcement mentioned engineering collaboration spanning Wetzlar (Leica headquarters), Antwerp (Gpixel's Belgian subsidiary), and Changchun. Gpixel has a subsidiary in Antwerp, Belgium — a legacy it inherited from the original CMOSIS technical team.

**Counter-argument**: the camera-grade CIS market is extremely small (under $1 billion globally), and even if the Leica partnership succeeds, its contribution to revenue will be limited. Custom development cycles are long and volumes are small — the value here is more about brand endorsement than financial contribution. This shouldn't be given excessive weight.

## The truth about the technology gap: how far is Gpixel really from Sony?

This is one of the questions investors care about most. The answer is: **it depends on the dimension**.

| Dimension | Sony | Gpixel | Gap |
|------|------|---------|------|
| Consumer CIS | Global dominant leader (43% share) | Doesn't compete | Not comparable |
| Industrial CIS product-line breadth | Extremely wide | Wide (9 major lines) | Sony is more comprehensive |
| Individual performance metrics | Top tier | **Some metrics rival Sony directly** | Partial parity |
| BSI/stacking process | In-house fabs, leading process | Depends on foundries, process-constrained | **Clear gap** |
| Manufacturing capability | IDM, independent capacity | Fabless, capacity subject to others | **Enormous gap** |
| Economies of scale | Annual revenue at the ~RMB 120 billion level | Annual revenue RMB 857 million | 140x |
| R&D investment | Annual R&D >RMB 20 billion | Annual R&D ~RMB 200 million | 100x |

(Source: Sony's annual report, Gpixel's prospectus)

**On design capability, Gpixel is already close to the first tier** — this is confirmed by three facts: a 67% gross margin, third-place global share, and the Leica partnership.

**But on manufacturing capability and scale, the gap is orders of magnitude** — Sony is an IDM (with its own fabs), while Gpixel is fabless. Sony spends RMB 20 billion a year on R&D alone; Gpixel's total revenue is only RMB 857 million.

This means: **Gpixel's competitive advantage comes from "focus on a niche high-end market + accumulated technology + customer stickiness," not from "comprehensively surpassing the giants technologically."** Once a giant decides to seriously invest in the industrial/scientific CIS track (Sony is already doing this), the depth of Gpixel's moat will be tested.
