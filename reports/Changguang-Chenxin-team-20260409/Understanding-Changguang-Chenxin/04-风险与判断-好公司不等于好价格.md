# A Good Company Is Not the Same as a Good Price

> *Understanding Gpixel* Series · Part 04 · Risk and Judgment (Finale)
> Reading time: about 12 minutes

---

## Three Parts In, and the Hardest Question Is Still Unanswered

By this point, many investors already accept Gpixel's case: the technology is real, the gross margin is real, and the No. 3 global ranking is real. The natural next question is — so, do you buy it?

This part is dedicated to answering that question, but not by asking "should you buy" — instead, it **lays out every reason you could lose money on this stock, one by one, and then checks whether the current price already compensates for those risks**. The first three parts covered "what this company has"; this one covers "where this company could go wrong, and whether it's expensive right now."

The conclusions of the first three parts were: 67% gross margin, 9 major product series, No. 3 globally, a small pond, and a fabless lifeline held overseas. The question now is harder: **How big are the risks? Is the current price worth it?**

## Five Layers of Risk, Unpacked One by One

### Layer One: Foundry Dependence — The Deadliest Cut

Gpixel operates a fabless model — all chip manufacturing is outsourced. The problem isn't the model itself, but **who it's outsourced to**:

| Stage | Main Supplier | Country |
|------|----------|--------|
| Wafer foundry | Tower Semiconductor (Tower) | Israel |
| Wafer foundry | DB HiTek | South Korea |
| Packaging | KFT | Japan |

In 2025, the top five suppliers were **entirely overseas companies**, accounting for over **70%** of procurement, with reliance on the single largest supplier reaching **50.1%**.

(Sources: prospectus, National Business Daily)

**What does this mean?**

If US chip sanctions escalate further — not just restricting advanced process nodes but also specialty CIS processes — Tower Semiconductor and DB HiTek could be barred from providing foundry services to Gpixel. In that scenario, the company would face the extreme outcome of **capacity dropping to zero**.

Even if sanctions don't directly target Gpixel, foundries may **proactively limit cooperation** out of their own compliance considerations — this kind of "soft decoupling" is harder to predict and harder to respond to.

**What is the company doing about it?**

1. Building its own packaging lines through subsidiary Changguang Yuanxin; the in-house packaging ratio had risen to **41%** by 2025
2. Advancing joint tape-out efforts with domestic wafer foundries
3. **55%** of IPO proceeds are earmarked for R&D investment — some of which relates to process adaptation

**But the reality is**: high-end CIS wafer fabrication requires a specialized image-sensor process (not a general logic process). Very few domestic foundries can provide this process, and their process maturity lags overseas foundries. Localizing packaging is achievable; localizing wafer fabrication is not achievable in the short term.

**Risk rating: Medium-high probability + fatal impact. This is Gpixel's single largest risk.**

**Counterargument**: Tower Semiconductor is based in Israel (a US ally, but not US soil) and DB HiTek is based in South Korea — neither is directly subject to US export-control jurisdiction. Reaching these two companies with sanctions would require broader secondary sanctions, and there is no clear sign of that happening yet. Moreover, CIS is not an AI chip and is not at the core focus of sanctions.

### Layer Two: Market Ceiling — The Pond Is Only So Big

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| Global industrial + scientific + medical CIS, combined | ~**$3.3 billion** |
| Gpixel's 2025 revenue | ~**$120 million** |
| Current global share (industrial + scientific, weighted) | ~**15%** |
| Optimistic assumption: share doubles to 30% | Revenue of about **$700 million-$1.0 billion** |
| Corresponding net profit (at a 30% net margin) | About **$200-300 million** (RMB 1.5-2.0 billion) |

Even under the most optimistic assumptions — a doubled share and steady growth in the global market — Gpixel's net-profit ceiling is roughly **RMB 1.5-2.0 billion**.

Against a current market cap of roughly **HKD 30.0 billion** (~RMB 27.5 billion), even reaching that ceiling would only compress the PE to **14-18x**. For a stock that would take many years to reach that ceiling — with major risks along the way — that return profile isn't especially attractive.

**The one real variable is** whether Gpixel can successfully expand into **medical CIS** (a ~$800 million market) and **automotive CIS** (a ~$4.0+ billion market). If it can, the pond grows from $3.3 billion to $8.0+ billion, and the story changes completely.

But right now, medical CIS contributes less than **3%** of revenue, and automotive CIS has no substantive products yet. This is a "possible future," not a "proven present."

**Risk rating: High probability + moderate impact. The ceiling won't kill the company, but it will cap long-term returns.**

### Layer Three: Related-Party Transactions and Governance Structure

Gpixel was spun off from the **Changchun Institute of Optics, Fine Mechanics and Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CIOMP)**. Founder Wang Xinyang joined CIOMP in 2012 before founding the company. This lineage has left behind two legacy issues:

**1. Related-party transactions: discrepancies between the A-share and Hong Kong prospectuses**

During the STAR Market review, regulators focused closely on transactions between Gpixel and CIOMP and its affiliates. According to Sina Finance, related-party transaction amounts differ "substantially" between the A-share and Hong Kong prospectuses. Although the company has carried out remediation, this kind of inconsistency is itself a governance signal.

(Sources: Sina Finance, STAR Market inquiry letter)

**2. Husband-and-wife control plus multiple concurrent roles**

Wang Xinyang and his wife Zhang Yanxia jointly control **49.53%** of the equity. Zhang Yanxia simultaneously serves as deputy general manager, board secretary, COO, and joint company secretary — one person holding four roles.

The upside is fast decision-making and strong execution. The downside is a lack of effective internal checks and balances — when a founding couple controls nearly half the equity and holds all the core management posts, the mechanisms protecting minority shareholders are relatively weak.

**3. Compensation controversy**

Wang Xinyang's 2023 compensation reached **RMB 365 million**, most of it share-based payment. While share-based payment is a one-time item (it doesn't mean he takes this much cash every year), the figure still drew market attention at a company with RMB 170 million in annual net profit.

(Source: Beiduo Finance)

**Risk rating: Medium probability + high impact. Not a fatal risk, but one that requires ongoing monitoring.**

**Counterargument**: Related-party transactions are common at companies with research-institute backgrounds, and the issue is manageable after remediation. Husband-and-wife control is not unusual among private chip companies either (such as Yu Renrong at Will Semiconductor). The share-based payment in the compensation figure is a one-time item and does not affect operating cash flow.

### Layer Four: Withdrawing from the STAR Market and Switching to Hong Kong

Gpixel applied for a STAR Market listing in 2023, withdrew in January 2025 after going through the inquiry process, and then pivoted to Hong Kong, successfully listing there in April 2026.

**Stated reason**: The company said it "voluntarily withdrew based on future development strategy and other factors."

**More likely reasons**:

1. STAR Market review has tightened — the reasonableness of a valuation in the tens of billions was directly questioned, and related-party transactions were repeatedly probed
2. The Hong Kong market has higher tolerance for related-party transactions, and valuation anchors are more flexible
3. The Hong Kong IPO window was more favorable — a lineup of 24 cornerstone investors shows strong underwriting capability
4. Hong Kong allows red-chip structures and more flexible equity arrangements

**This switch is not, by itself, a negative signal**, but it's worth investors thinking about: why did the company choose the market with relatively looser review? Under the stricter STAR Market review, which questions were never fully answered?

**Risk rating: Low probability (already successfully listed) + moderate signal value.**

### Layer Five: Technology-Shift Risk

| Potential Substitute Technology | Maturity | Threat Timeline |
|------------|--------|-----------|
| Event cameras | Medium | 5-10 years |
| Quantum image sensors | Low | 10+ years |
| SPAD array sensors | Medium | 5-8 years |

Technology in industrial CIS turns over much more slowly than in consumer CIS — factories won't swap out an entire inspection line just to chase new technology. But over a 10-year horizon, new sensor types such as event cameras could replace traditional CIS in specific scenarios (high-speed motion detection, extreme lighting environments).

**Risk rating: Low probability + moderate impact. Not a near-term threat, but worth watching over the long term.**

## Valuation: What Exactly Is a 54x PE Pricing In?

### Current Valuation

| Metric | Value |
|------|------|
| IPO offer price | HKD 39.88 |
| First-day closing price | ~HKD 70 |
| PE at IPO offer price (2025E) | **~54.6x** |
| PE at first-day closing price | **~100x+** |
| PS (at IPO offer price) | **~18.6x** |

### Comparable Companies

| Company | PE (TTM) | Gross Margin | Revenue Scale | Track |
|------|---------|--------|---------|------|
| Will Semiconductor | ~35x | ~30% | ~RMB 25.0 billion | Consumer + automotive-grade CIS |
| SmartSens | ~45x | ~25% | ~RMB 4.0 billion | Security + industrial CIS |
| GalaxyCore | ~30x | ~20% | ~RMB 5.0 billion | Consumer-grade CIS |
| Sony Semiconductor | ~20x | ~40% | ~RMB 120.0 billion | Full-range CIS |
| **Gpixel** | **~55x (IPO)** | **~67%** | **~RMB 860 million** | **Industrial/scientific CIS** |

(Sources: company financial reports, Wind)

Gpixel's PE is significantly higher than all comparable companies. The market's logic for the high valuation is:

1. **Highest gross margin** (67% vs. 20-40% for peers), indicating strong product pricing power
2. **Scarcity premium** — the only pure-play industrial CIS name in Hong Kong
3. **Growth expectations** — 2025 profit grew 49%, and the market is paying up for continued high growth
4. **The domestic-substitution narrative** — the geopolitical dividend has not yet been fully realized

**But this logic has several holes**:

- How many years can 49% growth continue? The precedent of near-zero revenue growth in 2023 shows that growth is volatile and the semiconductor industry is cyclical
- The scarcity premium is a double-edged sword — scarcity also means a small market, weak liquidity, and few comparable anchors
- Domestic substitution is a long-cycle logic that won't materialize in sudden jumps over the short term

### Three-Scenario Valuation

| Scenario | Assumption | 2028 Net Profit | Target PE | Implied Market Cap | Change (vs. first-day close) |
|------|------|-------------|---------|---------|------------------|
| Optimistic | 30% annual growth, medical breakthrough | ~RMB 640 million | 60x | ~HKD 38.5 billion | +27% |
| Neutral | 20% annual growth, steady share gains | ~RMB 510 million | 45x | ~HKD 22.8 billion | -25% |
| Pessimistic | 10% annual growth, ceiling becomes apparent | ~RMB 390 million | 30x | ~HKD 11.7 billion | -62% |

(Based on the first-day closing market cap of ~HKD 30.4 billion; 2028 net profit is calculated as compound growth from the 2025 base of RMB 293 million)

**In the neutral scenario, buying at the first-day close could mean a 25% loss three years later.** Only in the optimistic scenario (30% annual growth sustained for 3 years, plus a medical/automotive breakthrough, plus the market awarding a 60x PE) is there a 27% return — and that assumption is overly aggressive.

**Calculated from the IPO offer price of HKD 39.88**, the picture is a bit better: the neutral scenario implies about HKD 52 (+30%), and the pessimistic scenario implies about HKD 27 (-32%).

## What Price Is Worth Paying Attention To?

This series does not give a precise "target price" — because a precise target price gives readers a false sense of certainty. But here are a few reference anchor points:

| Price Range (HKD) | Corresponding PE (2025E) | Judgment |
|----------------|-----------------|------|
| 70+ (near first-day close) | 100x+ | **Overheated** — pure sentiment pricing |
| 45-55 | 60-75x | **Expensive** — needs 3 years of 30%+ growth to digest |
| 35-40 (near the IPO offer price) | 48-55x | **Reasonably expensive** — consensus market pricing |
| 28-32 | 38-44x | **Margin of safety starts to appear** — worth watching |
| 22-28 | 30-38x | **Attractive** — risk compensation starts to become adequate |
| <22 | <30x | **A good price, if fundamentals haven't deteriorated** |

**The key takeaway from this table**: buying at the IPO offer price (HKD 39.88 / PE ~55x), you're paying a price that only makes sense with flawless growth. Buying at the first-day closing price (~HKD 70 / PE ~100x), you're paying a pure sentiment premium.

If you believe in Gpixel's long-term value, the most rational move is to **wait** — wait for market sentiment to cool, wait for the first full post-listing quarterly report to validate growth, wait for the price to fall back below HKD 30.

## Final Judgment

Summed up in one table:

| Dimension | Conclusion |
|------|------|
| **Is this a good business?** | **Yes** — 67% gross margin, genuine technology barriers, strong B2B customer stickiness |
| **Is this a good company?** | **Largely yes** — No. 3 globally, No. 1 in China, and a founder with a solid technical background. But the governance structure isn't fully clean, and related-party transactions carry historical baggage |
| **Is the market big enough?** | **Not big enough** — the addressable market is ~$3.3 billion, and the ceiling is clearly visible, unless it successfully expands into medical/automotive |
| **Are the risks manageable?** | **Foundry dependence is a hard flaw** — overseas foundry reliance exceeds 70%, and sanctions risk is real and cannot be resolved in the short term |
| **Is the current price reasonable?** | **Expensive** — 55x PE at the IPO offer price, and over 100x at the first-day close. A good company, but not a good price |
| **When should you pay attention?** | **When PE falls back below 35-40x** (about HKD 25-30), when risk compensation starts to become adequate |

**In one sentence**: Gpixel is a scarce, good business — but "scarce" does not mean "worth buying at any price." In a small pond with a $3.3 billion ceiling, paying a HKD 30.0 billion market cap for a company earning RMB 300 million a year requires every optimistic assumption to hold at once. The margin of safety in investing lies not in "how good the company is," but in "how low the price is."

## Follow-Up Tracking Checklist

If you decide to track Gpixel over the long term, here are the key signals worth following:

- [ ] **2026 interim report**: Can the 49% profit growth rate from 2025 continue? Can the 67% gross margin be sustained?
- [ ] **Foundry supply chain dynamics**: Changes in US chip sanctions policy — especially restrictions on the CIS category and specialty processes
- [ ] **Domestic foundry progress**: Is the joint tape-out effort with domestic wafer foundries succeeding? How mature is the process?
- [ ] **Medical/automotive CIS progress**: Can new markets be opened up to break through the $3.3 billion ceiling?
- [ ] **Leica partnership execution**: When will the custom chip go into mass production? Will more high-end customers follow?
- [ ] **Related-party transaction disclosure**: Is it standardized and transparent post-listing? Are transactions with CIOMP continuing to decline?
- [ ] **Major shareholder selling**: Selling behavior by CIOMP and pre-IPO shareholders after the lock-up period ends
- [ ] **Customer concentration**: Whether the top-five customer share declines (high concentration = high risk)
