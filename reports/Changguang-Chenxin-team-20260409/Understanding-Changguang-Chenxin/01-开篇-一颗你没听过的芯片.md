## On April 17, 2026, a company you've never heard of slapped Hong Kong stocks in the face

That day, a chip company from Changchun listed on the exchange. Nobody knew it, no broker was pushing it hard, it wasn't trending — the offer price was **HKD 39.88**, the stock opened by jumping straight to **HKD 72**, hit an intraday high of **HKD 85**, a gain of over **113%**, and closed up **75.53%**, putting its market cap at roughly **HKD 30.4 billion**.

HKD 213.8 billion in margin financing subscriptions, the public offering oversubscribed by **1,138 times**, and 24 cornerstone investors lining up to subscribe — these are rare numbers even by the standards of Hong Kong IPO history. Most people who woke up that morning had no idea this company's name.

This company is called **Gpixel** (03277.HK).

Most investors have never heard of this name. That's perfectly normal — it doesn't make smartphone chips, it doesn't make consumer electronics, and it isn't riding any current "hot trend." What it makes is something you've most likely never come into direct contact with: **high-performance CMOS image sensors**, used in factory machine-vision inspection lines, in observatory telescopes, in hospital X-ray machines, and in digital cinema cameras.

**One question: how does a company with only RMB 857 million in annual revenue and RMB 293 million in net profit debut with a market cap of HKD 30.4 billion? At a P/E ratio of over 54x?**

Either the market has gone mad, or this company has something you haven't yet seen.

What this series sets out to do is take it apart and see clearly.

## What is Gpixel? The one-sentence version

> **Gpixel = the only fabless chip design company in China that competes head-on with Sony and onsemi in high-end industrial/scientific CMOS image sensors.**

Breaking down each key term in that sentence:

| Keyword | Meaning |
|-------|------|
| CMOS Image Sensor (CIS) | The chip that converts light signals into electrical signals — the "eye" of every imaging device |
| High-end | Not the kind that costs a few yuan in a smartphone — this is industrial/scientific grade, averaging about **RMB 4,000 per unit** |
| Industrial/scientific | Downstream applications are machine-vision inspection, astronomical telescopes, spectrometers, and medical CT — not consumer electronics |
| Fabless | Only designs the chips, doesn't manufacture them — manufacturing is outsourced to foundries |
| China's only | In this specific sub-segment, there is no second domestic player that can compete |

## A set of numbers that will make you reconsider this company

| Metric | 2024 | 2025 | Change |
|------|---------|---------|------|
| Total revenue | **RMB 673 million** | **RMB 857 million** | **+27%** |
| Net profit | **RMB 197 million** | **RMB 293 million** | **+49%** |
| Gross margin | **58.96%** | **66.93%** | V-shaped rebound |
| Net margin | 29.3% | **34.2%** | Continued improvement |
| Global share of industrial imaging CIS | — | **15.2%** (3rd) | Behind only Sony and onsemi |
| Global share of scientific imaging CIS | — | **16.3%** (3rd) | Behind only comparable giants |
| IPO market cap | — | **~HKD 30.4 billion** | First-day close |
| IPO P/E (2025E) | — | **~54.6x** | On the expensive side |

(Source: Gpixel's Hong Kong IPO prospectus, Frost & Sullivan)

A few key figures worth keeping in mind:

1. **A 67% gross margin** — this is top-tier among chip design companies. By comparison, Will Semiconductor (OmniVision) runs about 30%, and SmartSens about 25%. A 67% gross margin points to one thing: **this company's products carry genuine pricing power**.
2. **Revenue of only RMB 857 million (about $120 million)** — against the global CIS market (**$31.2 billion**), that's a rounding error. This is a "small but excellent" company, not a "large and comprehensive" one.
3. **A P/E of 54.6x** — not cheap. You'd need it to sustain more than 30% growth for five straight years to digest this valuation to a reasonable level.

Put these three numbers together and you get Gpixel's core contradiction: **a good business, a small pond, an expensive price.**

## Three common misconceptions about Gpixel

### Misconception one: "Just another domestic-chip concept stock"

There are too many chip companies going public under the banner of "domestic substitution," and it's understandable that investors have grown fatigued. But Gpixel differs from most domestic chip companies in one fundamental way:

**It isn't fighting a price war in the low-end market — it's taking share directly from Sony and onsemi in the high end, and it has already grabbed the No. 3 spot globally.**

Frost & Sullivan data shows that by 2024 revenue, Gpixel ranked third globally in industrial imaging CIS (**15.2%**) and third in scientific imaging CIS (**16.3%**) — both the top-ranked Chinese company in their respective markets.

Its products average about **RMB 4,000 per unit**, tens or even hundreds of times the price of consumer-grade CIS. Being able to command that price isn't a function of "cheap and domestic" — it comes from technical specs that genuinely hold up.

**Counter-argument**: a 15% global share sounds good, but the entire global industrial/scientific CIS market is only about **$2.5-3.3 billion**. Taking 15% of that is only **$400-500 million** in revenue. The ceiling on this track is plainly visible.

### Misconception two: "Fabless model = asset-light, good business"

The fabless (no in-house wafer fab) model does indeed mean an asset-light structure with high ROE potential. But under the current geopolitical environment, the fabless model has one **fatal weakness**:

**Your fate rests in the foundry's hands.**

Gpixel's wafer manufacturing relies heavily on Israel's **Tower Semiconductor** and South Korea's **DB HiTek** — together accounting for **47.5%** of purchases in 2024; packaging relies heavily on Japan's **KFT**. In 2025, all of the top five suppliers were overseas companies, together accounting for more than **70%** of purchases, with the single largest supplier accounting for **50.1%**.

(Source: Gpixel prospectus, National Business Daily)

If US chip sanctions were to expand, or if foundries voluntarily restricted cooperation for compliance reasons, Gpixel could find itself with **nothing to sell**. This isn't a theoretical risk — it's written in black and white in the risk-factor section of the prospectus.

**The good news is**: the company is already taking steps to protect itself — building its own packaging line through its subsidiary Gpixel Chip Assembly, with the in-house packaging ratio already up to **41%** in 2025, while also advancing tape-out cooperation with domestic foundries. But domestic substitution for wafer fabrication is far harder than for packaging, and the company cannot fully shed its overseas dependence in the short term.

### Misconception three: "A 1,138x oversubscribed IPO = a good company"

The oversubscription multiple reflects market sentiment, not company quality.

HKD 213.8 billion in margin financing, a public offering oversubscribed 1,138 times, a 75% first-day gain — these numbers are genuinely explosive. But coming back to fundamentals: a company earning **RMB 293 million** a year debuted with a market cap of **HKD 30.4 billion** — implying a P/E of over **100x** (based on the first-day closing price).

**The market isn't pricing 2025's profit — it's pricing profit expectations for 2028 or even 2030.** If future growth falls short of expectations, a valuation pullback is inevitable.

## The founding story of a 45-year-old Zhejiang University graduate

Understanding Gpixel is impossible without understanding its founder, **Wang Xinyang**.

| Time | Event |
|------|------|
| ~2003 | Graduated from Zhejiang University with a bachelor's degree in Applied Electronics |
| ~2005-2009 | Pursued a PhD at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands, specializing in CMOS image sensors |
| During PhD | Collaborated on CIS R&D with his advisor (head of Philips' imaging division in the Netherlands) |
| After graduation | Joined Belgium's CMOSIS NV, leading the pixel design of the CMV2000/CMV4000, two sensors that shaped the company's fate |
| 2012 | Returned to China to join the Changchun Institute of Optics, Fine Mechanics and Physics (CIOMP) under the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and co-founded Gpixel with Changchun UP Optotech and Luxshare Optics |
| 2012-2022 | Raised no outside funding for a full decade, relying on product revenue to sustain the company |
| 2023 | Applied to list on the STAR Market, withdrew after receiving regulatory inquiries |
| April 2026 | Listed in Hong Kong, up 75% on the first day |

(Source: prospectus, 36Kr, Tencent News)

A few details worth noting:

1. **Delft University of Technology** — this Dutch university is a world-class center for lithography technology and one of the technical origins of ASML, the lithography-machine giant. It was here that Wang Xinyang first encountered CIS technology.
2. **CMOSIS** — this Belgian company was later acquired by ams (now ams-OSRAM) and was an important force in European industrial-grade CIS. It was here that Wang Xinyang built up complete experience from design through to mass production.
3. **A decade without raising funding** — in a Chinese semiconductor scene where "burning cash for growth" is the norm, this is highly unusual. It shows the company had self-sustaining cash generation from an early stage, and that the founder was extremely cautious about diluting equity.
4. **Husband-and-wife control of 49.53%** — Wang Xinyang and his wife Zhang Yanxia together control nearly half of the equity, with Zhang Yanxia simultaneously serving as Deputy General Manager, Board Secretary, COO, and Joint Company Secretary. The upside is efficient decision-making; the downside is a governance structure that isn't especially clean.

**On April 20, 2026** (just 3 days after listing), Gpixel announced a strategic partnership with Germany's **Leica Camera**, developing custom high-performance CMOS image sensors for Leica's next-generation camera products. This is the first time a domestic CIS maker has custom-designed a sensor chip for a top-tier camera brand.

(Source: IT Home, company website)

## A small hook: is Gpixel expensive or not?

Here's a set of numbers to let you form your own impression. These will be unpacked in detail in the articles that follow:

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| 2025 revenue | **RMB 857 million** (+27%) |
| 2025 net profit | **RMB 293 million** (+49%) |
| Gross margin | **66.93%** |
| P/E at IPO offer price | **~54.6x** |
| P/E at first-day close | **~100x+** |
| Total addressable global market | **~$2.5-3.3 billion** |
| Current global share (industrial imaging) | **~15%** |
| Overseas foundry dependence | **>70%** |
| Average product unit price | **~RMB 4,000** |

Putting these together:

- A company with a 67% gross margin, ranked top three globally in its niche — scarce
- But annual revenue is only RMB 857 million, and the global market ceiling is $3 billion — the pond is small
- IPO priced at 54x P/E, then bid up to over 100x on the first day — expensive
- Its manufacturing lifeline is overseas, and the sanctions risk is real — fragile

**"Scarce" and "small" are not contradictory, and neither are "good" and "expensive." The question is: how much of a premium are you willing to pay for "scarce"? And can you tolerate the tail risk that comes with "fragile"?**
