# A Survey of Foundation Model Training Methodology, 2025-2026: From DeepSeek V4 to Kimi K2.5

## I. Introduction

2025-2026 has been two years of structural change in the foundation-model competitive landscape. If 2024's theme was "the rise of MoE and open-source catch-up," then the theme of 2025-2026 is "architecture innovation diversifying faster, post-training paradigms shifting dramatically, and Agent capability becoming a core competitive dimension."

**On the international front**, Anthropic's Claude series continues to hold the top spot; OpenAI's GPT series remains highly competitive; Google's Gemini series keeps pushing hard on multimodality. The capability ceiling of frontier closed-source models is still rising fast.

**On the domestic front**, competitive intensity has far exceeded expectations. DeepSeek V4 broke through simultaneously on attention compression with sparse selection and on online policy distillation, pioneering a new "distillation replaces RL" paradigm; Qwen 3 refreshed the field's understanding of training efficiency with minimalist RL (a large boost in math reasoning from just 3,995 queries and 170 steps); Kimi K2/K2.5 built a distinctive moat in Agent capability training; GLM-5 made differentiated progress on domestic-chip adaptation and asynchronous Agent RL infrastructure; MiniMax M3's sparse attention achieved a 28.4x reduction in attention compute; Hunyuan TurboS boldly adopted a Mamba-Transformer hybrid architecture; and Step 3.5 Flash and MiMo-V2-Flash each carved out their own niche in the small-model race.

**Models covered in this article**: DeepSeek V4 Pro/Flash, Qwen 3, Kimi K2, Kimi K2.5, GLM-5, MiniMax M3, Hunyuan TurboS, Step 3.5 Flash, MiMo-V2-Flash — 9 model families in total.

**Technical sources**: the official technical reports and arXiv papers of the models above. The level of detail varies considerably — the technical reports for DeepSeek V4, GLM-5, and Qwen 3 are the most thorough; MiniMax M3 and Step 3.5 Flash come next; Hunyuan TurboS and MiMo-V2-Flash's reports have a moderate information density; and Kimi K2/K2.5's reports are richest in information on Agent training.

**What you'll get from reading this article**: a panoramic understanding of the technical roadmaps of 9 representative foundation models released in 2025-2026 across five dimensions — architecture design, attention mechanisms, training strategy, post-training methodology, and Agent-capability training; a deep understanding of core trends such as "sparse attention blooming in a hundred flowers," "distillation replacing RL," and "Muon displacing AdamW"; and, for practitioners, a reference point for which technical directions are worth following and which have already become settled conclusions.

## III. Pretraining

### 3.1 Architectural Evolution

#### 3.1.1 MoE Has Become the Absolute Mainstream

Of the 9 model families, 8 adopt an MoE architecture. Dense architectures have completely disappeared from the new generation of flagship models.

Notable differences worth highlighting:

| Model | Total Experts | Shared Experts | Active Experts | Special Design |
|------|---------|---------|-----------|---------|
| DeepSeek V4 Pro | 384 routed | 1 shared | Top-6 | — |
| DeepSeek V4 Flash | 256 routed | 1 shared | Top-6 | — |
| Qwen 3 | 128 routed | None | Top-8 | Removed shared expert |
| Kimi K2 | 384 routed | 1 shared | Top-8 | Dense first layer, MoE for the remaining 60 layers |
| GLM-5 | 256 routed | — | — | MLA-256 + Muon Split |
| MiniMax M3 | 128 routed | 1 shared | Top-4 | — |
| Step 3.5 Flash | 288 routed | 1 shared | Top-8 | 3 dense layers + 42 MoE layers |
| MiMo-V2-Flash | 256 routed | None | Top-8 | No shared expert |

One interesting point of divergence is **whether to keep the shared expert**: Qwen 3 and MiMo-V2-Flash chose to remove the shared expert, on the grounds that increasing the number of routed experts is more effective than keeping a small number of shared ones; whereas DeepSeek V4, Kimi K2, MiniMax M3, and Step 3.5 Flash all retained 1 shared expert. This design choice remains unsettled.

**Routing mechanism details**

MoE routing is a core design choice that affects both model capability and training stability, and models differ significantly in their routing functions and load-balancing strategies:

**Routing function**: DeepSeek V4 changed V3's Sigmoid activation to Sqrt(Softplus(·)), while Qwen 3 uses standard Softmax gating. Sigmoid/Softplus routing lets each expert score independently (the scores across experts need not sum to a fixed value), whereas Softmax routing normalizes all experts' scores into a probability distribution (summing to 1). The former in theory allows more flexible expert combinations, while the latter provides a clearer competitive signal.

**Load-balancing strategy comparison**:

| Model | Strategy | Core Mechanism |
|------|------|---------|
| DeepSeek V4 | Auxiliary-loss-free + sequence-level balance loss | A learnable bias term guides balancing (not involved in gating-weight computation); balance-loss weight is 0.0001 |
| Qwen 3 | Global batch load-balance loss | Expert frequency f_i is synchronized across micro-batches, via the formula N_E×Σ(f_i×p_i) (where N_E is the total number of experts, f_i is the fraction of times expert i is selected within the current batch, and p_i is the average gating probability the router assigns to expert i), which promotes domain specialization |
| Kimi K2 | Standard auxiliary loss | — |

**Expert granularity choice**: research (arXiv:2505.06839) shows that, holding sparsity constant, increasing MoE expert granularity can exponentially boost a network's expressive capacity. This explains why DeepSeek V4 uses 384 small experts (each with an intermediate dimension of 2048) rather than 64 large experts — finer-grained experts allow for narrower, more specialized divisions of labor. DeepSeek V4 also uses hash routing in its first 3 MoE layers (target experts are determined by a predefined hash mapping of input tokens; the specific hash features are not disclosed in detail in the report), replacing the dense FFN layers used in V3.

**Hunyuan TurboS's outlier route** deserves special discussion. It adopts a 128-layer Mamba-Transformer hybrid architecture, made up of 7 AMF blocks (Attention→Mamba2→FFN) interleaved with a number of MF blocks (Mamba2→FFN), totaling 128 layers, containing 7 attention layers, roughly 57 Mamba2 layers, and 64 FFN/MoE layers (interleaved, not stacked contiguously). Across all 128 layers, attention layers account for only 7, or 5.5%. The core bet behind this aggressive design is that Mamba2's O(n) linear complexity can deliver a fundamental efficiency advantage in long-sequence scenarios, at the cost of needing a very small number of attention layers to make up for Mamba's weakness in precise positional retrieval. Judging by its 7th-place finish on the LMSYS Arena leaderboard with a score of 1356, this architectural choice is viable, but whether it outperforms a pure Transformer+MoE remains to be further evidenced.

A reference data point on inference efficiency: as sequence length grows from 4K to 256K, a Transformer's attention compute grows by roughly 4,096x (quadratic complexity), while Mamba's grows by only 64x (linear complexity). Note, however, that Hunyuan TurboS still retains 7 layers of GQA attention, so its overall complexity is not strictly linear.

#### 3.1.2 Attention Mechanisms: The Divergence from Full Attention to Sparse Attention

Among this round of models, innovation in attention mechanisms is the most exciting technical dimension. The various approaches can be grouped into four schools by design philosophy:

- **Compression school** — compress and reduce the dimensionality of KV first, then compute attention. Representative approach: DeepSeek V4's CSA+HCA
- **Selection school** — don't compress KV, but select the most relevant subset to participate in the computation. Representative approaches: MiniMax M3's MSA, GLM-5's DSA
- **Window school** — use sliding windows to cover local context, with a small number of global layers covering long-range dependencies. Representative approaches: Step 3.5 Flash's MFA+SWA, MiMo-V2-Flash's SWA+GA
- **Linear school** — replace attention entirely with a state space model. Representative approach: Hunyuan TurboS's Mamba2

Each of these four schools has its own strengths and weaknesses, and they are unlikely to converge on a single paradigm in the near term. Each is discussed in turn below.

**DeepSeek V4's CSA+HCA dual mechanism (Compression school)**

Building on MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention, first introduced in DeepSeek V2), DeepSeek V4 proposes two complementary attention mechanisms:

- **CSA (Compressed Sparse Attention)**: the KV pairs of every m=4 adjacent tokens are compressed into 1 via a learnable linear projection that weights and aggregates the adjacent tokens' latent variables within MLA's latent space, and then a lightweight "Lightning Indexer" performs top-k sparse selection on the compressed KV (k=1024 for V4 Pro). This means that within a 1M context, the number of tokens actually participating in attention computation is compressed from the million level down to the thousand level.
- **HCA (Hierarchical Compressed Attention)**: a higher compression ratio (m'=128), but without sparse selection — instead, it performs full dense attention over all compressed KV. This preserves the completeness of global information.

CSA and HCA are used alternately across Transformer layers, forming a complementary pattern of "precise local selection + coarse-grained global coverage." The inference-efficiency gains are extremely significant: V4 Pro's KV cache is only 10% of V3's, and its FLOPs are only 27% of V3.2's.

Moving from the compression school to the selection school, another approach is to skip KV compression altogether and instead directly pick out the most relevant KV subset.

**MiniMax M3's MSA (Selection school)**

MSA (Mixed Sparse Attention) uses a two-branch design:
- **Index branch**: a lightweight selector quickly determines which KV blocks are relevant to the current query
- **Main branch**: performs precise block-sparse attention over the selected KV blocks

Specific parameters: block size 128, with 16 blocks selected per query. At a 1M context length, attention compute is reduced by 28.4x, prefill is accelerated 9x, and decoding is accelerated 15x. Accompanying GPU kernel optimizations include an exponent-free top-k (2.5-5.1x faster than torch.topk) and a KV outer-loop kernel.

Also in the selection school, but taking a lighter-weight path, is GLM-5.

**GLM-5's DSA (Selection school, low adaptation cost)**

DSA (Dynamic Sparse Attention)'s standout advantage is its extremely low adaptation cost: it requires only 20B tokens of continued training to migrate from full attention to sparse attention (compared with the 943.7B-token cost of training DeepSeek V4 from scratch). DSA uses deterministic top-k selection, avoiding the training instability that randomness can introduce.

**GLM-5's MLA improvement: Muon Split**

GLM-5 adopts MLA-256 (head dimension raised from the standard 192 to 256, with the number of attention heads correspondingly reduced by one third), but found that directly applying the Muon optimizer to MLA — orthogonalizing the unified KV projection matrix — causes gradient signals across different attention heads to interfere with one another. Muon Split's solution is to perform matrix orthogonalization separately for each independent attention head within MLA, rather than orthogonalizing a single unified matrix. Ablation experiments show that MLA + Muon Split reaches 62.5 on MMLU (vs. 61.5 for standard MLA and 61.2 for GQA-8) and 51.8 on BBH (vs. 48.9 for MLA), effectively compensating for MLA's limited expressive capacity within an MoE architecture.

Unlike the compression and selection schools, the window school's approach is more straightforward: use sliding windows to handle local context, and perform global attention in only a small number of layers.

**Step 3.5 Flash's MFA+SWA hybrid (Window school)**

Step 3.5 Flash uses a hybrid of sliding window attention (SWA) and global attention, at a ratio of 3:1 (3 out of every 4 layers use SWA, 1 uses global attention). The SWA window size is 512 tokens. Its unique feature is **head-level gated attention** — each attention head can learn its own gating weight to determine the mixing ratio between SWA and global attention.

MiMo-V2-Flash also takes the window-school route, but with a more aggressive parameter choice.

**MiMo-V2-Flash's SWA+GA hybrid (Window school, extreme parameters)**

MiMo-V2-Flash likewise combines SWA with global attention, but at a more extreme ratio: 5:1 (39 of 48 layers use SWA, 9 use GA). The SWA window is only 128 tokens — the smallest window of any model surveyed here. To prevent the attention collapse that such an extremely small window can cause, MiMo introduces a **learnable sink bias**: a learnable bias term is added to specific positions (such as the BOS token), allowing the model to route "garbage attention" to the sink token and avoid degradation of attention weights.

Finally, the most aggressive approach: replace attention entirely with a linear-complexity state space model.

**Hunyuan TurboS's extreme design (Linear school)**

Only 7 of 128 layers are attention layers (GQA, 64 query heads / 8 KV heads), with the rest composed of Mamba2 and FFN/MoE. Mamba2 uses a configuration of d_state=128 and chunk_size=128, providing O(n) linear complexity. This is the design with the lowest proportion of attention layers among all the models surveyed.

**Qwen 3: representative of the conservative route**

Worth noting is that Qwen 3 took a conservative route on attention architecture — standard GQA, with no sparse attention mechanism introduced at all. The QK-Norm mentioned in its technical report is a training-stability technique (normalizing Query and Key vectors to prevent attention-score blow-up), not an innovation at the attention-architecture level. Against a backdrop where other models are actively exploring sparse attention, Qwen 3 achieved strongly competitive results using standard GQA + 36T of data + minimalist RL, showing that attention-architecture innovation is not the only path to improvement.

#### 3.1.3 Redesigning Residual Connections: From V3's HyperConnection to V4's mHC

The problem with the traditional residual connection (y = x + f(x)) is that as network depth increases, the gradient contribution of each layer to the final output can become imbalanced. DeepSeek V3 already introduced the concept of HyperConnection, replacing simple additive residuals with a learnable weight matrix so that each layer's contribution is more balanced. V4 builds further on this with **mHC (manifold-constrained Hyper-Connection)**, adding a doubly-stochastic-matrix manifold constraint to achieve tighter mathematical control over residual connections.

The specifics of mHC:
1. Replace the residual connection with a **doubly stochastic matrix** (every row and every column sums to 1), used to control the mixing weights of each layer's inputs/outputs
2. The doubly-stochastic constraint is enforced via the **Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm** (20 iterations) — a learnable matrix is alternately row-normalized and column-normalized until it converges onto the doubly-stochastic manifold
3. The matrix parameters are dynamically parameterized (rather than fixed values), and can adapt as training proceeds

The doubly stochastic matrix guarantees a "conservation property" of information flow — no layer's contribution is ever over-amplified or over-suppressed. Empirically, mHC adds only 6.7% extra computation time, while significantly improving training stability and performance for deep networks.

#### 3.1.4 Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)

MTP is broadly adopted in this round of models:

- **GLM-5**: 3 shared MTP heads, with an average accepted length of 2.76 at inference time
- **Step 3.5 Flash**: 3 MTP heads, introducing **Fast-MTP position-aware loss** — each prediction head uses a different loss weight depending on which future position it predicts (the 1st, 2nd, or 3rd future token)
- **MiMo-V2-Flash**: at each speculative decoding step, the model accepts an average of 3.6 consecutive tokens, achieving a 2.6x throughput improvement over token-by-token autoregressive generation

The value of MTP is twofold: during training it provides richer gradient signal (predicting multiple future tokens at each position), and during inference it significantly boosts throughput via speculative decoding.

#### 3.1.5 Attention-FFN Decoupled Parallelism (AFD)

AFD (Attention-FFN Decoupling), proposed by Step 3.5 Flash, is an engineering-oriented architectural innovation. In a traditional Transformer, attention layers and FFN layers execute serially. AFD decouples the two into parallel execution: attention and FFN are computed simultaneously, with the results merged at the layer output, reducing per-layer serial latency and better exploiting GPU parallel compute capability at inference time.

**Data mix and curriculum learning**

Neither DeepSeek V4's nor Qwen 3's technical report discloses the precise ratio of English/Chinese/code/math in their training data (this is commercially sensitive information), but a common curriculum-learning pattern can be seen from the training strategies that have been disclosed:

- **Qwen 3**: a three-stage curriculum — Stage 1 (30T) broadly covers general knowledge across 119 languages; Stage 2 (5T) substantially increases the proportion of STEM/code/reasoning data (using Qwen2.5-Math and Qwen2.5-Coder to generate synthetic data); Stage 3 (a few hundred billion) focuses on long context (75% of lengths in the 16K-32K range, 25% in the 4K-16K range)

- **MiMo-V2-Flash**: three stages — 22T of general data → 4T with code up-sampling + 5% synthetic reasoning → 1T of long-context extension

#### 3.2.2 Synthetic Data at Scale

Synthetic data has shifted from being "supplementary" to being a "core component." The most representative examples:

- **Kimi K2's knowledge-rewriting strategy**: raw web text is rewritten by a large model into a more structured, training-friendly format, and this strategy is applied at scale as early as the pretraining stage
- **Kimi K2's Agent data synthesis pipeline**: starting from 3,000+ GitHub MCP (Model Context Protocol) tools, 20,000+ tool definitions are synthesized, from which multi-turn Agent interaction traces are then generated. Based on currently public information, this is the largest-scale Agent training data synthesis scheme available
- **Qwen 3's reasoning-enhancement stage**: out of the 36T total data tokens, 5T are set aside specifically for reasoning enhancement, containing a large volume of synthetic math and code reasoning data

#### 3.2.3 Data Rewriting Strategy

A notable feature of Kimi K2's data engineering is **Knowledge Rewriting**. Raw internet text is often full of noise — ads, navigation bars, irrelevant links — and using it directly for training is not very efficient. Kimi K2 uses an already-trained model to rewrite such text — preserving the core knowledge content but reorganizing it into a format more suitable for language-model learning. The trade-off between the inference compute required for rewriting and the resulting improvement in training efficiency is a question worth further study.

#### 3.3.2 FP8/FP4 Mixed Precision

Low-precision training has deepened further in this round of models. FP8 mixed precision is now common practice and no longer a standalone technical highlight. DeepSeek V4 goes a step further, introducing FP4 quantization-aware training (QAT) for the first time in trillion-parameter pretraining, with the following design:

**Numerical format**: MXFP4 (E2M1) is used — 1 sign bit + 2 exponent bits + 1 mantissa bit, with every 32 elements sharing one E8M0-format scale factor. Dequantization from FP4 to FP8 is lossless (FP8 E4M3 has 2 more exponent bits than FP4 E2M1).

**Quantization locations**: (1) MoE routed-expert weights (the main consumer of GPU memory); (2) the QK path of the CSA indexer (accelerating long-context attention-score computation).

**Training pipeline**: FP32 master weights → quantized to FP4 → dequantized back to FP8 for the forward pass. Gradients are computed on the FP8 weights and propagated back to the FP32 master weights directly via a Straight-Through Estimator (STE). Round-to-nearest is used in the forward pass and stochastic rounding in the backward pass; combining the two improves training stability.

**Effect**: the top-k selector achieves a 2x speedup, while KV-entry recall stays at 99.7%. However, the technical report does not disclose detailed ablation comparison data for FP4 vs. FP8 vs. BF16.

#### 3.3.3 Training Stability

Training stability is a core engineering challenge for large-scale pretraining, and this round of models shows particularly notable innovation on this dimension.

**Kimi K2's MuonClip — zero loss spikes**

MuonClip is a stability constraint added on top of the Muon optimizer: a threshold τ=100 is set on the QK inner products of attention layers, and clipping is applied when the threshold is exceeded. This design allowed Kimi K2 to achieve **zero loss spikes** across the entire 15.5T-token pretraining run — by comparison, most large-scale training runs experience anywhere from a few to dozens of loss spikes, each of which means a degradation of training state and wasted recovery time.

**DeepSeek V4's Foresight Routing + SwiGLU clamping**

DeepSeek V4 employs a dual stability safeguard:
1. **Foresight Routing**: decouples the update of the MoE router from the update of the model backbone. During normal training the two update in sync; when a potential loss-spike signal is detected, router updates pause and only the backbone continues updating. This adds roughly 20% computational overhead, but since it only activates when spike risk is detected, the actual average overhead is far below 20%
2. **SwiGLU clamping**: clamps the output of the SwiGLU activation function to the range [-10, 10], preventing extreme activation values from propagating

**Step 3.5 Flash's Polar Express**

Step 3.5 Flash made dedicated precision optimizations to the Muon optimizer (called Polar Express), experiencing only 1 loss spike across 17.2T tokens of training.

**Stability technique comparison**

| Model | Stability Approach | Number of Loss Spikes | Extra Overhead |
|------|-----------|--------------|---------|
| Kimi K2 | MuonClip (QK-Clip τ=100) | 0 | Very low |
| Step 3.5 Flash | Polar Express | 1 | Undisclosed |
| DeepSeek V4 | Foresight Routing + SwiGLU clamping | Undisclosed | ~20% (at peak) |

#### 3.3.4 Optimizer: Muon Displacing AdamW

The adoption of the Muon optimizer is a notable trend in this round of models:

| Model | Optimizer | Special Configuration |
|------|-------|---------|
| DeepSeek V4 | Muon | 10 Newton-Schulz iterations (first 8 steps fast, last 2 steps precise), hybrid bucket allocation |
| Kimi K2 | MuonClip | Muon + QK-Clip stability constraint |
| GLM-5 | Muon Split | MLA-256 head-wise splitting |
| Step 3.5 Flash | Muon + Polar Express | Precision optimization |

Muon retains a momentum mechanism, but replaces AdamW's element-wise second-moment estimate with Newton-Schulz orthogonalization of the momentum buffer matrix, making the update direction more evenly distributed across the singular-value space of the parameter matrix. DeepSeek V4's implementation details are especially refined: the first 8 Newton-Schulz iterations use a fast but lower-precision method to accelerate convergence, while the last 2 steps switch to a high-precision mode to ensure the quality of the final orthogonalization; hybrid bucket allocation assigns parameter matrices to different compute buckets based on their size, optimizing parallel efficiency.

## IV. Post-Training

### 4.1 SFT Strategy

#### 4.1.1 Comparison of Thinking-Mode Design

"Thinking mode" — the model performing internal reasoning before generating its final answer — has become a standard feature. But there are notable differences in design philosophy across vendors.

**Qwen 3's /think and /no_think**

Qwen 3 adopts the simplest binary design: users control whether the model performs internal reasoning by adding a `/think` or `/no_think` tag to the prompt. This relies on a "thinking-mode fusion" stage in post-training — training data with and without thinking is mixed together, allowing the same model to switch modes according to the instruction.

An important finding from Qwen 3 is that **thinking budget is a naturally emergent capability** — after RL training, the model automatically learns to allocate different lengths of internal reasoning depending on problem difficulty, with no need for an explicit length-control mechanism.

**DeepSeek V4's three reasoning intensities**

DeepSeek V4 provides more fine-grained control:
- **Non-think**: no internal reasoning, answer generated directly (suitable for simple questions)
- **Think High**: a standard depth of internal reasoning
- **Think Max**: maximum-depth internal reasoning (suitable for extremely difficult math/coding problems)

The three intensities are triggered via different system prompts, and the model learns to distinguish between the three modes during the OPD distillation stage.

**GLM-5's three modes**

GLM-5 likewise offers three reasoning modes, but its design philosophy leans more toward "scenario adaptation" than "intensity gradation" — providing different thinking strategies for different task types (such as math reasoning, code generation, general conversation).

**Hunyuan TurboS's adaptive CoT**

Hunyuan TurboS's design is the most aggressive: **adaptive long/short CoT** — the model automatically decides, based on problem difficulty, whether to use a long chain of thought or a short one, with no manual switching required by the user. Empirically, this strategy allows Hunyuan TurboS to reach comparable reasoning performance using only 52.8% of the token volume of DeepSeek-R1, nearly halving inference cost.

**Thinking-mode comparison**

| Model | Number of Modes | Control Method | Distinctive Feature |
|------|-------|---------|---------|
| Qwen 3 | 2 | /think /no_think tags | Thinking budget emerges naturally |
| DeepSeek V4 | 3 | Non-think/Think High/Think Max | Three-level intensity gradient |
| GLM-5 | 3 | Scenario adaptation | — |
| Hunyuan TurboS | Adaptive | No manual switching needed | Only 52.8% of R1's token volume |

#### 4.1.2 Multi-Round Deliberative Learning

Hunyuan TurboS's proposed **Multi-round Deliberative Learning** proceeds in three steps:
1. **Judging stage**: have the model critique its own answer, identifying weaknesses
2. **Weakness deliberation**: for the identified weaknesses, have the model generate an improved answer
3. **Iterative SFT**: use the original answer–critique–improvement triples as training data, and perform SFT iteratively

In essence, this lets the model learn from its own mistakes — similar to the "mistake notebook" method used in human learning.

### 4.2 Post-Training Methodology: RL, Distillation, and Hybrid Paradigms

Post-training methodology shows notable divergence in this round of models: a pure-RL route, a distillation-replaces-RL route, and a hybrid RL-plus-distillation route stand as a three-way split. This section is organized by technical paradigm rather than model-by-model.

#### 4.2.1 The RL Route: GRPO and Its Improvements

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, first introduced by DeepSeek) remains the most widely adopted RL algorithm in this round of models, but vendors have made improvements in different directions on top of it.

**Qwen 3's minimalist GRPO**

Qwen 3's RL results are among the most surprising findings of this round: using just **3,995 queries** (fewer than 4,000 questions) and only **170 steps of GRPO** training, the AIME math competition benchmark rose from 70.1 to 85.1 (+15 points).

This result challenges the widespread belief that "RL requires large amounts of data and long training time." The Qwen 3 team's explanation is: if pretraining and SFT have already established sufficiently strong foundational capability, RL's role is more about "activation" than "teaching" — a small amount of high-quality RL signal is enough to trigger reasoning capability the model already possesses but has not yet fully unlocked.

Qwen 3's post-training follows a four-stage process:
1. **Long-CoT cold start**: SFT on long chain-of-thought data to establish a thinking habit
2. **Reasoning RL**: the minimalist GRPO described above (3,995 queries, 170 steps)
3. **Thinking-mode fusion**: mixing /think and /no_think training data
4. **General RL**: RL on a broader set of tasks

**GLM-5's IcePop — resolving the train/inference distribution mismatch**

IcePop's core insight is that standard RL (such as GRPO) has a systematic difference between the policy used during training, π_train, and the policy used during inference, π_infer — training typically uses a lower temperature, different sampling strategies, or batch-processing optimizations. IcePop explicitly models this mismatch:

1. **Compute the mismatch ratio**: ρ = π_train(y|x) / π_infer(y|x)
2. **Pop-operator clipping**: when ρ falls outside the range [1/β, β] (β=2), the Pop operator completely masks that sample's gradient
3. **Removing KL regularization**: IcePop holds that while KL regularization limits policy drift from the reference model, it also hampers the model's effective exploration in new domains, and removes it outright

This design keeps GLM-5's training stable across all three stages — reasoning RL, Agent RL, and general RL — and, because KL regularization is removed, RL improves faster.

**Step 3.5 Flash's MIS-PO — two-level filtered RL**

MIS-PO (Multi-level Importance Sampling Policy Optimization)'s core innovation is **two-level filtering**:
1. **Trajectory-level filtering**: at the level of the complete response, low-quality trajectories are filtered out based on the reward signal
2. **Token-level filtering**: at the token level, abnormal tokens are further filtered out based on importance-sampling weights

Two-level filtering prevents a small number of anomalous tokens or low-quality trajectories from having an outsized effect on the policy update, making RL training more stable.

**Hunyuan TurboS's two-stage GRPO**

Hunyuan TurboS adopts a two-stage GRPO — 300,000 examples in the reasoning stage plus 160,000 examples in the general stage — embodying a "reasoning first, general second" post-training paradigm: first build strong thinking capability on reasoning tasks, then ensure general conversational ability is not lost via the general stage.

#### 4.2.2 The Distillation-Replaces-RL Route: OPD and MOPD

The role of distillation in this round of models has undergone a qualitative shift — from an "optional efficiency optimization" to a "core training paradigm."

**DeepSeek V4's OPD — fully replacing traditional RL with distillation**

OPD (Online Policy Distillation) is DeepSeek V4's major innovation in post-training paradigm. Its workflow:
1. **Independent training of domain experts**: separate expert models are trained for different domains (math, code, general, etc.), with each expert model going through a full SFT+GRPO pipeline
2. **Online policy distillation and merging**: rather than a simple model merge (such as parameter averaging), **reverse KL divergence** is used to distill the full-vocabulary logit distribution — the student model (the final V4) generates samples under its own policy distribution, then matches the logit distributions of 10+ teacher models

The deeper logic behind this design: traditional RL's reward signal is scalar (a single number measuring how good a response is), whereas distillation's "reward signal" is the probability distribution over the entire vocabulary — several orders of magnitude more informative. This explains why OPD can achieve better results with fewer training steps.

The word "online" in OPD is essential: the student model generates samples under its own distribution (rather than using teacher-generated samples), which avoids the distribution-shift problem present in traditional offline distillation.

More subtly, DeepSeek V4 also introduces a **Generative Reward Model (GRM)**: whereas a traditional reward model is discriminative (input a response, output a score), a GRM is generative (it generates evaluative text, and a score is then extracted from that evaluation). Moreover, DeepSeek V4 also **applies RL to the GRM itself** — using RL to improve the reward model's judgment quality, forming a nested optimization loop.

**MiMo-V2-Flash's MOPD — multi-teacher online distillation**

MOPD (Multi-teacher Online Policy Distillation) belongs to the same online policy distillation paradigm as OPD, but has several distinctive features:
1. **Multi-teacher architecture**: different domains use different expert teacher models, with each teacher providing the highest-quality guidance in its own domain
2. **Token-level reward signal**: whereas traditional RL's reward is trajectory-level (one score for one complete response), MOPD uses the teacher model's token-level logits as the reward signal, refining the granularity of information by 1-2 orders of magnitude
3. **Three-way signal blending**: reverse-KL distillation provides a distribution-matching signal, clipped importance sampling prevents excessive distribution shift, and the ORM (Outcome Reward Model) provides supervision on the correctness of the final answer

A key engineering challenge for MOPD is: in RL training of an MoE model, rollout generation and parameter updates use different versions of the model, causing MoE routing decisions to become inconsistent. MiMo-V2-Flash resolves this with **R3 (Rollout Routing Replay)**: during the parameter-update stage, it replays the routing decisions from the rollout stage, ensuring the consistency of the training signal.

#### 4.2.3 Strong-to-Weak Distillation and Cross-Stage Distillation

**Qwen 3's strong-to-weak distillation**

Qwen 3 adopted an extremely efficient strategy for small-model training: using the logit distribution of the large model (235B) as the teacher signal to distill-train a small model. Key figure: this achieves near-large-model-level performance for the small model using **only 1/10th the GPU training time** of the large model.

This result implies that, given a strong teacher model, the marginal cost of training a smaller model in the same family is extremely low. This explains why Qwen 3 was able to simultaneously release multiple model sizes across both Dense and MoE product lines.

**GLM-5's cross-stage distillation to prevent forgetting**

In its multi-stage post-training, GLM-5 introduces a cross-stage distillation mechanism to prevent forgetting. Post-training is typically split into multiple stages (SFT→RL→alignment, etc.), and training in a later stage can degrade capabilities established in an earlier stage (catastrophic forgetting). GLM-5 retains the model from the previous stage as a "teacher" at every stage, using a distillation signal to constrain the current stage's training from drifting too far from the knowledge of the previous stage.

#### 4.2.4 Post-Training Methodology Comparison

| Model | Paradigm | Method | Core Value |
|------|------|------|---------|
| DeepSeek V4 | Distillation replaces RL | OPD (10+ domain experts, full-vocabulary logit) | Information density far exceeds RL's scalar reward |
| MiMo-V2-Flash | Distillation replaces RL | MOPD (multi-teacher, token-level logit + ORM) | Multi-signal fusion |
| Qwen 3 | RL-primary + distillation-assisted | Minimalist GRPO (3,995 queries/170 steps) + strong-to-weak distillation | RL activates existing capability, distillation propagates it efficiently |
| GLM-5 | RL + distillation to prevent forgetting | IcePop + cross-stage distillation | Resolves distribution mismatch + prevents catastrophic forgetting |
| Step 3.5 Flash | Pure RL | MIS-PO (two-level filtering) | Stable, discretely filtered RL |
| Hunyuan TurboS | Pure RL | Two-stage GRPO (300K reasoning + 160K general) | Reasoning first, then general |

#### 4.2.5 Reward Function Design

The reward function is the core component that determines the direction of optimization in RL training, and models adopt different reward strategies:

**Rule-based verifiable rewards** (math/code):
- Math: the final answer must be placed inside \boxed{}, and it is automatically compared against the reference answer (1 point for correct, 0 for incorrect)
- Code: executed via a compiler and compared against predefined test cases, a binary reward
- Format reward: the model is required to output the reasoning process wrapped in `<think>` and `</think>` tags, ensuring it can be parsed
- GRPO's group-relative advantage estimate A_i = (R_i - mean) / std, which requires no separate Critic model

**DeepSeek V4's Generative Reward Model (GRM)**:
GRM is trained using the SPCT method, and its core difference from a traditional discriminative RM lies in its output form — a discriminative RM directly outputs a scalar score, whereas a GRM generates a "principle → critique → score" text pipeline. GRM based on DeepSeek-GRM-27B achieves 69.9% accuracy on RewardBench (this score is based on the standard RewardBench evaluation set; GRM's advantage lies not in a higher absolute score than discriminative RMs, but in stronger cross-domain generalization and inference-time scalability — its performance can keep improving via multi-sample aggregation, something a discriminative RM cannot do), which rises to 71.0% via 32-round voting aggregation, and further to 72.8% with MetaRM filtering added. More crucially, V4 also applies RL optimization (rule-based RL) to the GRM itself, forming a nested optimization loop.

### 4.3 Agent-Capability Training

Agent capability (i.e., the model's ability to use tools, interact with an environment, and complete complex multi-step tasks) has risen in this round of models from a "bonus feature" to a "core competitive dimension." Kimi K2/K2.5 have invested most deeply in this direction.

#### 4.3.1 Kimi K2's Large-Scale Agent Data Synthesis Pipeline

Kimi K2 has built what is, based on currently public information, the most complete Agent training data synthesis pipeline available:

1. **Tool collection**: real tool definitions are extracted from 3,000+ GitHub MCP tool libraries
2. **Tool expansion**: based on these real tools, 20,000+ tool definitions are synthetically generated — expanding coverage by transforming parameters, combining functionality, and simulating new tools
3. **Trajectory generation**: existing strong models are used to generate multi-turn interaction trajectories on the synthetic tools — including complex scenarios such as tool calls, result parsing, error handling, and multi-step reasoning
4. **Quality filtering**: the generated trajectories are filtered on multiple quality dimensions, retaining only high-quality training data

The key insight of this pipeline is: the bottleneck in training Agent capability lies not in the algorithm but in the data — high-quality Agent interaction data is extremely scarce and must be manufactured at scale via synthesis.

#### 4.3.2 Kimi K2.5's Agent Swarm and PARL Framework

Kimi K2.5 pushes Agent capability further on top of K2, introducing two key innovations.

**Agent Swarm architecture**

Agent Swarm is a multi-Agent collaborative architecture:
- **Orchestrator**: responsible for task decomposition and sub-Agent scheduling
- **Sub-Agents**: each focused on tool use within a specific domain (such as code execution, file operations, search, etc.)
- **Decoupled design**: the orchestrator is trainable, while sub-Agents remain frozen

Training the orchestrator does not require simultaneously training all sub-Agents, which significantly lowers training complexity. At the same time, new sub-Agents can be plugged in without needing to retrain the orchestrator.

**PARL (Parallel Agent Reinforcement Learning) framework**

PARL addresses an efficiency problem in Agent RL training: traditional Agent RL must execute tool calls and environment interactions serially, resulting in extremely low GPU utilization (a great deal of time is spent waiting for the environment to return results). PARL parallelizes the rollout process across multiple Agents, overlapping wait time with compute time, cutting latency by 3-4.5x empirically.

**Toggle heuristic**

Kimi K2.5 also proposes the Toggle heuristic strategy: at inference time, it dynamically decides whether to enable thinking mode based on task characteristics, achieving a 25-30% token reduction with almost no loss of performance.

**Vision Agent capability**

Kimi K2.5's vision capability is based on the **MoonViT-3D vision encoder**: it processes 4-frame spatiotemporal volumes, achieving 4x compression along the temporal dimension. One surprising finding is **zero-shot vision SFT**: performing SFT with text data alone is enough to activate vision reasoning capability, with no visual annotation data required. This suggests that the cross-modal representations built up during pretraining are already sufficiently strong.

Further cross-modal RL enhancement experiments show that visual RL not only improves performance on visual tasks but can also improve performance on pure text tasks — MMLU-Pro +1.7, GPQA +2.1. This hints at a potential positive-transfer effect from cross-modal training signals.

#### 4.3.3 GLM-5's Slime Asynchronous RL Infrastructure

Slime is a dedicated asynchronous framework built by GLM-5 for Agent RL training, addressing Agent RL's core efficiency bottleneck — Agents need to interact with an environment (a code sandbox, terminal, browser, etc.), and each step incurs IO latency, so traditional synchronous RL has extremely low GPU utilization.

Slime's key design elements:
- **Complete decoupling of inference and training**: the inference engine continuously generates Agent trajectories; once the number of trajectories reaches a threshold, they are sent in batches to the training engine, and the training engine pushes new weights back to the inference engine every K updates
- **1,000+ concurrent rollouts**: over 1,000 Agent-environment interactions run simultaneously, with joint training across heterogeneous workloads achieved through microservice-based task registration
- **TITO gateway (Token-In-Token-Out)**: intercepts all generation requests to the inference engine, recording the token IDs and metadata of every trajectory, ensuring the training side uses the exact tokenization produced by the inference engine and avoiding alignment errors caused by re-tokenization
- **DP-aware routing**: uses consistent hashing to route requests from the same rollout to the same data-parallel rank, maximizing KV-cache reuse

GLM-5's Agent RL covers over 10,000 verifiable environments (spanning GitHub repositories across 9 programming languages), using the RepoLaunch framework to automatically analyze repository installation and dependency configuration, and generating test cases via LLM-driven log parsing.

#### 4.3.4 MiniMax M2's Forge RL System

Although MiniMax M3's technical report focuses mainly on architectural innovation, the **Forge RL system** developed in its predecessor version, M2, provides an engineering reference for Agent RL training. Forge is a general-purpose RL training platform that supports flexible combinations of multiple reward signals and training algorithms.

## VI. Trends and Implications

### 6.1 Five Well-Established Trends

#### Trend One: MoE Engineering Capability Becomes a Core Competitive Advantage

8 of the 9 models adopt MoE, and the one exception (Hunyuan TurboS's Mamba-Transformer hybrid) itself also contains MoE expert layers. Dense architectures have completely exited the stage among flagship models. With DeepSeek V4 achieving its performance using 49B active parameters out of a 1.6T total, it is hard for any team to justify training a 1.6T Dense model.

This means that MoE engineering capability — routing stability, expert load balancing, All-to-All communication optimization — is becoming a core competitive moat for large-model teams.

#### Trend Two: Sparse Attention Blooming in a Hundred Flowers

CSA/HCA, MSA, DSA, MFA, SWA+GA — every major model has proposed its own distinctive sparse-attention approach. It is now consensus that standard full attention hits an efficiency bottleneck in long-context scenarios, but the optimal replacement has not yet converged. The compression, selection, window, and linear schools each have their own suitable scenarios, and are unlikely to unify into a single paradigm in the near term.

#### Trend Three: The Muon Optimizer Displacing AdamW

DeepSeek V4, Kimi K2, GLM-5, Step 3.5 Flash — 4 models explicitly adopt Muon or a variant of it. AdamW dominated the deep-learning optimizer landscape for the past decade, and Muon is the first alternative to demonstrate superiority in large-scale pretraining. The customized improvements each vendor has made on top of Muon (MuonClip, Muon Split, Polar Express) show that Muon's basic framework has already been accepted, and the competitive focus has shifted to the specifics of implementation optimization.

#### Trend Four: Distillation Is Replacing Traditional RL

DeepSeek V4's OPD and MiMo-V2-Flash's MOPD represent a trend that could reshape the post-training paradigm: **replacing (or drastically reducing) traditional RL with distillation**.

The underlying logic is clear: RL's reward signal is scalar and low in information density; distillation's "reward signal" is a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary, several orders of magnitude higher in information density; RL training is unstable, while distillation is more stable; RL requires substantial rollout compute, while distillation is more compute-efficient.

But distillation has one fundamental limitation: it needs a teacher model stronger than the student. When you are training the "strongest model," where does the teacher come from? DeepSeek V4's answer is "domain experts" — expert models trained to the extreme in specific domains can still provide an effective teacher signal within their own domains, even if their overall capability falls short of the final model's.

#### Trend Five: Agent Capability Becomes a Core Competitive Dimension

Kimi K2/K2.5's Agent data synthesis pipeline (3,000+ MCP tools → 20,000+ synthesized tools), GLM-5's Slime asynchronous Agent RL infrastructure (1,000+ concurrent rollouts), and MiniMax M2's Forge RL system — multiple teams have poured substantial engineering resources into Agent training.

The importance of Agent capability stems from demand pull at the application layer: large models are evolving from "answering questions" to "completing tasks," which requires using tools, interacting with environments, and handling multi-step tasks. These capabilities cannot be acquired through pure language modeling alone and require dedicated training methods and data. Kimi K2's strong performance on Agent benchmarks such as SWE-bench, and DeepSeek V4's 80.6% score on SWE-bench Verified, both validate this direction.

### 6.2 Open Questions

#### Will sparse attention converge on a single paradigm?

There are currently at least 5 distinct design philosophies (compression, selection, window, linear, hybrid). Will they, like MoE unified model architecture, eventually converge onto a single optimal approach?

Arguments **leaning toward no**: different approaches optimize for different objectives — CSA/HCA optimizes KV cache size, MSA optimizes computation volume, SWA optimizes implementation simplicity, and Mamba optimizes theoretical complexity. These objectives involve fundamental trade-offs against one another.

Arguments **leaning toward yes**: the market will ultimately select whichever approach has the highest overall efficiency, just as the Transformer unified the competition among RNN/CNN/Attention. DeepSeek V4's CSA+HCA currently achieves the best overall balance of "KV cache compression + compute efficiency," and could potentially become the de facto standard.

#### Can distillation completely replace RL?

DeepSeek V4's OPD has already, in practice, fully replaced traditional RL with distillation. But if training the strongest model also relies on distillation, where does the "teacher" come from?

Possible answers:
1. **The domain-expert route** (already being pursued by DeepSeek V4): train multiple domain experts, each pushed to the extreme within its own domain, then distill and merge them
2. **The self-distillation route**: the model uses its own historical versions as teachers, continuously improving through iterative self-improvement
3. **The RL+distillation hybrid route**: RL is used to explore the boundary of capability, while distillation is used to efficiently propagate the knowledge already acquired

Qwen 3's minimalist-RL result hints at another possibility: perhaps a small amount of high-quality RL is already sufficient, with distillation handling the bulk of the remaining work. The post-training paradigm of the future may well be a combination of "a small amount of RL exploration + a large amount of distillation propagation."

#### Is the Mamba-Transformer hybrid the future?

Hunyuan TurboS, with only 7 of 128 layers (5.5%) as attention, has demonstrated the viability of the Mamba-Transformer hybrid architecture. But note: Hunyuan TurboS is the only one of the 9 models to adopt this architecture; Mamba still has theoretical limitations in tasks such as precise positional retrieval; and the linear-complexity advantage is not pronounced for short sequences, only becoming apparent in long-sequence (>64K) scenarios.

The Mamba-Transformer hybrid is very likely a valuable alternative route, especially in long-context and low-latency scenarios. But whether it can displace pure Transformer+MoE as the mainstream approach is not yet supported by sufficient evidence.

## Conclusion

Looking back at the foundation-model race of 2025-2026, three judgments emerge. First, the space for architectural innovation is far from closed — directions such as sparse attention, Mamba hybrids, and mHC residual connections are all evolving rapidly, and the assumption that "the Transformer is the final form" has been broken. Second, the post-training paradigm is undergoing a shift from "RL-dominated" to "distillation-dominated," as proven from two opposite extremes by DeepSeek V4's OPD and Qwen 3's minimalist RL. Third, Agent capability is becoming the core vehicle for delivering model value, and investment in data synthesis and asynchronous RL infrastructure will determine the competitive landscape of the next stage.

For practitioners, three directions are worth focusing on: the engineering deployment of sparse attention (which will directly affect inference cost and the upper bound on context length), the practical methodology of online policy distillation (especially the training and combination strategy for domain experts), and the construction of Agent data synthesis pipelines (currently the biggest bottleneck to improving Agent capability).

**Practical recommendations for teams of different scales**:
- **Teams with roughly a thousand GPUs**: consider the Qwen 3 route (standard GQA + minimalist RL), which carries the lowest technical risk and manageable engineering complexity, and already has open-weight releases with ample community validation
- **Teams with roughly ten thousand GPUs**: could consider the DeepSeek V4 route (CSA/HCA + OPD), though it requires substantial engineering investment in sparse-attention and distillation infrastructure, and the engineering bar for routing stability and FP4 quantization-aware training is not low
- **Agent direction**: refer to the Kimi K2/K2.5 data synthesis pipeline (3,000+ MCP tools → 20,000+ synthesized tools), which is, based on currently public information, the most complete scheme available; GLM-5's Slime asynchronous RL infrastructure also provides a reference architecture for engineering Agent RL
