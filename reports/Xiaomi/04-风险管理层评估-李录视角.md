# Xiaomi Corporation (1810.HK): Investment Risk and Management Quality Assessment

**Analytical Framework: Li Lu Value-Investing Perspective | Assessment Date: June 23, 2026**

---

## Part One: Management Assessment

### 1.1 Overall Assessment of Lei Jun

| Dimension | Rating | Core Judgment |
|------|------|---------|
| Circle of Competence | ★★★★ | Deep experience in consumer electronics and internet; vehicles are an extension rather than the core |
| Integrity | ★★★★ | Communicates publicly and transparently, but a 2025 top-of-range placement raising HK$42.5 billion diluted shareholders by 3.1% |
| Strategic Vision | ★★★★★ | His standout strength — from Kingsoft to Xiaomi to car-making, he has seized the era's opportunities every time |
| Capital Allocation | ★★★ | His weakest link — zero dividends since listing, with cumulative financing raised of over HK$80-90 billion |
| Owner Mindset | ★★★★ | Holds 23.4% of the equity and 55.7% of the voting rights; his personal wealth is tightly bound to the company |

**A structural weakness in his circle of competence**: Lei Jun excels at "efficiency-driven" business models (extreme value-for-money), but lacks a track record of success with "brand-premium-driven" models. After years of premiumization efforts, models priced above RMB 4,000 still hold only 17.3% market share.

**Controversial capital allocation**: In March 2025, Xiaomi placed 800 million shares at a top-of-range price of HK$53.25, raising roughly HK$42.5 billion net; the placement triggered a 6.32% share-price plunge and wiped out more than HK$90 billion in market cap. The company has never paid a dividend since listing.

**Overall Management Rating: ★★★★ — Excellent but not exceptional; strong strategic ability, but capital-allocation discipline needs strengthening.**

### 1.2 The Core Management Team

The co-founder camp has fragmented. Li Wanqiang and Hong Feng have both exited the domestic subsidiaries. Lin Bin moved from President to Vice Chairman. The core team has evolved from the "Eight Elders" into a model centered on Lei Jun's personal authority.

On the positive side: high decision-making efficiency and strong strategic consistency. On the negative side: heavy reliance on the judgment of one man, and a lack of effective internal checks and balances.

## Part Two: Regulatory Risk

### 2.1 India Regulatory Risk 【Severity: Extreme | Status: Already occurred】

- Roughly RMB 4.8 billion in funds have been frozen for more than four years, with about $545 million still restricted
- Xiaomi's India market share has plunged from a peak of 31% to 12%
- In February 2026, a fresh $72 million tax dispute arose
- **Hidden risk**: the India playbook could be copied by other emerging markets. Overseas revenue accounts for roughly 50% of Xiaomi's total, so this is not a tail risk that can be ignored

### 2.2 U.S. Geopolitical Risk 【Severity: Medium | Probability: Medium】

Xiaomi's reliance on Qualcomm chips is a core point of vulnerability. In-house chip development remains a long way from full autonomy over core SoCs.

### 2.3 Vehicle Safety and Recall Risk 【Severity: High | Status: Already occurred】

- Two serious fatal accidents in 2025 (three deaths in Tongling, three deaths in Chengdu)
- Recall of nearly 117,000 SU7 vehicles (a defect in the smart-driving function)
- Xiaomi's brand DNA is consumer electronics; consumers have far less tolerance for automotive accidents than for phone issues

## Part Three: Competitive Risk

### 3.1 Huawei's Full-Scale Comeback 【Severity: Extreme | Status: Already occurred】

**Smartphone front**: In Q1 2026, Huawei led the China market with 20% share, while Xiaomi plunged 35% and dropped to fifth. Huawei has blocked Xiaomi's path to the premium tier.

**Vehicle front**: Harmony Intelligent Mobility delivered 589,000 units in 2025, targeting 1-1.3 million units in 2026.

**IoT front**: Huawei's HarmonyOS ecosystem directly threatens Xiaomi's IoT connectivity advantage.

**Hidden risk**: Huawei represents "self-reliant innovation" (in-house chips), while Xiaomi represents "integration-based innovation" (dependent on Qualcomm). In a Chinese market where nationalist sentiment increasingly drives consumption, this difference in narrative could keep suppressing brand premium over the long run.

### 3.2 The Smartphone Gross-Margin Ceiling 【Severity: High | Probability: High】

Full-year 2025 margin was 10.9%, falling further to 10.1% in Q1 2026. The real impact of the 300% surge in DDR5 memory-chip prices is expected to fully materialize in Q2-Q3.

---

## Part Four: Business Risk

### Uncertainty in the Vehicle Business 【Severity: High】

- Another RMB 3.1 billion loss in Q1 2026 (including pre-production spending on the YU7)
- Only two models in the product line — a hit to either one would cause severe volatility
- Does the automotive industry's long-run return match the capital invested? Historically, almost no new entrant has achieved sustained excess returns

---

## Part Five: Governance Structure

### Dual-Class Share Structure 【Risk Rating: Medium-High】

Lei Jun controls 55.7% of the voting rights with just 23.4% economic interest. Outside shareholders have almost no veto power over major decisions. The 2025 top-of-range share placement is a textbook example.

### Shareholder Returns

The company has never paid a dividend, and while buybacks have intensified, their net effect has been diluted by financing from share placements. Whether it is right for a company earning RMB 39.2 billion in annual profit to choose zero dividends depends on the long-run returns of the vehicle and AI businesses.

## Part Seven: Long-Term Certainty Through Li Lu's Lens

### What Will Xiaomi Look Like in Ten Years?

| Scenario | Probability | Description |
|------|------|------|
| Optimistic | 30% | The Human-Car-Home ecosystem goes global, annual vehicle sales exceed 2 million, and market cap tops HK$2 trillion |
| Neutral | 45% | Top-three in smartphones but gross margin under pressure; annual vehicle sales of 1-1.5 million at a margin below 5%; profit of RMB 50-70 billion |
| Pessimistic | 25% | Squeezed on two fronts by Huawei, chip controls escalate, profit falls back below RMB 20 billion |

### Assessing "A Long Runway, Thick Snow"

**The runway is long enough**: consumer electronics, smart vehicles, IoT, and AI together offer plenty of room to grow.

**The snow isn't thick enough**: its core competitive strengths — extreme value-for-money, rapid iteration, ecosystem synergy — are mostly operational advantages rather than structural barriers. Operational advantages can be copied; only structural barriers produce durable excess returns.

### Core Risk Judgment

**One-line conclusion: Xiaomi is an "efficiency-type" enterprise led by an excellent entrepreneur, but it is not a "monopoly-type" enterprise with a deep moat.**

Three core concerns:
1. **Insufficient moat depth** — success is highly dependent on Lei Jun personally
2. **Aggressive capital allocation** — heavy investment across five fronts simultaneously sits in tension with Li Lu's preference for "conservative capital allocation"
3. **Valuation embeds too many optimistic assumptions** — success in vehicles, a breakthrough in premiumization, and smooth globalization must all hold true at once

**Final rating: Management 4★, business-model durability 3★, overall risk rating "medium-high" — not a poor company to be avoided, but also not the kind of "certain" holding Li Lu would hold at a heavy weight for ten years.**
