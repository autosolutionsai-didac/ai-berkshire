# Xiaomi Corporation (1810.HK): Business Model, Moat, and User Value Analysis

**Analyst Perspective: Duan Yongping Investment Framework | Information Richness: Grade A | Data as of: 2026 Q1**

---

## I. The Essence of the Business Model: Defining the Core Business and Breaking Down the Revenue Structure

### Defining the Core Business

At its core, Xiaomi is a **"hardware + internet" platform company that uses smartphones as its traffic entry point, extends user touchpoints through IoT hardware, and monetizes through internet services**; starting in 2024 it added smart electric vehicles as a second growth curve. Lei Jun defines this as the "Human × Car × Home" full ecosystem.

But it must be said honestly: while this framing makes for a beautiful narrative, **its profit structure reveals a plainer truth** — Xiaomi remains, at its core, a hardware company, and its ability to monetize internet services falls far short of that of pure internet companies.

## II. Platform/Product Flywheel Effect

### The Theoretical Flywheel

```
Smartphones (traffic entry) → IoT (expands touchpoints/increases stickiness) → Internet services (monetizes traffic) → Vehicles (higher ticket price/brand upgrade)
      ↑                                                                    |
      └──────────── Brand momentum flows back/ecosystem lock-in ←───────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Does the Flywheel Actually Form a Closed Loop? — A Link-by-Link Check

**Link 1: Smartphones → IoT (partially holds)**
The number of devices connected to Xiaomi's AIoT platform has reached 1.04 billion, and users owning five or more IoT devices number 22.7 million, up 23.6% YoY. Mijia's monthly active users have surpassed 100 million. The data looks good on the surface, but it invites a follow-up question: those 22.7 million deeply engaged users represent just 3% of the 742 million monthly actives, meaning the vast majority of users are not locked into the IoT ecosystem at all.

**Link 2: IoT → Internet Services (weak)**
Internet services revenue was RMB 37.4 billion, with ARPU up just 3%. Advertising is the main component, while gaming and value-added services growth has stalled. Compared with pure internet companies like Tencent and ByteDance, Xiaomi's user-monetization efficiency is extremely low.

**Link 3: Vehicles → Ecosystem Synergy (early-stage validation)**
45% of vehicle buyers also purchase a Xiaomi smartphone, and 32% add smart-home products to their cart. The numbers look impressive at first glance, but note: **Xiaomi's early car buyers are themselves loyal Xiaomi fans, so this conversion rate is bound to decline as the customer base widens.**

**Conclusion: The flywheel exists but is not spinning fast — it looks more like a "loose alliance" than a "tightly closed loop."**

**Rating: ★★★ (3/5)**

## III. Moat Analysis — Verified One by One

### 1. Brand Moat: ★★☆☆☆ (a pseudo-moat)

The core perception of the Xiaomi brand is "value for money." A true brand moat means consumers are willing to pay a premium for the brand itself. Xiaomi does the opposite — its brand promise is precisely "we don't charge a premium." Smartphone gross margin fell from 12.6% to 10.9% (just 8.3% in Q4), and while its share of the RMB 6,000-10,000 price band has doubled, it is still only 4.5%.

**Duan Yongping's view**: A brand moat requires that consumers pay a premium because of the brand. Xiaomi's brand promise is "we won't overcharge you" — in Duan Yongping's framework, that is a fatal flaw.

### 2. Switching Costs: ★★☆☆☆ (a pseudo-moat)

Switching brands within the Android ecosystem costs almost nothing. Only 22.7 million users own five or more IoT devices — less than 3% of the total user base. **For the vast majority of users, switching costs are effectively zero.**

### 3. Network Effects: ★★★☆☆ (a weak moat)

The network effect of the IoT ecosystem is **one-sided** (user-to-device), not **two-sided** (like buyers and sellers on Taobao). Users don't want to use Xiaomi IoT more just because "other people also use Xiaomi IoT."

### 4. Economies of Scale: ★★★★☆ (a genuine moat, but limited)

This is Xiaomi's most solid moat. The supply-chain bargaining power that comes from shipping 165 million smartphones a year is real. But competitors have comparable scale too (Samsung shipped 226 million units, Apple 234 million), so it isn't an exclusive advantage.

### 5. Technology Barriers: ★★☆☆☆ (does not constitute a moat)

Xiaomi's 2025 R&D spending was RMB 33.1 billion, but that is dwarfed by Huawei's (over RMB 160 billion). Progress on in-house chip design is slow, and its autonomous-driving capability lags Tesla and Huawei by 2-3 years.

**Overall Moat Rating: ★★★ (3/5)**
> Xiaomi lacks a genuine "wide moat." In Duan Yongping's words: **this is not a business that "makes money even while you sleep."**

## IV. User/Customer Value

### Does "Value for Money" Constitute Genuine Differentiation? — No

1. **Value-for-money is replicable**: any company with supply-chain integration capability can do it
2. **Value-for-money depends on a "follower" positioning**: always benchmarking against others rather than defining the category
3. **Value-for-money is inherently at odds with profit margins**: a 10.9% smartphone gross margin says it all
4. **Duan Yongping's core rebuttal**: value-for-money is "the excuse you make when your own product isn't good enough." When a product is truly good, users are willing to pay a premium

**The only exception is the vehicle business**: the SU7's 24.3% gross margin shows that the automotive category has, for now, achieved a combination of "strong value-for-money plus reasonable profitability" — but this mainly benefits from the category still being immature.

**Rating: ★★★ (3/5)**

---

## V. Quantitative Test of Synergy Effects

If synergy effects were genuinely strong, internet-services ARPU should rise meaningfully as the number of IoT devices grows. In reality, ARPU grew just 3% (far below the 22% growth in device count). **Growth in connected devices has not translated effectively into improved monetization.**

**Rating: ★★★ (3/5)**

## VI. Assessing Xiaomi Against Duan Yongping's "Good Business" Standard

| Assessment Dimension | Standard | Xiaomi's Performance | Rating |
|---------|------|---------|------|
| **Differentiation** | Does the product have a unique, hard-to-replicate quality? | Value-for-money is replicable; the ecosystem has some uniqueness but lacks depth | ★★☆☆☆ |
| **Pricing Power** | Can prices be raised without losing sales volume? | Smartphone gross margin sliding to 10.9% proves a lack of pricing power | ★★☆☆☆ |
| **Sustainable Competitive Advantage** | Will the moat be wider in 10 years? | Depends on deepening the vehicle and IoT businesses; currently uncertain | ★★★☆☆ |

### Why Has Duan Yongping Never Invested in Xiaomi?

1. **"Value-for-money is just an excuse you make for yourself"** — long-run profit margins will be squeezed by competition
2. **Opposed to diversified expansion** — "companies whose product portfolio spans too wide a range struggle to make great products over the long run"
3. **The promise of a 5% net hardware margin** — voluntarily gives up the core defining feature of a "good business"
4. **Lacks the discipline of "knowing what not to do"** — Duan Yongping stresses that "knowing what not to do" matters more than "knowing what to do"
5. **The lesson of Apple's abandoned car project** — Duan Yongping praised Apple's decision to abandon car-making as the right call; Xiaomi did exactly the opposite

**Rating: ★★ (2/5)**
> By Duan Yongping's standard, Xiaomi is not a "good business."

---

## Overall Conclusion

**Composite Rating: 2.8★/5★**

**Duan Yongping's core judgment: Xiaomi is a "hardworking, mediocre business."** Lei Jun is undoubtedly an excellent entrepreneur with remarkable execution ability, but the business model itself sets a ceiling on profitability. Xiaomi's central contradiction is this: **its brand promise (value-for-money/fairness to customers) is precisely the reason it cannot build a wide moat.**

Duan Yongping would say: Xiaomi may be a good company, but it is not a good business — **and when a good company runs a bad business, in the end the bad business wins.**

The vehicle business is the one variable that could change this judgment. If Xiaomi can build genuine brand premium and pricing power in the automotive category, the entire investment thesis may need to be rewritten. But the evidence for that is currently insufficient.
