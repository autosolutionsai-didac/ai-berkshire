## The Logic of Custom ASICs: Why Cloud Providers Don't Use Nvidia Exclusively

Nvidia's GPUs are already this strong — and yet AWS has spent billions of dollars building Trainium, Microsoft built Maia, and Google built the TPU. **This isn't emotional decision-making — it's a very clear economic calculation.**

Three core reasons:

### Reason One: The Economics of Inference

AI has two phases — training and inference — and their hardware requirements are completely different.

| Phase | Characteristics | Hardware requirement | Best fit |
|------|------|---------|---------|
| Training | One-off, large-scale computation | Flexibility > efficiency | GPU (Nvidia) |
| Inference | Continuous, high-frequency, massive scale | Efficiency > flexibility | ASIC (custom silicon) |

Analogy: training is like "building a factory" — you need a universal machine tool; inference is like "assembly-line production" — you need dedicated equipment.

As AI inference becomes cloud providers' single largest compute expense, **efficiency per dollar per watt** becomes critically important. A custom ASIC's performance-per-watt on specific inference tasks can be **3-5x** that of a general-purpose GPU — and the bigger the scale, the more money is saved.

### Reason Two: Supply Chain Security

AWS generates more than **$100 billion** in annual revenue; if its entire AI infrastructure depended on Nvidia alone, that would mean putting its fate in a single supplier's hands — an unacceptable risk in any industry.

In-house design + external custom ASIC (Marvell/Broadcom) = a second source of supply.

### Reason Three: Competitive Differentiation

Google's TPU, AWS's Trainium, Microsoft's Maia — every cloud provider wants to build a unique advantage in AI infrastructure. Anyone can buy a general-purpose GPU, but only you have your own custom chip.

**Counterpoint:** This logic holds, but it rests on an implicit premise — "that the cost-performance advantage of custom ASICs is large enough." Nvidia is not a stationary target. The Blackwell architecture has already substantially improved inference efficiency, and if GPU inference performance keeps closing the gap, the economic advantage of ASICs could shrink to the point where custom design "isn't worth it." Historically, there are no shortage of cases where general-purpose solutions ultimately beat out specialized ones (x86 versus various dedicated processors).

## How Big Is the Market: From $10 Billion to $40 Billion

Estimates of the custom ASIC market's size vary by source, but they point in the same direction:

| Source | 2025 | 2028 projection | CAGR |
|------|---------|-----------|------|
| Counterpoint Research | ~$10 billion | ~$40.8 billion | ~47% |
| Implied by management guidance | ~$10 billion | ~$40 billion | ~45% |
| Sell-side consensus | ~$8-12 billion | ~$30-45 billion | ~40-50% |

Sources: Counterpoint Research, Marvell management guidance, sell-side research reports

**Even taking the conservative end of $30 billion, this is still a market that triples in three years.** For comparison, the GPU market's growth over the same period is forecast at roughly **16%** (source: industry analysis) — ASIC growth is nearly 3x that of GPUs.

But a few things bear watching:

1. **TAM forecasts have a notoriously poor track record** — especially in emerging markets. In 2020, no one predicted how large the AI chip market would become by 2025
2. **TAM does not equal Marvell's revenue** — even if the market reaches $40 billion, if Broadcom takes 55-70% of it, Marvell may only capture 15-20%
3. **TAM is the sum of "everyone combined"** — if cloud providers' in-house design capabilities strengthen further (Google is already designing parts of its TPU in-house), the share flowing to external design houses will shrink

## Broadcom Versus Marvell: A Lopsided Competition

This is the central question anyone trying to understand Marvell must answer — **how much share can Marvell take from Broadcom in the custom ASIC space?**

### The Competitive Landscape

| Dimension | Broadcom | Marvell |
|------|-----------------|------------------|
| ASIC market share | **55-70%** | **~15%** |
| Core customers | Google (TPU, contract through 2031), Meta (MTIA), OpenAI, ByteDance | AWS (Trainium), Microsoft (Maia), Google (partial) |
| Total annual revenue | **~$51 billion** | **~$8.2 billion** |
| ASIC-related revenue | **~$12 billion+** | **~$1.5 billion** |
| Gross margin | **70%+** (Non-GAAP) | **~61%** (Non-GAAP) |
| Breadth of IP | Networking + storage + security + ASIC + software | ASIC + optical interconnect + DPU + networking |
| M&A firepower | Acquired VMware ($61 billion) | Acquired Celestial AI ($3.25 billion) |

Sources: TradingKey, SimplyWall St, company earnings reports

**Put plainly: Broadcom is a heavyweight boxer, and Marvell is a middleweight.** The two are in the same ring, but the weight gap is significant.

### Marvell's Room for Differentiation

Within this lopsided competition, Marvell has three points of differentiation:

**1. Technology leadership in optical interconnect**

Broadcom also does optical interconnect, but Marvell holds **60%+** market share in PAM4 optical DSP, with a 1-2 generation technology lead. When a customer needs a combined "ASIC + optical interconnect" solution, Marvell's bundled capability has unique value.

**2. "Not being Broadcom" is itself an advantage**

Just as cloud providers don't want to depend solely on Nvidia, they also don't want to depend solely on Broadcom. As the **second choice** for custom ASIC, Marvell naturally benefits from customers' "supply chain diversification" strategy.

**3. Strategic backing from Nvidia**

Nvidia's **$2 billion** investment in Marvell (rather than Broadcom), with the two companies jointly developing an NVLink Fusion interconnect solution, is more than just capital — it's a signal: Nvidia has chosen Marvell as its interconnect ecosystem partner, indirectly helping Marvell build credibility with large customers.

**Counterpoint:**

1. **A share gap this large can't be closed by "differentiation" alone.** Broadcom at 55-70% versus Marvell at 15% — this isn't "each has its own strengths," this is a rout. Broadcom's TPU contract with Google is locked in through **2031**, and Meta, OpenAI, and ByteDance are all in Broadcom's camp. Marvell's customer list does include AWS and Microsoft, but contract details aren't public, and renewal uncertainty is high.

2. **Broadcom has the scale advantage to fight a price war.** Its revenue is 6x Marvell's, and its gross margin is 10 points higher — if Broadcom decides to cut prices to win customers, Marvell will struggle to keep up.

3. **The ceiling of being the "second choice."** Customers genuinely do need a second choice, but the share ceiling for that "second choice" may be around 20-25%. To exceed that, Marvell would need to be better than Broadcom in some specific area — and beyond optical interconnect, there's no clear direction in sight yet.

### The Quantified Risk of Customer Concentration

Data center revenue accounts for **74%** of total revenue, and that data center revenue is itself heavily concentrated among 3-4 customers. While Marvell doesn't disclose single-customer revenue shares, industry analysis suggests that **the top two customers combined may contribute 60%+ of data center revenue**.

This means: **if AWS fails to renew a single contract, Marvell could face a 15-20% revenue cliff.** This isn't a theoretical risk — cases of large customers switching suppliers are common in the semiconductor industry.

## Management's Growth Targets: Aggressive or Reasonable?

| Metric | FY2026 (actual) | FY2027 (guidance) | FY2028 (target) |
|------|--------------|--------------|--------------|
| Total revenue | **$8.195 billion** | **>$11 billion** (+30%+) | **~$15 billion** |
| Data center revenue | ~$6.0 billion | — | — |
| Custom ASIC revenue | ~$1.5 billion | — | **$9-11 billion** |
| Celestial AI revenue | 0 | 0 | Begins in FY2028 second half, annualizing at **$0.5 billion** |

Sources: Marvell management guidance, Marvell IR

### What It Would Take to Deliver on These Targets

1. **AI capex needs to keep growing at a high rate** — the combined capex of AWS, Microsoft, and Google needs to sustain **30%+** annual growth. The current trend supports this (Microsoft's FY2025 capex was **$50 billion+**), but cyclical downside risk always exists
2. **No loss of major customers** — the loss of any single core customer would make the target impossible to hit
3. **Broadcom doesn't start a price war** — if Broadcom cuts prices aggressively, both Marvell's margins and growth rate would suffer
4. **Celestial AI commercializes on schedule** — a $3.25 billion acquisition that won't generate revenue until FY2028, on a technology path not yet proven
5. **No worsening of China geopolitics** — China accounted for **43%** of FY2025 revenue, and tighter export controls could directly hit revenue

**What's the probability that all five conditions are met simultaneously?** That's a question every investor needs to answer for themselves.

## Endgame Scenarios for the Custom ASIC Market

Looking out 5-10 years, the custom ASIC market could plausibly land in one of three or four end states:

| End state | Impact on Marvell | Probability assessment |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Stable duopoly**: Broadcom 50% + Marvell 25% + others 25% | Best-case scenario — steady growth, manageable margins | Moderate (35%) |
| **Broadcom dominance**: Broadcom 70% + Marvell 15% + long tail 15% | Marvell survives but growth is constrained, possibly acquired | Moderate (30%) |
| **Rise of in-house design**: cloud providers self-design 40% + Broadcom 30% + Marvell 15% + others 15% | The whole outsourced-design market shrinks, squeezing Marvell | Moderate-to-low (25%) |
| **Marvell breaks through**: via M&A/technology breakthrough, rises to co-leader | Best outcome but lowest probability | Low (10%) |

**Historical reference:** In the semiconductor industry, the long-term fate of a "strong runner-up" typically falls into one of three paths — (1) getting acquired (Xilinx acquired by AMD, Altera acquired by Intel, LSI Logic acquired by Avago); (2) surviving independently by carving out a defensible niche (Qualcomm in mobile chips); (3) being progressively marginalized as the gap widens (3Com versus Cisco).

Marvell currently looks most like scenario (2) — it has found a differentiated niche in the combination of optical interconnect plus custom ASIC — but whether it can survive independently over the long run depends on the durability of AI demand and management's strategic execution.
