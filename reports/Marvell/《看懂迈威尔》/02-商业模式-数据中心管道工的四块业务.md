## The Revenue Picture First

Many people look at Marvell and see only the label "AI chip company," but where the money actually comes from, which pieces are growing, and which are shrinking — most people can't say. This part breaks down the four business lines one by one to work out exactly how Marvell makes its money.

Marvell's FY2026 revenue was **$8.195 billion**, and here's how it breaks down:

| Business segment | Revenue | Share | YoY growth | Character |
|----------|------|------|----------|------|
| Data Center | **~$6.0 billion** | **74%** | +46% | The absolute core |
| Enterprise Networking | ~$0.63 billion | 8% | Bottoming out and recovering | Legacy business |
| Carrier Infrastructure | ~$0.34 billion | 4% | 5G slowdown | Shrinking |
| Consumer | ~$0.32 billion | 4% | Stable | Peripheral business |
| Automotive/Industrial | ~$0.32 billion | 4% | Divested | Fully spun off |
| **Total** | **$8.195 billion** | **100%** | **+42%** | — |

Sources: Marvell FY2026 annual report, Futurum Group

One glance tells you: **Marvell's story is the data center story — the other four segments together account for only about a quarter of revenue.**

Within data center, the business splits into three product lines. These three lines determine what kind of company Marvell really is.

## Product Line One: Custom ASIC (Custom XPU)

### What This Is

ASIC = Application-Specific Integrated Circuit. In plain terms: **an AI chip designed to a customer's own specifications.**

Nvidia's GPU is a "general-purpose kitchen knife" — it can cut anything, but it isn't the most efficient tool for any one job; a custom ASIC is a "dedicated surgical scalpel" — it does one thing, but with extremely high efficiency and extremely low power draw.

Why do hyperscale cloud providers want custom silicon? Three reasons:

1. **Reducing dependence on Nvidia** — not wanting to be beholden to a single supplier
2. **Improving efficiency for specific workloads** — in inference scenarios, an ASIC's performance-per-watt can be 3-5x that of a GPU
3. **Lowering total cost of ownership (TCO)** — at scale, small differences in dollars-per-watt efficiency get magnified enormously

### What Marvell's Role Is

Marvell isn't designing an entire chip from scratch — it provides **critical IP blocks** (high-speed SerDes interfaces, interconnect logic, packaging solutions, and the like), collaborating with the customer's own in-house design team to integrate everything into a finished chip.

Think of it this way: AWS is the architect, and Marvell is the specialty contractor supplying the steel structure and the plumbing system. The house belongs to AWS, but the core skeleton comes from Marvell.

### Core Numbers

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| FY2026 custom ASIC annualized revenue | **~$1.5 billion** |
| Starting point | Near zero in FY2025 |
| One-year growth | **From zero to $1.5 billion** |
| Core customers | AWS (Trainium), Microsoft (Maia), Google |
| Design cycle | **3-4 years** (once selected, extremely difficult to displace) |

Sources: Marvell FY2026 Q4 earnings call, Futurum Group

### Key Assessment

**The good news:** Custom ASIC is Marvell's fastest-growing business, and a 3-4 year design cycle means that once a customer commits, it is effectively "locked in" — switching costs are a genuine moat. Management is guiding for this business to generate **$9-11 billion** in annual revenue by FY2028 (source: Marvell management guidance).

**The bad news:** This is also a business where you answer to the customer. AWS and Google both generate annual revenue above **$100 billion**, versus Marvell's $8.2 billion — the bargaining power is completely lopsided. Gross margin of **~61%**, versus Nvidia's **75%+**, is 15 points lower, partly because of customer price pressure.

**Counterpoint:** The "lock-in" in custom ASIC cuts both ways — the customer is locked into the supplier, but the supplier is also locked into the customer's technology roadmap. If AWS changes architectural direction for its next-generation chip, years of Marvell's investment could go to waste. And Google already has some in-house TPU design experience, so the risk of a "customer turning into a competitor" is always present.

## Product Line Two: Optical Interconnect (Optical DSP)

### What This Is

AI training and inference require thousands upon thousands of chips working in parallel. The speed at which data moves between those chips sets the efficiency ceiling for the entire system. Optical interconnect is the **"highway" that connects these chips**.

Marvell's core product is the PAM4 optical DSP chip — it converts electrical signals into optical signals, transmits them at high speed through fiber, and converts them back to electrical signals. The current mainstream standard is **800G**, with the next generation, **1.6T**, now ramping into production.

### Core Numbers

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| Global optical DSP market share | **60%+** |
| Technology generation lead | **1-2 generations** ahead of competitors |
| Current products | 800G PAM4 (in production), 1.6T (sampling) |
| Partnership with Nvidia | 1.6T optical interconnect integrated into the Rubin architecture, reference design expected to ship Q3 2026 |

Sources: Marvell IR, NVIDIA announcements

### Key Assessment

**The good news:** Optical interconnect is Marvell's deepest moat. A combination of **60%+ market share and a 1-2 generation technology lead** — in the semiconductor industry, that combination means competitors will struggle to catch up within 3-5 years. Nvidia's choice to partner with Marvell rather than build its own is itself a validation of this barrier.

**The bad news:** Optical interconnect's TAM (total addressable market) is far smaller than that of custom ASIC. Currently around **$5 billion**, and even if it grows to **$15 billion+** by 2028 (source: industry forecasts), that's still only a third of the custom ASIC market's **$40.8 billion**.

**Counterpoint:** A 60% market share sounds impressive, but two things bear watching. First, competitors like Coherent and Broadcom are accelerating their push into 1.6T technology; second, if the silicon photonics technology path ultimately wins out, traditional optical DSP's advantages could be leapfrogged entirely. Marvell's **$3.25 billion** acquisition of Celestial AI was made precisely to hedge against this risk — but Celestial AI's Photonic Fabric technology has not yet been commercialized, and won't generate revenue until the second half of FY2028 (source: Marvell official announcement).

## Product Line Three: Network Switching and DPUs

### What This Is

Beyond custom ASIC and optical interconnect, Marvell has two more data center product lines:

1. **Network switching chips** — the "traffic controller" inside the data center, determining how data packets move
2. **DPUs (Data Processing Units)** — take over networking, storage, security, and other "chores" from the CPU, freeing the CPU to focus purely on compute

Both of these businesses were acquired between 2017 and 2021 through the purchases of Cavium (**$6.0 billion**) and Innovium (**$1.1 billion**).

### Key Assessment

**An awkward position:** In network switching, Broadcom is the undisputed leader; in DPUs, both Nvidia (BlueField) and AMD are competing hard. Marvell is not the leader in either of these sub-segments.

**The value lies in the bundle:** Standing alone, these businesses aren't especially impressive, but when packaged together with custom ASIC and optical interconnect and sold to the same customer, the value of a "one-stop solution" becomes clear. This is also what differentiates Marvell from pure-play ASIC design houses like Alchip and GUC.

## Legacy Businesses: Being Actively Wound Down

| Segment | Current status | Strategic positioning |
|------|------|---------|
| Enterprise Networking | ~$0.63 billion, bottoming out and recovering | Maintain cash flow |
| Carrier Infrastructure | ~$0.34 billion, 5G slowdown | Natural decline |
| Consumer | ~$0.32 billion, stable | Neither invest nor exit |
| Automotive Ethernet | Sold to Infineon | **$2.5 billion cash received** |

In August 2025, Marvell sold its automotive Ethernet business to Infineon for **$2.5 billion**, generating a pre-tax gain of **$1.8 billion** (source: Marvell IR announcement). This deal makes management's strategic intent very clear: **cut everything that isn't data center, and go all-in on AI infrastructure.**

**Counterpoint:** The risk of cutting the legacy businesses is that if AI data center spending experiences a cyclical downturn (historically, the semiconductor industry sees a downturn roughly every 3-4 years), Marvell will have no "cushion." The 7% year-over-year revenue decline in FY2024 is a cautionary precedent — at the time, data center hadn't yet taken off, and the legacy businesses were shrinking, hitting the company from both sides at once.

## What the Gross Margins Reveal

| Company | Non-GAAP gross margin | GAAP gross margin | Source of the gap |
|------|----------------|-------------|---------|
| Nvidia | **75%+** | ~73% | Small gap (mostly organic growth) |
| Broadcom | **70%+** | ~60% | Heavy acquisition-related amortization |
| Marvell | **~61%** | **~41%** | **20-point gap** |

Sources: each company's latest annual report

Marvell's **20-point gap** between Non-GAAP and GAAP gross margin comes from intangible asset amortization tied to more than **$20 billion** in cumulative acquisitions over the past decade — Inphi ($10 billion), Cavium ($6.0 billion), Innovium ($1.1 billion), Celestial AI ($3.25 billion), and others.

What does this mean?

1. **The 61% Non-GAAP gross margin** reflects the true earning power of the "chip design business" — a normal level for a fabless semiconductor company, but not especially outstanding
2. **The 41% GAAP gross margin** reflects the cost of "acquisition-driven growth" — you can say the amortization is "non-cash," but the actual cash spent at the time of acquisition was real money
3. **The gap versus Nvidia's 75%** shows that Marvell is not a "platform" company — it has no software-ecosystem moat like CUDA, and its pricing power is constrained by large customers

**Counterpoint:** Some will say "Non-GAAP is the true measure of earning power." But if a company needs to keep making large acquisitions to obtain technology and customers, then the cost of those acquisitions is part of the business. Looking only at Non-GAAP is like looking only at "recurring profit" while ignoring "recurring acquisition spending" — over the long run, the two cannot be separated.

## The Essence of Marvell's Business: One Sentence

Putting all of the above together:

> **Marvell = the AI data center's "senior plumber" — helping hyperscale cloud providers design custom chips, providing high-speed optical interconnects between chips, and integrating networking and storage functions. It doesn't do the most expensive job (GPUs); it does the most critical but least noticed job (interconnect + custom design).**

The characteristics of this business:

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|------|------|
| Once a customer commits, switching is hard (high switching costs) | Extreme customer concentration (3-4 cloud providers) |
| Structural growth driven by AI demand | Growth tied to customers' capex cycles |
| Technology lead of 1-2 generations in optical interconnect | Gross margin constrained by large-customer bargaining power |
| Best-in-class management M&A and integration ability | Large gap between GAAP and Non-GAAP profit |
| Nvidia's $2 billion investment validates ecosystem value | Custom ASIC market share far below Broadcom's |

**Using Duan Yongping's framework — is this "the right business"?**

The answer leans toward "upper-middle": there is a real technological moat and customer lock-in, but at its core this is **"outsourced design for large customers"** — bargaining power is limited, and Marvell has less autonomy than Nvidia, with its own proprietary ecosystem, or Broadcom, with its broad customer base.
