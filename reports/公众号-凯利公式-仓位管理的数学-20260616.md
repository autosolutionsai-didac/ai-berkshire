# Winning Every Bet, Yet Going Broke — Investing's Most Counterintuitive Mathematical Trap

Suppose there's an investment opportunity: each time you bet, you have a 60% chance of winning. If you win, you double your stake; if you lose, you lose the entire stake. In other words, for every 100 yuan wagered, the mathematical expectation is a profit of 20 yuan — this is clearly a game stacked in your favor.

What if you bet your entire capital every single time? Win three times in a row and your capital multiplies eightfold — but lose even once, and it's wiped out completely. And a 40% failure probability means that the more times you win in a row, the more certain it becomes that the one fatal loss is coming. Play long enough, and ruin is inevitable.

This isn't a far-fetched hypothetical. In 1998, Long-Term Capital Management's partners included two Nobel laureates in economics, and their strategy genuinely made money — but under 25-to-1 leverage, a single Russian debt crisis wiped out $4.6 billion. Their mistake wasn't calling the direction wrong; it was betting too much.

The question isn't "should you invest" — it's "how much should you invest." More than sixty years ago, an information theorist gave a precise answer.

## The Kelly Criterion: How Much Should You Actually Bet

In 1956, Bell Labs physicist John Kelly solved a problem: in a repeated game where the win probability and payout odds are known, what fraction of your capital should you bet each time to maximize long-term wealth growth?

The answer, in plain terms: **take your edge — the win probability minus the loss probability — divide it by the payout odds, and that's the fraction you should bet.**

Written as a formula:

> Optimal position size = (payout odds × win probability − loss probability) ÷ payout odds

In symbols: f = (b×p − q) / b, where f is the optimal bet fraction, b is the payout odds (how many times your stake you win), p is the win probability, and q is the loss probability (1 − p).

Back to the opening example: a win doubles your stake (b=1), the win probability is 60% (p=0.6), and the loss probability is 40% (q=0.4). Plugging in: f = (1×0.6 − 0.4) / 1 = 20%. The Kelly criterion tells you that betting 20% of your total capital each time is the strategy that maximizes long-term growth.

Try another: a win pays out twice your stake (b=2), with a 50% win probability. f = (2×0.5 − 0.5) / 2 = 25%. The better the odds, the more you can bet.

Bet too little, and growth is slow. Bet too much, though, and growth doesn't speed up — it slows down, or even reverses. This is the most counterintuitive part of the Kelly criterion.

## Exceed the Limit, and Ruin Is Certain

![Kelly curve](../assets/kelly/fig1-kelly-curve.png)

The table below is the single most important piece of information in this article:

| Betting Strategy | Long-Term Growth Rate | Volatility | What It Means |
|---------|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Half-Kelly (0.5x) | 75% of full Kelly | 50% of full Kelly | Half the volatility for three-quarters of the growth |
| Full Kelly (1x) | 100% (theoretical optimum) | 100% | Fastest growth, but extremely bumpy |
| 1.5x Kelly | 75% of full Kelly | 150% of full Kelly | Same growth as half-Kelly, but triple the volatility |
| **2x Kelly** | **Zero** | 200% of full Kelly | **Zero long-term growth — all that risk for nothing** |
| **Beyond 2x** | **Negative** | Even higher | **Ruin is inevitable in the long run** |

Notice the 1.5x row: the growth rate is identical to half-Kelly, but the volatility is three times higher. Overbetting doesn't just fail to help — it forces you to endure far more pain for no benefit.

2x Kelly is even more extreme — every single trade has positive expected value, yet the long-term growth rate is zero. Beyond 2x, even though every trade is individually profitable, you are certain to lose everything in the long run.

This is the mathematical answer to the paradox posed at the start.

Wall Street has proven this twice, with real money:

**Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM), 1998.** With 25-to-1 leverage, it lost $4.6 billion in four months, nearly triggering a collapse of the global financial system. It took the Federal Reserve coordinating fourteen banks to contain the fallout.

**Bill Hwang's Archegos Capital, 2021.** Using derivatives to build extreme leverage, the fund managed $36 billion in assets at its peak. Within two days, it went to zero, costing banks over $10 billion in losses. Hwang himself was later convicted on ten criminal counts.

## Why a 50% Loss Is Worse Than You Think

Overbetting is fatal because of a mathematical asymmetry at the heart of losses.

![Loss asymmetry](../assets/kelly/fig2-loss-asymmetry.png)

| Loss | Gain Needed to Break Even |
|---------|-------------|
| -10% | +11% |
| -20% | +25% |
| -30% | +43% |
| -50% | **+100%** |
| -75% | **+300%** |
| -90% | **+900%** |

Lose 10%, and an 11% gain gets you back — that looks about the same. But lose 50%, and you need to double your money just to break even. Lose 75%, and you need a threefold gain. Lose 90% — and you need a ninefold gain.

The reason is simple: investing is multiplicative, not additive. Losing 50% means multiplying by 0.5; gaining 50% means multiplying by 1.5. And 0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75, not 1. A loss shrinks your principal, and climbing back to the same absolute value from a smaller base requires a larger percentage gain.

**This is the mathematical reason the Kelly criterion exists: it inherently avoids large drawdowns.** What the Kelly criterion maximizes is the logarithmic growth rate (the geometric mean), not the arithmetic mean. The logarithmic growth rate inherently penalizes large swings — because in a multiplicative world, a single big loss can wipe out many small wins.

So the Kelly criterion isn't really a formula for "helping you earn more" — it's a formula for "helping you not lose too much." Over the long run of compounding, those two things are the same thing.

## The Masters in Practice: Once You Truly Understand It, You Can Bet Big

If the Kelly criterion were merely a mathematical curiosity, it wouldn't hold much interest for investors. But the real-world moves made by Buffett, Munger, and Duan Yongping read almost like a living textbook of the Kelly criterion — even though they may never have used the formula itself.

First, consider an investment version of the Kelly criterion. When returns aren't a simple win/lose outcome but follow a continuous distribution (as with stocks), the Kelly formula becomes:

> Optimal position size = (expected return − risk-free rate) ÷ variance (volatility squared)

In symbols: f = (expected return − risk-free rate) / variance. In plain terms: **the higher the excess return and the lower the volatility, the larger your position should be.**

This formula also has an information-theoretic interpretation: your optimal growth rate equals your informational edge relative to the market. If your understanding of a company is no deeper than the market's, the optimal position size is zero.

Now let's examine the masters' moves through this lens:

![Master investors' concentrated bets](../assets/kelly/fig3-master-cases.png)

**Buffett buys American Express, 1964.**

In 1963, American Express's stock was cut in half by the "salad oil scandal." Buffett personally visited restaurants and shops to observe firsthand, and found that people were still using their American Express cards as usual — the business hadn't been damaged; the market had simply panicked.

Analyzed through the Kelly lens: Buffett judged that American Express's intrinsic value was far above its market price (extremely high expected return), and his on-the-ground research confirmed the business itself hadn't been damaged (in his eyes, the volatility was low). With a large numerator and a small denominator, the Kelly value was high. He invested about $13 million, roughly 40% of his partnership's assets. The result: a multi-fold profit.

**Duan Yongping buys the bottom in NetEase, 2002.**

Due to financial troubles, NetEase's stock fell below $1 and faced delisting. After researching the company, Duan Yongping concluded that its gaming business was severely undervalued. He scraped together $2 million (including a $1 million loan) and bought in between $0.50 and $0.80 per share.

Analyzed through the Kelly lens: Duan judged the probability of delisting to be low, and that the value of the gaming business was far above the market price (extremely high expected return). He described himself as having "truly understood" the business (in his eyes, volatility was close to zero). The Kelly value supported a heavy position. The result: NetEase eventually climbed to over $100 a share, a roughly 130-fold gain worth about $200 million.

**Duan Yongping buys Apple, 2011.**

Bought at an average price of about $13.75, held for 14 years with almost no trading. The result: roughly an 18-fold gain.

These three cases share one thing in common: **they weren't gambling — they were sizing up their positions logically, from a position of overwhelming informational advantage.** Once you truly understand a business, volatility isn't risk in your eyes — it's just the price swinging around.

Munger put it bluntly: "The idea of excessive diversification is madness. Wide diversification, which necessarily includes investment in mediocre businesses, only guarantees mediocre results."

Duan Yongping put it more simply: "Understanding a business is hard. Holding onto something you don't understand is hard. Most people can't hold on because they don't understand."

Translated into Kelly terms: **when you understand it, volatility is low, the Kelly value is high, and you should concentrate. When you don't understand it, volatility is high, the Kelly value is low, and you should diversify — or not touch it at all.**

## Half-Kelly: The Strategy You Should Actually Use in Practice

In theory, full Kelly maximizes growth. But nearly every serious practitioner recommends using only half of it.

**First, your parameter estimates are bound to have errors.** In a casino, win probability and payout odds can be calculated precisely. In the stock market, expected return and volatility are both guesses. If you overestimate your edge, full Kelly turns into over-Kelly — and we already know what happens to over-Kelly. Half-Kelly builds in a buffer for error.

**Second, the loss in growth rate is far smaller than the reduction in volatility.** Half-Kelly sacrifices only 25% of the growth rate while cutting volatility by 50%. Trading 75% of the growth for 50% of the volatility is an extremely good deal.

**Third, psychological tolerance is limited.** Full Kelly can produce drawdowns of over 50% in extreme scenarios. Half-Kelly keeps drawdowns within a range that's humanly bearable.

Research by mathematicians Rotando and Thorp confirms this: **half-Kelly has roughly a 90% probability of avoiding catastrophic losses, while full Kelly has only about 50%.**

Speaking of Thorp — he may be the greatest practitioner of the Kelly criterion. This MIT mathematics professor published "Beat the Dealer" in 1962, using the Kelly criterion to guide his blackjack betting, and got himself blacklisted by casinos. He later moved on to Wall Street, where he founded a fund that returned 15%-20% annualized for 19 consecutive years, losing money in only 3 of 230 months. During the 1973-74 bear market, when the S&P fell 42%, he turned a profit.

Thorp himself also recommends half-Kelly. Between the theoretical optimum and the real-world optimum lies a gap filled with parameter error, black-swan events, and the limits of human psychology.

## Not Losing Money Is the Best Way to Make Money

Back to the question posed at the start: why can something with positive expected value on every single bet still lead to ruin over the long run?

Because investing isn't a single bet — it's a game repeated an unlimited number of times. In a repeated game, what determines the final outcome isn't the arithmetic mean (expected value) but the geometric mean (the compound growth rate). And the geometric mean is exactly what the Kelly criterion maximizes.

In practical terms, three principles follow:

**First, if you have no informational edge, don't bet.** The Kelly criterion states plainly: when your understanding of a company doesn't exceed the market consensus, the optimal position is zero. This is entirely consistent with Duan Yongping's rule of "don't touch what you don't understand."

**Second, even when you have an edge, use only half-Kelly.** Your estimate of your own edge is almost certainly too high. Sizing at half leaves enough room for error.

**Third, never exceed the Kelly ceiling.** This is a line that must never be crossed. Beyond 2x Kelly, no matter how correct your analysis is, the long-term outcome is ruin. LTCM and Archegos weren't defeated by bad judgment — they were defeated by position sizing.

Buffett has said the first rule of investing is "never lose money," and the second rule is "never forget rule number one." These two rules sound like empty platitudes, but the Kelly criterion proves mathematically that they are among the deepest pieces of investment wisdom.

> Investing isn't a contest over who can score the biggest gains — it's about who can stay at the table. Survival matters more than anything else.
