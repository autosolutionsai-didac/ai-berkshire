# 04 Risk and Management Assessment — Li Lu's Perspective

> "In investing, the most important thing is to figure out what you don't know." — Li Lu

## I. Management assessment

### 1.1 Founder Xu Yingtong

| Dimension | Assessment | Rating |
|------|------|:----:|
| **Industry experience** | Joined Huawei in 1999, 23 years at Huawei, served as President of Huawei Smart PV from 2010-2022, took Huawei's solar inverter business to No. 1 in global shipments | ★★★★★ |
| **Entrepreneurial ability** | Grew revenue from zero to RMB 9 billion in 3 years, built a global footprint across 85 countries and 172 distributors, made the product No. 1 globally in its sub-segment | ★★★★★ |
| **Technical understanding** | Deep understanding of power electronics, solar, and storage technology; the 5-in-1 product architecture is a product of his core insight | ★★★★★ |
| **Business judgment** | Precisely targeted the blue-ocean market of stackable solar-plus-storage units, avoiding Huawei's main battlefield (traditional inverters) | ★★★★ |
| **Capital operations** | Completed 7 rounds of financing plus a Hong Kong IPO in 3 years, valuation grew from zero to HKD 80 billion | ★★★★ |
| **Compensation reasonableness** | Xu Yingtong's annual salary is about RMB 70 million (disclosed in the prospectus), on the high side for a company at the IPO stage | ★★★ |

**Assessment of Xu Yingtong:** Without question, he is the right person for this undertaking. Huawei DNA, combined with the experience of taking solar inverters to global No. 1 and a deep understanding of distributed energy, makes him a founder who was "in the right place, with the right capability, who chose the right track."

**But Li Lu would press further:**
- "Why did he leave Huawei? Was it his own choice, or was he pushed out?"
- "Can he build a durable brand independently, without Huawei's brand endorsement behind him?"
- "Is an RMB 70 million annual salary reasonable when the company is only 3 years old? Are the founder's incentives aligned with long-term value creation?"

### 1.2 Core team

Sigenergy's core team has an extremely high concentration of Huawei alumni — many senior managers and technical leads came from Huawei.

**Strength:** The Huawei-alumni team has strong execution, with first-rate engineering and channel capabilities.
**Risk:** Huawei-alumni startups have a classic problem — their success at Huawei depended heavily on Huawei's brand, resources, and platform. Whether they can replicate that same success outside of Huawei remains to be proven over time.

## II. Core risk checklist

### Risk 1: Founded only 3 years ago — the durability of its performance is highly questionable ★★★★★

| Metric | Fact | Concern |
|------|------|------|
| Founding date | May 2022 | Less than 4 years of history |
| Profit history | Turned profitable for a full year only in 2025 | Only 1 year of profit data |
| Business cycle | Has not been through a complete industry downturn | Resilience unknown |
| Customer stickiness | Unknown | No data on repeat-purchase or replacement rates |

**Li Lu's standard of judgment:** "I typically need to see a company go through at least one full industry cycle (usually 5-7 years) before I can assess the durability of its competitive advantage. Sigenergy hasn't been through even one cycle."

### Risk 2: Single-product dependence above 90% ★★★★★

SigenStor accounts for 93% of revenue. This means:
- Any technology shift, product quality issue, or supply-chain disruption would be fatal
- There is no product portfolio to diversify risk
- The "second growth curve" remains undefined

**Counterargument:** Apple started with the iPhone, and SigenStor could similarly become a platform-level product that gradually expands into an ecosystem. But Apple had 10+ years to build its ecosystem; Sigenergy has had only 3.

### Risk 3: Geopolitics and tariff barriers ★★★★

| Market | Risk level | Specific threat |
|------|:-------:|---------|
| United States | Extremely high | Chinese solar-plus-storage products face steep tariffs (potentially >50%), making entry nearly impossible |
| Europe | Medium-high | No targeted tariffs currently, but the risk of anti-subsidy investigations is rising |
| Australia | Medium-low | Currently friendly, but policy could shift with changes in China-Australia relations |
| Southeast Asia / Middle East | Low | Currently open, and a growth opportunity |

**The global trade environment continues to deteriorate in 2026:**
- The Trump administration's trend of raising tariffs on Chinese products remains unchanged
- The EU is evaluating anti-subsidy measures against Chinese batteries and storage products
- The global supply chain's "security first" restructuring trend disadvantages Chinese brands

**Sigenergy's exposure:** The company currently manufactures mainly in China (the Nantong factory) and has no overseas production base. If its main markets impose tariffs, gross margin would take a direct hit.

### Risk 4: Risk of Huawei retaliation ★★★★

**This is the most delicate and yet most critical risk:**

Xu Yingtong left Huawei to start his own company. In the near term, Huawei may "tolerate" this for the following reasons:
- Xu Yingtong retains goodwill and connections within Huawei
- Stackable solar-plus-storage units are a niche segment that doesn't fully overlap with Huawei's main business (inverters)
- Huawei faces its own geopolitical problems (US sanctions)

**But over the long run, Huawei retaliation is almost inevitable:**
- If the stackable all-in-one segment grows large enough (>$10 billion), Huawei will inevitably enter it
- Huawei has an overwhelming advantage in technology, brand, channels, and capital
- Huawei FusionSolar holds 18% of the distributed solar-plus-storage market — 14 times Sigenergy's share
- There are no shortage of cases where Huawei-alumni startups have been crushed by their former parent

### Risk 5: Intensifying competition could erode the gross margin ★★★★

- The "overseas price war" among Chinese solar-plus-storage companies has already begun
- GoodWe, Ginlong Solis, and others are already competing head-to-head with Sigenergy in Australia and Europe
- A 50% gross margin is extremely rare among Chinese manufacturing companies and will inevitably attract more entrants
- **Prediction: the gross margin could fall back from 50% to 35-40% within 2-3 years**

### Risk 6: Over-concentration in overseas markets ★★★

Almost 100% of Sigenergy's revenue comes from overseas; the domestic market is essentially zero.

**This is both a strength (avoiding the domestic price war) and a risk (all eggs in one basket):**
- Unable to tap into mainland China, the world's largest storage market
- Overseas policy shifts (tariffs, subsidy adjustments) affect the entire business
- If it needs to retreat into the domestic market, it would face crushing competition from Huawei, BYD, and others

## IV. Li Lu's overall verdict

### Rating: ★★★ 3.0/5

**Management strengths:**
- Xu Yingtong is an outstanding entrepreneur, and Huawei DNA is a core competitive strength
- The team's execution is first-rate — revenue grew from zero to RMB 9 billion in 3 years
- Strong capital-operations capability

**Core risks:**
- The company is too young (3 years) and has not been through any industry downturn
- Single-product dependence above 90% means extremely concentrated risk
- Geopolitics and tariffs are a structural headwind
- Huawei retaliation is a Sword of Damocles that cannot be ignored
- The 50% gross margin will most likely be pulled down by competition

**Li Lu's likely conclusion:** "Xu Yingtong is an excellent entrepreneur, but investing can't be based solely on the founder's ability. I need to see this company go through at least one full industry cycle to prove the resilience of its business model. Three years of data is too short to distinguish between 'this company is excellent' and 'it just caught a good moment.' I'd put it on my watch list and revisit the decision one to two years after listing."
