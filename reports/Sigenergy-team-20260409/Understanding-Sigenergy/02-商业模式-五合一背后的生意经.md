# The Business Logic Behind the Five-in-One

> *Understanding Sigenergy* Series · Part 02 · Business Model

## I. How SigenStor Actually Makes Money

How does a four-year-old company manage to sell at a 50% gross margin and cover 85 countries within two years? The answer isn't in the phrase "five-in-one" — it's in the business logic behind it.

93% of Sigenergy's revenue comes from a single product, SigenStor. Once you understand where this machine's money comes from and where it goes, you basically understand the company. First, the revenue structure:

| Layer | Content | Revenue Share (2025) |
|------|------|:-----------------:|
| Hardware layer | SigenStor five-in-one solar-storage all-in-one system | **~93%** |
| Software layer | SigenOS AI energy management system | Bundled into the hardware price |
| Services layer | Installation, maintenance, cloud services | Negligible |

**Sigenergy is, at its core, currently a hardware company.** Software (SigenOS) is a value-add feature, but it isn't priced separately; service revenue is nearly negligible.

That means: **Sigenergy's fate is tightly bound to a single product, SigenStor. If the product sells well, the company does well; if the product runs into any trouble, so does the company.**

## II. The Real Value of the Five-in-One

Why did SigenStor manage to become the global No. 1 in its niche within two years? Because it genuinely solves a real pain point.

### Traditional Setup vs. SigenStor

| Dimension | Traditional Separate-Component Setup | SigenStor Five-in-One |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| Number of devices | 5 (inverter + storage converter + battery + charger + energy management) | 1 |
| Number of brands | 3-5 | 1 |
| Number of installations | 3-5 | 1 |
| Installation time | 1-2 days | **Half a day** |
| Compatibility issues | Multi-brand devices require compatibility debugging | None |
| After-sales responsibility | Different brands pass the buck to each other | One brand, fully responsible |
| Expansion capability | Depends on whether individual devices support it | **Stackable, flexible expansion from 5-100kWh** |
| Total cost | Baseline | **20-30% cheaper** |

For users: it saves money, saves hassle, and manages the whole home's energy through one app.

**But the real driver of sales isn't the end user — it's the installer.**

### Installers Are the Key Customer

The sales chain for solar-storage products is: **brand owner → distributor → installer → end user.** End users (households or businesses) typically don't pick a brand themselves — they follow the installer's recommendation.

Why are installers willing to recommend SigenStor?

| Installer Pain Point | How SigenStor Solves It |
|------------|------------------|
| Long installation times limit how many jobs they can take | Installation time cut by 50%+, allowing one more job per day |
| Multi-brand compatibility debugging is a hassle | No compatibility issues with the five-in-one |
| Users hold installers accountable for after-sales issues | One brand takes full responsibility, easing pressure on installers |
| Profit margins are being squeezed | SigenStor's premium pricing leaves installers a large profit margin |

**Sigenergy's real customers aren't households — they're its 172 distributors worldwide and the installer networks behind them.** Installers are willing to recommend it because doing so means more money and less hassle.

**Counter-argument:** installer loyalty is profit-driven. If competitors (Huawei, GoodWe) launch similar five-in-one products with even better margins for installers, channel loyalty could collapse quickly. The distribution network is rented, not owned.

## III. Dissecting the 50% Gross Margin

A **50.1%** gross margin is extremely rare in the solar-storage industry. Peers typically run 20-30%, and even Enphase, the highest among them, only reaches ~45%. This number needs unpacking.

### Breaking Down the Gross Margin

| Factor | Contribution | Sustainability |
|------|------|---------|
| **Five-in-one integration premium** | Users pay a premium for a one-stop solution, while manufacturing cost is lower than the sum of separate components | Medium — the premium will shrink as competitors catch up |
| **Premium market positioning** | Focused on high-electricity-price markets in Europe, the US, and Australia, where users are less price-sensitive | Medium — price wars have already spread from China to overseas markets |
| **Early-mover market dividend** | Few competitors in stackable all-in-one systems gives strong pricing power | Low — Huawei, GoodWe, and others are catching up fast |
| **Software value-add** | SigenOS raises the product's added value | High — software moats tend to be more durable |
| **Scale effects** | The Nantong factory ramping up, output rising from a few thousand units to tens of thousands | High — benefits continue to compound as scale grows |

### But Prices Are Falling

This is a signal that's easy to overlook:

| Year | SigenStor Average Selling Price (RMB/Wh) | Change |
|------|:---------------------------:|:----:|
| 2023 | **3.17** | — |
| 2024 | **2.69** | -15% |
| First three quarters of 2025 | **2.22** | -17% |

*Source: Yema Finance, IPO prospectus*

Prices are falling, yet gross margin rose from 36.2% to 50.1% — meaning costs are falling even faster. This is a result of scale effects and supply-chain optimization.

**But the question is:** can falling costs keep offsetting the falling-price trend? If a flood of competitors accelerates the price decline while cost optimization is already near its limits, gross margin will turn downward.

**Most analyses suggest gross margin could fall from 50% back to 35-40% within 2-3 years.** That would still be top-tier for the industry, but the impact on valuation shouldn't be ignored — the profit pool implied by a 35% margin is on an entirely different scale than one implied by 50%.

## IV. Huawei-Pedigree Channel DNA

Sigenergy's founder, Xu Yingtong, spent **23 years** at Huawei (1999-2022), and his final position there was President of Huawei's Smart PV business. During his tenure, Huawei's solar inverters became **the No. 1 in global shipments**.

Xu Yingtong's core strength isn't technology — it's **distribution**.

### The Pace of Channel Building

| Date | Milestone |
|------|--------|
| May 2022 | Company founded |
| June 2023 | SigenStor product launched |
| End of 2024 | Coverage of **85 countries**, **172 distributors** |
| 2024 | **No. 1** in Australian residential storage shipments (surpassing Tesla Powerwall) |
| 2025 | Signed a 1GWh MOU with Lawrence & Hanson (a leading Australian distributor) |

*Source: IPO prospectus*

Going from zero to 85 countries in two years — this pace is nearly unprecedented in the solar-storage industry. The Huawei pedigree shows through vividly in channel building.

### Geographic Revenue Distribution

| Region | Revenue Share (First Three Quarters of 2025) | Characteristics |
|------|:------------------------:|------|
| Asia-Pacific (excluding mainland China) | **47.8%** | Australia is the core base; 4 of the top 5 customers are in Australia |
| Europe | **43.6%** | UK, Sweden, and Netherlands lead |
| Mainland China | **~0%** | Almost no presence in the domestic market |
| Other | **~8.6%** | Emerging markets like the Middle East and Latin America |

*Source: IPO prospectus*

Two points worth noting:

**First, 100% of revenue is overseas.** Sigenergy has almost no presence in mainland China — this is both an advantage (avoiding the domestic price war) and a risk (all its eggs are in the overseas basket).

**Second, customer concentration is on the high side.** The top 5 customers account for **48.6%** of revenue, and distributors account for **97.7%** of revenue. If one or two major distributors switched to a competitor, the impact would be significant.

## V. Single-Product Dependence — The Biggest Structural Risk

| Year | SigenStor Revenue Share |
|------|:-----------------:|
| 2023 | **96.4%** |
| First three quarters of 2024 | **~90.5%** |
| 2025 | **~93%** |

*Source: IPO prospectus*

**For comparison:** Apple's iPhone accounts for roughly 50% of its revenue, and the market already considers that a high level of dependence. Sigenergy's 93% is an extreme level.

Single-product dependence means:

1. **Anything that affects SigenStor affects the whole company.** A technology shift, a competitor substitute, a tariff barrier, a quality incident — any one of these could shake the entire business.

2. **There is no "second curve" to cushion the impact.** The company has already launched a commercial/industrial storage product, SigenStack, but its revenue contribution is currently negligible. If SigenStor's growth slows, the company's overall growth will stall.

3. **Supply-chain risk is concentrated.** The security of supply for core components (battery cells, IGBTs, PCBs) is the company's lifeline.

**Counter-argument:** many great companies were single-product-driven early on — Tesla built its start on the Model S/3, Apple on the iPhone. Single-product dependence isn't inherently fatal; what matters is whether the product matrix can be broadened within the available window. Sigenergy's SigenStack (commercial/industrial storage) and EV-charging businesses are just getting started; if SigenStor's share can fall below 80% by 2026, this risk would be easing.

**But the counter to the counter-argument:** it took Tesla 10 years to expand from the Model S to Model 3/Y plus energy plus autonomous driving. It took Apple 6 years to expand from the iPhone to the App Store, iPad, and services. Sigenergy is only 4 years old — expanding its product matrix takes time, and the competitive environment may not give it much of it.

## VI. Moat Assessment: Present, But Not Deep

Combining the analysis above, Sigenergy's moat can be assessed layer by layer:

| Moat Source | Depth | Durability | Notes |
|-----------|:----:|:-----:|------|
| **Five-in-one integrated design** | Medium | Low-medium | The design concept can be replicated; Huawei, GoodWe, and Tesla are all capable of doing it |
| **Distribution network** | High | Medium | 172 distributors form a near-term barrier, but profit-driven loyalty isn't durable |
| **Brand recognition** | Medium | Medium | Established a "premium solar-storage" position in Europe, the US, and Australia, but only 2 years of brand accumulation |
| **First-mover advantage** | High | Low | First mover in stackable all-in-one systems, but the window is only 1-2 years |
| **Software (SigenOS)** | Low-medium | Medium | AI energy management has value but isn't unique; data-volume disadvantage (installed base far smaller than Huawei's and Tesla's) |
| **Scale effects** | Medium | High | Nantong factory ramping up; costs falling as scale grows |

**Overall judgment: Sigenergy has a moat of moderate strength, coming mainly from first-mover advantage plus distribution network plus brand recognition. But this moat is being eroded by time and competition — the five-in-one integrated design is not an insurmountable technical barrier, and competitors catching up is only a matter of time.**

The moat's "shelf life" is roughly **1-2 years**. Whether Sigenergy can convert its first-mover advantage into a durable brand and ecosystem barrier within this window will determine its long-term fate.

## VII. Summary

Summarizing Sigenergy's business model in one table:

| Dimension | Assessment | Rating |
|------|------|:----:|
| User demand | Real and rigid — driven by the broad energy-transition trend; five-in-one saves money and hassle | Strong |
| Product strength | SigenStor leads peers by 1-2 years on user experience | Strong |
| Pricing power | The 50% gross margin shows currently strong pricing power, but prices are declining | Medium-strong |
| Distribution | 172 distributors across 85 countries, Huawei-pedigree execution | Strong |
| Single-product dependence | 93% of revenue from a single product, an extreme level | Weak (major risk) |
| Moat depth | Moderate, driven mainly by first-mover advantage, distribution, and brand; shelf life 1-2 years | Medium |
| Business complexity | Spans power electronics, electrochemistry, software, AI, and EV charging | High (unfavorable) |

**In one sentence: this is a good business, but not a "simple" good business — technical complexity is high, single-product dependence is heavy, and the moat isn't deep enough.**
