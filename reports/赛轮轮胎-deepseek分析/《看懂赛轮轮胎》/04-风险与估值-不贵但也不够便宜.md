## Laying Out All the Risks

The first three parts laid out Sailun's business model, competitive advantages, and industry landscape. If you only read those three, you might conclude this is a severely undervalued company. But between a good company and a good investment lies one critical question: **has every place things could go wrong already been fully reflected in the price?**

This piece does something less pleasant: **lay out everything that could go wrong, and only then decide what this company is worth.**

### Risk One: Global Trade Protectionism (the Biggest Risk)

**76.7%** of Sailun's revenue comes from overseas. This is both its biggest advantage and its biggest risk exposure.

The current tariff-siege landscape:

| Market | Current status | Trend |
|------|------|------|
| United States | Combined tariff rate on Chinese tires ~270%, direct export from China essentially impossible | Focus has shifted to imposing tariffs on Southeast Asia |
| European Union | Launched an anti-dumping investigation into Chinese passenger-car tires in May 2025 | Cambodia's EBA preferences may come under review |
| US → Southeast Asia | Vietnam ~45%, Cambodia ~61-74% (potential), Indonesia ~57% | Continuing to worsen |
| Brazil/Colombia/UK | Each launched anti-dumping investigations into Chinese tires | Clear bandwagon effect |

*Source: China Trade Remedy Information website, publicly available industry data. Rates vary by product and investigation outcome.*

**Worst-case scenario**: if the US imposes across-the-board tariffs on Southeast Asia (moving from "targeted" to "systemic") while the EU's anti-dumping investigation concludes with high anti-dumping duties on Chinese-owned Cambodian output, three of Sailun's five overseas bases (Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia) would see their core export logic severely undermined. Only Mexico (under the USMCA framework) and Egypt (serving Africa and the Middle East) might remain relatively safe.

But there's another side to consider: **trade barriers don't just hurt Sailun — they hurt every Chinese tire maker.** And Sailun has the broadest overseas footprint and the strongest ability to respond. If the industry as a whole is hurt, the leader could actually gain share, because smaller companies unable to withstand the tariff shock would exit first.

**The nature of this risk is an unpredictable political variable.** Any attempt to quantify it through "probability weighting" merely gives you a false sense of precision. What investors need to do is: **judge whether you can tolerate the "worst-case scenario," and how much Sailun would still be worth under that scenario.**

### Risk Two: The Raw-Material Cost Cycle

Natural rubber accounts for **30-40%** of tire raw-material costs. The average natural rubber price in 2025 was about **RMB 17,000/tonne**, up roughly **24%** year over year. The industry expects natural rubber may be entering a **20-year upcycle**, with a supply-demand gap of roughly 400,000 tonnes; newly planted rubber trees take **6-7 years** to reach tapping maturity (source: research notes citing industry data).

Sailun's gross margin ran as high as 27-28% in 2023-24, and roughly **60%** of that improvement came from falling raw-material costs (source: research notes citing Southwest Securities statistics). If raw-material costs keep rising, Sailun's long-run gross margin may stabilize around **22-24%** rather than 25%+.

**Buffering factors**: Sailun's overseas plants are located near natural rubber growing regions (Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia), giving it lower procurement costs than domestic producers; the company also has a forward price-locking hedging system. But these can only partially buffer the impact — they cannot fully offset it.

### Risk Three: Founder and Succession

Yuan Zhongxue, Sailun's founder and guiding spirit, will be **71** in 2026.

Yuan's strategic foresight is rare among Chinese manufacturing entrepreneurs: he decided to build a plant in Vietnam in 2012 (the first in the industry to do so), and sustained a decade of R&D investment in Liquid Gold technology until it earned third-party authoritative certification. Sailun's core competitive strengths today — global footprint plus Liquid Gold technology — trace almost entirely back to Yuan's personal decisions.

But the issue is:

| Dimension | Current status |
|------|------|
| Age | 71, now serves as honorary chairman |
| Shareholding | Personal stake about 2.63%; concert-party group holds roughly 14-18% combined (figures vary by source) |
| Governance structure | Family-flavored, concert-party arrangement |
| Successor | **Not publicly verified** |

**The core question isn't "is Yuan Zhongxue still capable today" — it's "would Sailun without Yuan Zhongxue still be the same company."**

Much of Sailun's competitiveness stems from management's strategic judgment rather than a process or technology that can be standardized and replicated. If a successor lacks the same foresight and execution, Sailun could go from "industry leader" to "industry follower."

**Counterpoint**: Sailun's global footprint has largely taken shape already (five overseas bases); even with a change in management, these physical assets and customer relationships won't vanish overnight. Moreover, public-company governance mechanisms (the board, independent directors, institutional investors) will to some degree constrain a successor's decision-making deviations.

### Risk Four: Receivables and Collections

In 2024, Sailun's accounts receivable reached **RMB 5.086 billion**, up **50%** year over year — significantly faster than revenue growth (22.4%). The receivables-to-revenue ratio reached roughly **16%**.

This is a signal worth watching, but not one to panic over:

- Receivables growing far faster than revenue may indicate the company loosened credit terms to boost volume
- It could also simply reflect the normal pattern of overseas expansion — large overseas customers (like Caterpillar) typically have longer payment terms than domestic distributors
- 2025 annual-report data will need to confirm whether this trend continues

**Warning line**: if the receivables-to-revenue ratio keeps climbing to **20%+**, collection risk needs to be taken seriously.

### Risk Five: "Overseas Involution"

By 2025, Cambodia alone hosted **11** Chinese-owned tire plants. Vietnam, Thailand, and Indonesia each also host multiple Chinese-owned firms. Chinese tire companies' overseas production capacity already exceeds **223 million units/year**.

This means the "excess profit" (high gross margins) that Chinese tire makers once captured overseas is being eroded by competition. The first-mover premium of building plants overseas is fading, turning into a new "industry standard."

Sailun's response is to **move into harder markets** — Mexico (close to the US market) and Egypt (serving Africa and the Middle East). But these new bases are still ramping up, and returns will take 3-5 years to fully materialize.

### A More Grounded Valuation Judgment

Setting aside precise calculations, a few common-sense checks:

**First**: a company earning RMB 3.5 billion a year (and growing), with a market cap of RMB 42.5 billion and a PE of 12x — in China's A-share manufacturing sector, this pricing is not expensive. An earnings yield of ~8.3%, against a Chinese 10-year government bond yield of about **2.5%**, implies a risk premium of about 5.8 percentage points — reasonable.

**Second**: profit fell 13% in 2025, but by 2026Q1 gross margin had already rebounded to 26.86%, with net profit up 1.7% year over year — the worst of the profit decline may already be behind it. If 2026 profit recovers to RMB 3.8-4.2 billion (a neutral assumption), the implied PE on today's market cap would fall to **10-11x** — squarely into undervalued territory.

**Third**: Sailun is not a "value trap that never rises" — revenue has doubled in 5 years, ROE has run 17-23% for years, and its brand ranks 10th globally. Its growth is data-supported and auditable.

**Fourth**: but Sailun is also not a "luxury good on a 70% discount" — it lacks a deep margin of safety. A 25% decline under the pessimistic scenario (to RMB 9.7) isn't enough for investors seeking Graham-style margin of safety.

**Conclusion: Sailun's current price is "not expensive, but not cheap enough."**

## Is Sailun Worth Investing In? It Depends on How You View Three Things

Having read all four parts, the "full picture" of Sailun Tire has essentially been presented. Whether to ultimately invest depends on your judgment on three core variables:

### Variable One: Where Is the Global Trade Environment Headed?

If you believe trade protectionism is near its peak and will ease moderately going forward, Sailun's global footprint will continue to pay off, and the current price is attractive.

If you believe trade protectionism will escalate further (the US imposing across-the-board tariffs on Southeast Asia, the EU's anti-dumping case being upheld), Sailun's most core competitive advantage would be undermined, and the current price doesn't carry enough margin of safety.

### Variable Two: How Long Will Natural Rubber Costs Stay Elevated?

If you believe the "20-year natural rubber upcycle" thesis is overly pessimistic and prices will retreat within 2-3 years, Sailun's gross margin will rebound to 26-28%, and profit elasticity will be substantial.

If you believe natural rubber has indeed entered a long-cycle uptrend, Sailun's long-run gross margin center will shift down to 22-24%, and profit growth will lag revenue growth.

### Variable Three: Can Sailun Stay Competitive Without Yuan Zhongxue?

If you believe Sailun's competitiveness has already been "institutionalized" into the organization through its five overseas bases and Liquid Gold technology, successor risk is manageable.

If you believe Sailun's strategic edge is highly dependent on Yuan Zhongxue personally, this risk needs to be compensated for with a bigger discount.

**Three variables, no standard answer. Different combinations of judgment will lead to entirely different investment conclusions.**

## Shareholder Returns: A Detail Worth Crediting

In sharp contrast to Pinduoduo's "not distributing a single yuan of its RMB 420 billion cash pile," Sailun Tire has been **proactive** on shareholder returns:

- Cumulative dividends since its A-share listing: **RMB 4.758 billion**
- Cumulative dividends over the past three years: **RMB 2.758 billion**
- 2024 fiscal-year dividend: approximately **RMB 493 million**

For a company currently at the peak of overseas capacity buildout (annual capex of RMB 2.5-3.5 billion), still being able to maintain a stable dividend indicates management has a "shareholder mindset" in capital allocation.

**Counterpoint**: a dividend of RMB 493 million against net profit of RMB 3.522 billion implies a payout ratio of about 14% — not exactly generous. Most profit is being plowed back into building overseas plants. But that's a reasonable choice during an expansion phase.

## Core Conclusions of This Series

Having written four parts, here are Sailun Tire's core conclusions condensed into five sentences:

1. **Sailun is one of the most competitive companies in China's tire industry** — revenue has doubled in 5 years, ROE has run 17-23%, its overseas footprint is the broadest, and its brand ranks 10th globally. In an industry that's "not great but not bad," Sailun has achieved relative excellence.

2. **Its global production footprint is its biggest competitive advantage — and its biggest risk exposure.** 76.7% of overseas revenue sits directly in the line of fire of trade protectionism.

3. **Liquid Gold technology and off-road tires are two valuable points of differentiation** — the former has first-mover advantage in the new-energy tire track, and the latter delivers high margins and customer stickiness. But the long-term sustainability of both needs continued observation.

4. **The current valuation is "not expensive, but not cheap enough."** PE ~12x, PEG<1 is a reasonable level for A-share manufacturing. It suits investors who can tolerate cyclical swings and have a 3-5 year horizon. Investors seeking a deep margin of safety need to wait for a lower price.

5. **The final investment decision hinges on three variables you cannot precisely predict** — the trade environment, the raw-material cycle, and management succession. Different combinations of judgment lead to entirely different conclusions.
