# A Global Tire-Making Machine

> Understanding Sailun Tire series · Episode 02 · Business Model
> Reading time: about 10 minutes

## Where Sailun's money comes from

In 2025, Sailun spread 36.792 billion yuan of revenue across four overseas plants — in Vietnam, Cambodia, Mexico, and Indonesia — plus two domestic bases. 76.7% of revenue came from outside China — this company headquartered in Qingdao is, in substance, a multinational manufacturer that just happens to carry a Chinese stock code.

To understand how this machine runs, we first need to break down three questions: what it sells, who it sells to, and where its gross margin comes from.

Sailun's business can be split into three product lines:

| Product line | Products | Customer profile | Estimated gross margin | Competitive landscape |
|--------|------|---------|:----------:|---------|
| **Semi-steel radial tires** | Passenger vehicle tires | Mainly replacement market + some OEM fitment | 22-25% | Highly competitive; Chinese companies' share continues to rise |
| **All-steel radial tires** | Truck/bus tires | Logistics and transport companies | 18-22% (estimate) | Fiercely competitive, high price sensitivity |
| **Off-the-road (OTR) tires** | Mining/construction-equipment/agricultural tires | Caterpillar, Rio Tinto, etc. | **30%+** (estimate) | High barriers, global oligopoly |

*Gross margins are estimates based on industry data and research notes, not company-disclosed segment margins*

The profit contribution of the three segments differs completely. Semi-steel tires are the **volume workhorse** — in 2025, Sailun's total tire production was **82.54 million units**, with sales of **81.13 million units** (source: 2025 annual report), with semi-steel tires making up the bulk. All-steel tires are the **traditional core base**. But what truly sets Sailun apart is its **off-the-road tire business**.

### Off-the-road tires: an underappreciated profit engine

Off-the-road (OTR) tires are the most valuable business in Sailun's portfolio, for three reasons:

**First, high technical barriers.** Giant mining tires can be up to 4 meters in diameter and weigh several tons, operating under extreme temperature and load conditions. Sailun rolled out the world's largest 53/80R63 giant tire in 2016, having begun laying the groundwork back in 2009 — more than 15 years of accumulated experience. This is not a field that can be caught up to quickly just by spending money.

**Second, strong customer stickiness.** Once a mining or construction company has qualified a particular brand of tire, switching costs are extremely high — every replacement requires new safety testing and performance validation. Sailun already supplies top global customers including Caterpillar, John Deere, CNH Industrial, and Sany Heavy Industry, and mining giants such as Rio Tinto also use Sailun tires.

**Third, large room for domestic substitution.** The global off-the-road tire market has long been dominated by Michelin and Bridgestone. Chinese companies are at a "zero to one" breakthrough stage in this field, and Sailun has gone the furthest domestically.

**Counterpoint**: Sailun's annual report does not break out detailed revenue and profit figures for the OTR business. The 30%+ gross margin above is an estimate based on industry benchmarking. If the mining cycle turns down (for instance, a collapse in commodity prices), OTR demand would fall sharply — this segment's cyclicality may be even stronger than that of semi-steel tires.

## Overseas vs. domestic: two very different versions of Sailun

Sailun's 2025 revenue by geography was as follows:

| Market | Revenue (RMB billion) | Share | YoY growth |
|------|:----------:|:----:|:-------:|
| Overseas | **28.226** | **76.7%** | +18.5% |
| Domestic | 8.566 | 23.3% | +7.2% (estimate) |
| Total | 36.792 | 100% | +15.7% |

*Data source: Sailun Tire 2025 annual report*

**With more than three-quarters of revenue coming from overseas**, Sailun is, in substance, closer to a "multinational company that happens to be headquartered in Qingdao" than a traditional "Chinese tire maker."

### The cost advantage of overseas plants

Sailun's overseas plants typically post gross margins **3-5 percentage points** higher than domestic plants (source: research notes, brokerage estimates), for several combined reasons:

| Source of advantage | Explanation |
|---------|------|
| Nearby raw-material sourcing | Vietnam, Cambodia, and Indonesia are close to natural rubber production regions, lowering transport costs |
| Tariff avoidance | Tariff rates on exports from Southeast Asia to Europe and the US are far lower than on direct exports from China |
| Labor cost | Manufacturing wages in Vietnam and Cambodia are about 40-60% of those in China |
| Land and policy incentives | Most overseas bases enjoy tax breaks from local governments seeking to attract investment |

This model of "build overseas → source locally → export at lower tariffs" is, at its core, a **three-layer arbitrage structure**: raw-material arbitrage + labor arbitrage + tariff arbitrage. Stacked together, these three layers form Sailun's core cost advantage relative to purely domestic producers.

**Counterpoint**: This arbitrage structure is being eroded by two forces. **First, Chinese tire companies are collectively moving overseas** — Cambodia already has 11 Chinese-invested tire plants, and Vietnam has several as well; competition is shifting from the domestic market to overseas, so "higher overseas margins" may not be sustainable. **Second, trade barriers are extending into Southeast Asia** — the US has already launched anti-dumping investigations on tires from Vietnam, Cambodia, and Thailand, with potential combined tariff rates of 45-61%. If tariffs "chase down" Southeast Asia, Sailun's most critical advantage would be undermined.

## Liquid Gold: a genuine technology moat, or a marketing concept?

"Liquid Gold tires" is Sailun's loudest label in recent years. What exactly is it?

### The technology itself

Traditional tire manufacturing is "physical compounding" — solid rubber and fillers are mixed through mechanical stirring. The problem with this approach is uneven filler dispersion, which means a tire's rolling resistance, wet grip, and wear resistance can typically only be optimized "two out of three" — the industry calls this the "devil's triangle."

Sailun's "Liquid Gold" technology is, at its core, "chemical compounding" — a liquid-phase mixing process that lets fillers bond with rubber uniformly at the molecular level, optimizing all three metrics simultaneously. This technology was first presented at the 2016 Hannover International Tire Technology Conference, with products officially launched in 2021.

### Certifications and results achieved

| Achievement | Detail |
|------|------|
| TUV MARK certification | Four tire models certified simultaneously under the world's strictest quality certification, the best result in the history of Chinese tire companies |
| AUTOBILD testing | Real-world testing by the authoritative German magazine showed performance exceeding some international first-tier brand products |
| New-energy vehicle fitment | In 2026, fitted on models including the BYD Sealion 06 EV and Geely Boyue REV |
| Replacement-market growth | Replacement-market sales up **50%** YoY in Q1 2025 |
| Luxury vehicle fitment | Over **15,000** vehicles fitted with Mercedes-Benz, BMW, and Audi in Q1 2025 |

*Data source: Xinhua, Sailun Group official website, brokerage research reports*

### Objective assessment

Liquid Gold technology is **a substantive innovation**, not a pure marketing concept. TUV certification is authoritative third-party certification, and AUTOBILD is one of the most authoritative tire-testing media outlets globally — these are not things you can simply buy.

But two points deserve a level-headed look:

**First, weak consumer awareness.** The decision path for most drivers replacing tires is "brand → price → size," not "what compounding process was used in this tire." Liquid Gold has a reputation in professional circles, but its brand premium power among mass consumers falls far short of Michelin's "quiet ride" or Pirelli's "performance" positioning.

**Second, the durability of the technical barrier is uncertain.** Tire industry technology generally iterates slowly, but the R&D spending of giants like Michelin and Bridgestone is tens of times that of Sailun. If liquid-phase mixing technology proves commercially valuable, the giants are unlikely to sit idle. Sailun's window may be **5-10 years**, not permanent.

## A complete picture of how the money is made

Putting the above together, Sailun's business model can be understood as follows:

```
Raw material procurement (natural rubber, carbon black, etc.)
    ↓
Manufacturing (domestic Qingdao/Dongying + five overseas bases)
    ↓
Three product lines (semi-steel + all-steel + off-the-road tires)
    ↓
Sales channels
  ├── Replacement market (70%+ of demand) → distributors → end drivers/fleets
  └── OEM fitment → automakers (BYD, Geely, etc.)
```

**Three sources of profit**:

1. **Volume growth**: Continued release of overseas capacity (Mexico and Indonesia ramping up, Egypt to be built) supports continued double-digit revenue growth
2. **Structural upgrade**: Rising share of Liquid Gold tires, larger-size semi-steel tires, and off-the-road tires lifts overall gross margin
3. **Cost arbitrage**: The three-layer advantage of raw materials + labor + tariffs at overseas plants

**Three threats to profit**:

1. **Raw material costs**: Natural rubber accounts for 30-40% of costs, and its price is uncontrollable
2. **Trade barriers**: Tariff "encirclement" is extending from China into Southeast Asia
3. **Overseas overcrowding**: Chinese tire companies are collectively moving overseas, shifting competition from the domestic market to overseas markets

## A key comparison: Sailun vs. peers

| Company | 2025 Revenue (RMB billion) | 2025 Net Profit (RMB billion) | Gross margin | ROE | Overseas capacity footprint |
|------|:--------------:|:--------------:|:------:|:---:|------------|
| **Sailun Tire** | **36.8** | **3.52** | **24.7%** | **17.0%** | Vietnam/Cambodia/Indonesia/Mexico/Egypt |
| Linglong Tire | ~24.0 (estimate) | ~1.7 (estimate) | ~18% (estimate) | ~8% (estimate) | Thailand/Serbia/Brazil (planned) |
| Sentury Tire | ~9.5 (estimate) | ~1.6 (estimate) | ~28% (estimate) | ~15% (estimate) | Thailand/Morocco |
| Triangle Tyre | ~10.5 (estimate) | ~1.2 (estimate) | ~19% (estimate) | ~10% (estimate) | No large-scale overseas capacity |

*Linglong, Sentury, and Triangle data are estimates based on public information*

**Sailun's distinguishing features among the four**: the largest revenue scale, the highest ROE, and the broadest overseas footprint. Sentury has a higher gross margin (focused on premium semi-steel tires), but its scale is far smaller than Sailun's. Linglong has stronger brand recognition, but its profitability and overseas footprint are notably weaker than Sailun's.

**Counterpoint**: Sailun's 2025 gross margin of 24.7% is lower than the 27-28% of 2023-24. If natural rubber prices keep rising (the industry expects to enter a 20-year up-cycle), Sailun's long-term gross margin may stabilize at **22-24%** rather than 25%+. This would mean the high margins of 2023-24 were "a windfall from the low point of the raw-material cycle," not the new normal.

## Capital expenditure: the cost of expansion

Sailun is currently in a **capital-intensive period of overseas capacity building**. Estimated annual capex is **2.5-3.5 billion yuan** (source: derived from research notes), significantly higher than depreciation (estimated at 1.0-1.5 billion yuan/year).

This implies two things:

1. **Free cash flow is temporarily tight.** Operating cash flow in 2024 was about 2.26 billion yuan; after subtracting capex, free cash flow was close to zero or even negative. Operating cash flow improved to about 4.14 billion yuan in 2025, but after subtracting roughly 3.0 billion yuan of capex, free cash flow was only about **1.1 billion yuan** — a low conversion rate relative to net profit of 3.5 billion yuan.

2. **This is an active strategic investment, not passive deterioration.** Sailun is putting the cash it earns into new capacity in Mexico, Indonesia, and Egypt — trading today's cash flow for tomorrow's capacity and market share. The question is: **will these investments ultimately generate sufficient returns?**

**Counterpoint**: Sustained, massive capex is a classic feature of a "capital black hole." Unlike an internet business, the tire industry requires continuous maintenance and equipment upgrades even after a factory is built. If an industry downturn happens to coincide with the peak of capacity building, Sailun's balance sheet would come under significant pressure. In 2025, the current ratio was only **1.13** and the quick ratio only **0.66** — tight for a manufacturing company.

## Core conclusions of this episode

1. **Sailun's business model is clear but has structural weaknesses.** Tire manufacturing is an asset-heavy, strongly cyclical business with low switching costs and weak pricing power. Sailun has done relatively well within this industry, but the industry's own "ceiling" means it is not a "great business."

2. **Its overseas capacity footprint is Sailun's biggest competitive advantage.** Five bases on five continents, 5-10 years of head-start experience, and the strongest tariff-avoidance capability in the industry. But this advantage is now facing being "chased down" — trade barriers extending into Southeast Asia, plus "overseas overcrowding" from the collective move abroad by Chinese competitors.

3. **Liquid Gold technology and off-the-road tires are two valuable points of differentiation.** The former lifts product premium, the latter provides high margins and customer stickiness. But the long-term sustainability of both requires continued observation.

4. **Capex intensity is high, and free cash flow is under temporary pressure.** This is a normal feature of an expansion phase, but it also means the company's near-term "cash distribution capacity" is limited.
