## The Global Tire Market: A Power Transfer in Progress

The combined global tire share of the three giants — Michelin, Bridgestone, and Goodyear — has fallen from a peak of 56% to 35-39%. Where did that share go? The overwhelming majority of it was taken by Chinese companies.

This power transfer didn't happen by accident — it has deep structural causes, and Sailun is precisely the Chinese company that has gone furthest in this transfer. To understand Sailun's future, you first have to understand the logic of this market.

The global tire market is worth more than **USD 235 billion** (source: China Rubber Industry Association magazine), and it has several structural features worth noting:

**First, demand is essentially perpetual.** As long as cars are on the road, tires need replacing. Replacement tires account for **70%+** of global tire demand, which means that even if new-car sales decline, tire demand won't fall off a cliff.

**Second, the global landscape is shifting dramatically.** According to *Tire Business* magazine's 2025 Global Tire Company 75 ranking, the industry landscape looks like this:

| Rank | Company | 2024 sales | Characteristics |
|:----:|------|:----------:|------|
| 1 | Michelin | **USD 27.6 billion** | World No. 1, deepest technology and brand barriers |
| 2 | Bridgestone | **USD 24.0 billion** (estimate) | Japanese giant, largest by size but growth has stalled |
| 3 | Goodyear | **USD 17.0 billion** (estimate) | In operational difficulty in recent years, weak profitability |
| 4 | Continental | **USD 13.0 billion** (estimate) | Strong in the European market |
| ... | ... | ... | ... |
| Around top 10 | **Sailun Tire** | **~USD 5.0 billion** | Ranked 10th globally in brand value by Brand Finance |

*Source: US *Tire Business* magazine's 2025 annual ranking, CompaniesMarketCap, Brand Finance*

The key change is that **the combined global market share of the three giants (Michelin, Bridgestone, Goodyear) has fallen from a peak of 56% to 35-39%** (source: research notes citing industry data). Who took it? Mainly Chinese and South Korean companies.

### The Rise of China's Tire Army

China is currently the world's largest tire producer, accounting for roughly **40%** of global output. But for a long time, Chinese tire companies were "big but not strong" — first in the world by volume, yet far behind international giants in profit margin and brand value.

That picture has started to change over the past 5 years. The core driver of the change is **building plants overseas**.

| Chinese company | Number of overseas plants | Overseas countries | Overseas capacity (estimate) |
|---------|:----------:|------------|:------------:|
| **Sailun Tire** | **5** | Vietnam / Cambodia / Indonesia / Mexico / Egypt | Broadest in the industry |
| Linglong Tire | 2+1 (planned) | Thailand / Serbia / Brazil (planned) | Moderate |
| Sentury Tire | 2 | Thailand / Morocco | Smaller |
| Chemchina (Aeolus/Double Coin group) | 2+ | Thailand / Indonesia | Moderate |
| Cheng Shin (Maxxis) | Multiple | Vietnam / Thailand / India | Larger |

*Source: company announcements, industry news*

As of 2025, Chinese tire companies have built overseas production capacity exceeding **223 million units/year**, spread across **12 countries** (source: publicly available industry data). This is an unprecedented "tire age of exploration."

Sailun is the company that has gone **earliest and furthest** in this age of exploration. When it built a plant in Vietnam in 2012, no other Chinese tire company had the nerve to do the same.

**Counterpoint**: precisely because Sailun's "first bite" worked out so well, latecomers have swarmed in. Cambodia alone now hosts **11** Chinese-owned tire plants, and Vietnam has several as well. Building plants overseas was once a "blue ocean"; it's now turning into a "red ocean." The first mover's excess profit is being diluted.

## The Real Logic of "Going Overseas": Not a Choice, But a Necessity

Many people assume Chinese tire companies building plants overseas is "icing on the cake" — nice to have, not essential.

The truth is the opposite. **Not going overseas means dying.**

The reason lies in a set of tariff figures:

| Export route | Combined tariff rate (estimate) | Result |
|---------|:---------------:|------|
| China → US | **~270%** | Essentially sealed off |
| Vietnam → US | **~45%** | Barely workable, but margins get squeezed |
| Cambodia → US | **~61-74% (potential)** | Currently worsening |
| Cambodia → EU | Benefits from EBA tariff relief | Still an advantage for now |
| Mexico → US | Lower under the USMCA framework | This is the core logic behind Sailun's Mexico plant |

*Source: China Trade Remedy Information website, publicly available industry data. Specific rates vary by product type and investigation outcome.*

Several major events in 2025 intensified the tariff siege:

1. **The EU launched an anti-dumping investigation into Chinese passenger-car tires** (May 2025) — meaning the two core Western markets, Europe and the US, have essentially both "closed the door" on Chinese tires around the same time
2. **The US launched a second sunset review of the dual anti-dumping/countervailing case on Chinese passenger and light-truck tires** (January 2026)
3. **Brazil, Colombia, the UK**, and other countries have simultaneously launched anti-dumping investigations

In one sentence: **the era of exporting tires directly from China to Europe and the US is essentially over. Building plants overseas isn't "a better option" — it's "the only way out."**

This is also why Sailun's 2012 decision to build a plant in Vietnam was so critical — it made that call a full 5 years ahead of the industry.

**Counterpoint**: even exports from Southeast Asia are facing rising tariffs. The US has already begun imposing anti-dumping duties on tires from Vietnam, Cambodia, and Thailand. If US policy keeps "chasing down" its targets — from China to Southeast Asia, and now to Mexico — then Sailun's entire globalization logic would need to be reassessed. Sailun's Mexico plant (leveraging the USMCA framework) is one response to this, but if the US doesn't even respect the USMCA framework, there is no "safe harbor" left.

## New-Energy Vehicle Tires: The Next Structural Opportunity

The global tire industry is undergoing a new structural shift: **new-energy vehicle tires**.

EVs place fundamentally different demands on tires compared with gasoline vehicles:

| Characteristic | EV vs. gasoline vehicle | Tire requirement |
|------|----------------|------------|
| Weight | EVs are 20-30% heavier (battery weight) | Requires higher load capacity |
| Torque | EVs deliver instant, large torque | Requires stronger grip and wear resistance |
| Noise | No engine noise, so tire noise is more noticeable | Requires stronger noise suppression |
| Range anxiety | Owners care about every kilometer of range | Requires lower rolling resistance |

Taken together, these requirements mean **specialized EV tires are priced 50%+ higher than conventional tires**, and the industry expects a compound growth rate of **8-10%** (source: brokerage research reports).

Sailun has two advantages in this space:

1. **Liquid Gold technology is a natural fit for EV needs** — the "triangle optimization" of low rolling resistance + quietness + wear resistance is exactly what electric vehicles need most
2. **It has already won OEM supply contracts** — in 2026, it supplies the BYD Sealion 06 EV (as the primary supplier) and the Geely Boyue REV (as the exclusive supplier)

But the disadvantages are equally clear: in the OEM (original vehicle fitment) market, international brands like Michelin and Continental still dominate. Sailun's current OEM supply is concentrated mainly on domestic Chinese new-energy vehicle models; there's still a long way to go before it can break into the OEM supply lists of luxury brands like Mercedes-Benz, BMW, and Audi.

**Counterpoint**: new-energy tires are a good track, but every tire company is competing for it. Michelin launched a dedicated EV tire line in 2024, and Bridgestone is also stepping up its investment. Sailun's Liquid Gold technology has a first-mover advantage, but how long that lead can be sustained once the giants pour in heavy investment remains an open question.

## The Natural Rubber Upcycle: An Unavoidable Cost Variable

Discussing Sailun's profit outlook can't sidestep the question of natural rubber prices.

Natural rubber accounts for **30-40%** of tire raw-material costs, the single biggest variable affecting tire makers' gross margins. Here's the trend over the past two years:

| Period | Natural rubber price (RMB/tonne, estimate) | Change |
|------|:---------------------:|------|
| 2023 average | ~13,000-14,000 | At low levels |
| 2024 average | ~15,000-16,000 | Beginning to rise |
| 2025 average | ~17,000 (+24% YoY) | Rising significantly |

*Source: research notes citing industry data*

There's an unsettling judgment circulating in the industry: **natural rubber may be entering a 20-year upcycle.**

The reasoning: the global natural rubber supply-demand gap is roughly **400,000 tonnes** (source: research notes citing industry data), and newly planted rubber trees take **6-7 years** from planting to tappable maturity, so the supply side responds very slowly.

If this thesis holds, it means:

1. Sailun's gross-margin center could shift down from 27-28% in 2023-24 to **22-24%**
2. Rising costs would raise the industry's barriers to entry, which favors the market leader (favorable for Sailun)
3. Every tire company would feel the squeeze, but those with stronger cost control (such as overseas plants sourcing locally) would be less affected

Sailun has two buffers against raw-material costs: first, its Cambodia plant is near natural rubber growing regions, giving it lower procurement costs; second, the company has a forward price-locking hedging system. But these can only buffer the impact, not fully offset it.

**This variable is beyond management's control.** It's an external risk that investors need to judge the probability of on their own.

## Sailun in Ten Years: Global Top Five, or Just Another Also-Ran?

Here's a bold projection: if everything goes well, could Sailun realistically break into the global top five tire companies in 10 years?

**Optimistic path**:

- All five overseas bases run at full capacity, with annual output doubling
- Liquid Gold technology builds global brand recognition
- The OTR business completes domestic substitution and enters the global top three
- Annual revenue surpasses **RMB 80-100 billion**
- Global ranking enters the top 5-7

**Pessimistic path**:

- Global trade protectionism worsens to an extreme degree, and even the overseas plants can't escape tariffs
- Natural rubber stays high for the long term, compressing gross margin below 20%
- "Overseas involution" drags overseas plant margins back down to domestic levels
- The successor lacks sufficient ability, and strategic direction drifts
- Revenue growth slows to single digits, and profit keeps declining

The research notes estimate: **Sailun has roughly a 35-40% probability of entering the global top five within 10 years.** That probability isn't high, but given that the global tire industry is undergoing a structural "China substitution" shift, it isn't low either.

**The core variable isn't Sailun itself — it's the global trade environment.** If the trade environment holds steady or worsens only moderately, Sailun's global footprint will continue to pay off. If the trade environment deteriorates to an extreme degree (an all-out trade war), Sailun's core logic would be shaken.

## Core Takeaways from This Part

1. **The global tire landscape is shifting from "three-giant dominance" to "multipolarity."** Chinese companies' global share keeps rising, and over a 5-10 year horizon this trend is very likely irreversible.

2. **"Going overseas" isn't optional for Chinese tire companies — it's a matter of survival.** The path of exporting directly from China to Europe and the US is essentially sealed off. Sailun went overseas earliest and has the broadest footprint; its first-mover advantage is real, but it's being diluted by latecomers.

3. **New-energy tires are the next structural opportunity.** Sailun's Liquid Gold technology fits EV needs and has already won supply contracts, but there's still a substantial gap to close on international-brand OEM supply.

4. **The natural rubber upcycle is a medium-to-long-term profit headwind.** Sailun's overseas plants provide some cost buffer, but not a full hedge. Investors need to form their own judgment on raw-material costs.

5. **The biggest uncertainty comes from the global trade environment.** This is the core variable determining Sailun's investment value, and also the least predictable one.
