# The Quality of Tencent's Management

> *Understanding Tencent* series · Part 07 · Management
> Reading time: about 11 minutes

---

## I. The Same Thing That Keeps Showing Up in Value Investing

Across 70 years of value-investing tradition, alongside "good business" and "good price," one factor keeps getting emphasized and is easy to overlook — **management quality**. The logic behind it is simple:

- **Knowing "what not to do" matters more than knowing "what to do"** — making fewer big mistakes is enough to beat most peers.
- **Buying a stock is buying a company, and buying a company is buying people** — a long-term holding is ultimately entrusting your money to a group of people.

Why does this matter?

Because products and technology change (ten years ago you could not have predicted AI), but people's character does not. A management team with integrity, competence, and passion **will make shareholder-friendly decisions across different technology cycles**. A team lacking those traits **will destroy value at every opportunity**.

In this piece, we use that standard to take Tencent's management apart and examine it.

Most members of this team have been in place for more than 20 years.

## III. Martin Lau's 21 Years

If you can remember only one name, remember **Martin Lau**.

He is the key figure behind Tencent's transformation from a "product company" into a "platform empire."

### Key Contribution 1: Building an Investment Empire (2011-2021)

Over the past 15 years, Tencent has invested in more than **800 companies**, including:

- **15 that generated returns exceeding $1 billion**
- **6 that generated returns exceeding $5 billion**
- Examples: JD.com (invested in 2014; the 2021 in-kind distribution and partial sell-down generated a gain of roughly RMB 80 billion), Meituan (profit of more than RMB 100 billion), Sea Limited (a gain of roughly RMB 60 billion), Riot Games (**cumulative investment of roughly $700 million, current equity value of roughly $23 billion, a return of roughly 33x over roughly 18 years** — the commonly cited "58x" figure refers only to the 2011 controlling-stake valuation, not the cumulative-investment basis), Supercell, Epic Games, and more.

These are not coincidences. Martin Lau and James Mitchell led all of Tencent's major investment decisions; both have top-tier investment-banking backgrounds and are skilled capital allocators.

### Key Contribution 2: Executing the Strategic Pullback (2022-2024)

More impressively, starting in 2022, Martin Lau led Tencent through a **deliberate pullback** — cutting the pace of investment from an average of 300+ deals a year down to just 20-40.

What does that mean?

It means he **acknowledged the diminishing marginal returns on external investment** — instead of continuing to "cast a wide net," the company redirected capital toward buying back its own stock.

This kind of deliberate pullback and reduction in external-investment scale as a capital-allocation choice **is relatively rare among comparable decisions made by Chinese internet companies over the same period** — though that comparison is, of course, qualitative rather than based on an exhaustive statistical survey.

### Key Contribution 3: The 2023 "Stabilized" Declaration

In early 2023, Martin Lau announced on an investor call that Tencent had **"stabilized"** and would enter a new period of high-quality growth.

Looking back, that call **played out exactly as stated**:
- 2023 Non-IFRS net profit: +36%
- 2024: +41%
- 2025: +17%

He spotted the inflection point roughly two years ahead of the market.

## IV. Alignment of Interests

Ma Huateng holds 8.72% of Tencent (per the disclosure as of the end of 2024) — at the current market capitalization of roughly RMB 3.76 trillion, that stake is worth **roughly RMB 328 billion**.

The overwhelming majority of his wealth comes from the appreciation of his shareholding. When Tencent's stock falls, he loses the most; when it rises, he gains the most. The founder's interests and shareholders' interests are aligned.

## V. The Most Critical Integrity Test — The 2021-2022 Regulatory Storm

Value investing has a frequently cited test: **you only find out who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out.** A management team's quality is not visible in good times — it shows up in adversity.

Tencent's tide went out once, in 2021-2022.

Let's revisit that period:
- Antitrust fines
- A 263-day freeze on new game licenses
- The "830" rules restricting minors' game time
- The "double reduction" education crackdown that wiped out the online education business entirely
- The share price collapsed from HKD 750 to the HKD 200 range — a **73% decline**
- The market broadly considered Tencent "finished"

How did Tencent's management respond?

### 1. No complaining

In public, Ma Huateng **never complained about regulatory policy** — a rare degree of restraint.

### 2. Actively embracing regulation

Tencent was **the first to publish** a minors' gaming-time calendar, and its anti-addiction measures are the strictest in the industry.

By 2025, minors accounted for only 0.2% of Tencent's total game time — the lowest level in the industry.

### 3. Responding with action rather than words

In 2022, the company proactively cut loss-making businesses and reined in its pace of investment, rather than waiting until it was forced to act.

### 4. Protecting employees' basic interests

Even while cutting costs and headcount, the company offered **relatively generous severance packages by industry standards** — there were no large-scale labor disputes.

### 5. Sustained buybacks to support the share price

When panic selling drove the share price down to the HKD 316 range in 2022, Tencent **bought back HKD 33.8 billion worth of stock** — putting real money behind a signal of confidence to the market.

In hindsight, that buyback stands out as **one of the best-timed buybacks in Hong Kong stock market history**.

## VI. Five-Year Promise-Delivery Rate: Near 100% on Quantifiable Short/Mid-Term Commitments, With Some Slippage on Long-Horizon Visions

Talking about integrity requires looking not just at what was said, but at what was actually done.

Tencent management's core public commitments over the past five years:

| Commitment | Date Made | Delivery |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Cost cutting and efficiency gains | Late 2022 | ✅ Headcount optimized by 3,000+ in 2023; profit up 36% |
| Monetizing Channels (Video Accounts) | Early 2023 | ✅ GMV up 3x; ad monetization launched |
| Focus on premium games | Late 2022 | ✅ Premium-game strategy succeeded; DnF Mobile took off |
| Increase shareholder returns | 2023 | ✅ Buybacks rose from HKD 33.8 billion (2022) to HKD 112 billion (2024) to HKD 80 billion (2025) |
| Raise dividends | 2023 | ✅ Per-share dividend rose from HKD 2.4 to 3.4 to 4.5 to 5.3, increasing every year |
| Profitable cloud business | Early 2025 | ✅ Confirmed reaching scaled profitability in Q4 2025 |
| Double AI investment | Late 2025 | ⏳ In progress; capex has already hit a record high |

**Commitments due so far, delivered: 6/6**

But to be honest, we should also flag counterexamples — this table only covers commitments made "with a clear target and already due" since 2022; earlier commitments do not all have as clean a track record:

- **Industrial internet (the "930 Reorganization" announced in 2018)**: In its 2018 organizational restructuring, Tencent pledged to make its to-B business "half the empire." Progress was slower than expected — CSIG ran losses for years and did not reach scaled profitability until Q4 2025.
- **Yuanbao / the consumer-facing AI-assistant entry point**: Internally in 2024, expectations for Yuanbao were to reach MAU comparable to Doubao; the actual gap is more than 6x (57.35 million vs. 345 million) — a clear miss.
- **International games**: Tencent has emphasized global expansion since 2017-2018, but overseas revenue did not see a real breakout until 2024-2025 — slower to materialize than the earlier stated timeline.

Once these counterexamples are included, a more accurate judgment emerges: **Tencent management has a high delivery rate (near 100% over the past five years) on "pragmatic, quantifiable, short-to-mid-term" commitments, but like most companies, has seen delays and shortfalls on "visionary, long-horizon" commitments**. This is a fact that still leans positive, but should not be read as "management can do no wrong."

## VII. Capital Allocation — The Most Important Job of a CEO in the Value-Investing Framework

The value-investing framework repeatedly emphasizes one point: **the CEO's most important job is capital allocation** — deciding how to spend the money the company earns each year.

**Capital allocation = how do you best spend the money the company earns each year?**

Tencent generates roughly RMB 180 billion of free cash flow a year. Possible uses include:

| Use | Value Judgment |
|------|------|
| Reinvestment in the core business (AI capex) | Necessary, but ROIC still to be proven |
| External investment | Historical returns of 5-15%, with diminishing marginal returns |
| **Buybacks** | ✅ **Create value when PE is below intrinsic value** |
| **Dividends** | ✅ **Avoid destroying value** |
| Cash hoarding | ⚠️ Fails to keep pace with inflation (2% vs. 5%) |

Tencent's capital-allocation choices over the past five years:

```
✅ AI capex (RMB 79.2 billion) — a necessary bet
✅ Buybacks (RMB 80-112 billion/year) — opportunistic, buying more when the price is low
✅ Dividends (per-share dividend up from HKD 2.4 to 5.3) — steady growth
✅ Trimming non-strategic holdings (distributing JD.com and Meituan stakes to shareholders) — deliberate slimming down
✅ Retaining strategic holdings (Riot, Epic, Pinduoduo, ByteDance) — long-term compounding
❌ No large premium-priced acquisitions (avoiding classic value destruction)
❌ No scattershot cross-industry bets (e.g., new energy, chips, real estate)
```

This list, checked against the traditional value-investing framework for capital allocation (as articulated repeatedly by Buffett and Duan Yongping) — growth capex + opportunistic buybacks + steady dividends + proactive trimming of non-strategic holdings + no scattershot cross-industry investment — largely lines up.

Using the "one-dollar test" (every dollar of retained earnings should create at least one dollar of market value): **Tencent's cumulative retained earnings over the past five years total roughly RMB 600 billion (rough calculation: roughly RMB 980 billion of cumulative Non-IFRS net profit over five years, minus roughly RMB 150 billion of cumulative dividends, minus roughly RMB 230 billion of cumulative buybacks, leaving roughly RMB 600 billion retained); over the same period, shareholder market value fluctuated from roughly HKD 5.7 trillion in early 2021 to roughly HKD 4.29 trillion in 2026 — market value alone actually declined.**

But the one-dollar test should not rely solely on a market-value snapshot — a more robust framing is "retained earnings + shareholder returns vs. total five-year return (including dividends + buyback cancellations + share price)." On that rough basis, Tencent's five-year total return is positive and Non-IFRS EPS has roughly doubled — on the capital-allocation dimension, it passes the one-dollar test. But it must be acknowledged that looking at "market value" alone, the past five years have not looked good — this reflects the systemic de-rating of Hong Kong equity valuations that has weighed on all Chinese ADRs and H-shares, not a failure of capital allocation unique to Tencent.

Ma Huateng is not a CEO who makes headlines every day, but his remarks at the company's annual all-hands are widely reported by the media each year, so they can be checked against results after the fact.

This is one of the CEO qualities that value investing prizes.

## IX. The One Lingering Concern — Succession

Tencent's management scores close to an A across nine dimensions, and the one area that remains "to be watched" is **succession planning**.

Ma Huateng is 54 — not old, but not young either.

Assessment of potential successors:

| Potential Successor | Current Role | Readiness |
|-----------|---------|--------|
| Martin Lau | President | **A** — already the de facto operator |
| Dowson Tong | President of CSIG | Moderate — extensive cloud-business experience |
| Ma Xiaoyi | Head of gaming business | Moderate — a gaming-domain expert |
| Allen Zhang | President of the WeChat Business Group | Low — no personal interest in the role, but exceptionally strong product ability |

**No cause for concern in the short term** — Martin Lau is fully capable of running day-to-day operations, and in fact already does.

**Manageable in the medium term** — James Mitchell and John Lo provide strategic and financial stability.

**Something to watch in the long term** — Ma Huateng's product instincts and cultural influence are hard to replace.

Reference case: after Steve Jobs's death, Apple sustained continued prosperity on the strength of Tim Cook's operational ability. Tencent has Martin Lau as its "operations general" — but whether the succession will be smooth can only be confirmed once it actually happens; this piece does not make a strong prediction either way.

## X. Tencent Through the Lens of the "Three Questions for Buying People"

Value investing has a simple framework for judging "the people" — three questions:

### Question 1: Is this person honest enough (integrity)?

**Answer: Yes.**

Ma Huateng and Martin Lau have delivered on close to 100% of their commitments over the past five years. During the 2021-2022 regulatory storm, they did not shirk responsibility or over-promise — instead they pragmatically cut loss-making businesses and increased shareholder returns. The vast majority of Ma Huateng's wealth comes from his shareholding, fully aligning his interests with shareholders'.

### Question 2: Is this person capable enough (capable)?

**Answer: Yes.**

From product to strategy to capital allocation, Tencent's management team has an extremely broad circle of competence. Ma Huateng's product instincts (WeChat, Channels), Martin Lau's investment judgment (astonishing returns on JD.com/Meituan/Sea), James Mitchell's strategic planning, and John Lo's financial discipline — this is a highly complementary team.

The 2025 results — RMB 751.8 billion in revenue, RMB 259.6 billion in Non-IFRS net profit, RMB 85.7 billion in R&D spending, and HKD 121 billion in combined buybacks and dividends (HKD 80 billion in buybacks plus HKD 41 billion in dividends) — are the most direct expression of that capability.

### Question 3: Does this person love the business enough (passion)?

**Answer: Yes.**

Ma Huateng has led Tencent for 28 years, with his energy consistently focused on the business itself. Martin Lau has stayed for 21 years. The core team's average tenure exceeds 20 years.

This kind of long-term passion is extremely rare among global technology companies.

**All three questions pass.** Once the "people" test is passed, the judgments on "the business" and "the price" become meaningful.

## XII. A Closing Thought

Value investing has a repeatedly validated observation:

> **When a management team with a good reputation meets a business with a bad reputation, it is usually the business's reputation that ultimately survives intact.**

The implication: **a good management team can only make a good business better — it cannot save a bad business.**

But conversely — **a bad management team can destroy even the best business.**

Tencent's "business" has passed the moat test, and its "management" has earned an A-grade score under this framework. This combination of a passing business plus a passing management team does not appear very often in the Chinese internet industry — but passing this framework does not mean it will necessarily pass yours; in the end it depends on how much weight you choose to give to moat and management.

Put this fact together with the subsequent judgment on "price," and you get a complete investment framework: **good business + good management + reasonable price — all three must pass before long-term holding is worthwhile.** In the next piece, we answer the last question: "price."

## Key Takeaways From This Piece

1. **Ma Huateng holds 8.72% of shares, so his wealth is fully tied to the share price** — the founder's interests and shareholders' interests are aligned.

4. **The delivery rate on quantifiable short/mid-term commitments is near 100%** (long-horizon, visionary commitments such as the industrial internet, Yuanbao's MAU, and the pace of overseas games have seen delays) — a hard indicator of integrity.

6. **Succession is the only lingering concern** — but Martin Lau is already the de facto successor.
7. **All three "buying people" questions (honest / capable / passionate) pass** — this is the precondition for the valuation discussion that follows.
