## Putting All the Risks on the Table

The conclusion of the first three parts can be compressed into one sentence: **Uber is a money-making machine, but hanging over its head is a sword called "autonomous driving."** The profitability is real, the moat is measurable, but long-term survival carries a huge question mark.

This is the finale, and it has only two jobs: **lay out every risk one by one, then work out whether a 13x P/E is actually cheap or expensive.**

---

## Risk One: Regulation — Driver Classification

### What This Risk Is

Uber's business model rests on one key assumption: **drivers are "independent contractors," not "employees."**

If drivers were classified as employees, Uber would have to shoulder:
- Social security, health insurance, unemployment insurance
- Minimum wage guarantees
- Paid leave
- Workers' compensation

This would **increase Uber's operating costs by 20-30%**, directly eating into margins.

### The Current State

| Region | Regulatory development | Impact on Uber |
|------|---------|---------------|
| California | Prop 22 passed in 2020, preserving independent contractor status | Favorable for now, but litigation continues |
| New Jersey | **New rule passed in May 2026** that could classify drivers as employees | Negative signal |
| European Union | 2025 Platform Work Directive implemented | Requires more protections for some drivers |
| United States (federal) | Department of Labor announced in May 2025 it will no longer enforce Biden-era rule | Short-term relief, but policy uncertainty is rising |

(Sources: Jersey Vindicator, SEC filings)

### Two Sides

**The pessimistic view**: New Jersey could be the first domino to fall. If more states follow, Uber's margins could be squeezed by 5-10 percentage points. California's retroactive gig-worker compensation litigation involves billions of dollars.

**The optimistic view**: Uber has already proven its political lobbying muscle with California's Prop 22 (spending $200 million to win the ballot measure). The flexibility of gig work has broad support from both drivers and consumers, so full-scale "employeeization" faces significant political resistance. And even if employee status is implemented in some regions, Uber can pass along part of the cost through price increases.

### Quantified Estimate

If major U.S. states (accounting for more than 50% of Uber's U.S. revenue) classified drivers as employees:
- Operating costs would rise by roughly **20-30%**
- Assuming Uber can pass along half the cost through price increases, the net impact would be roughly a **10-15%** decline in margin
- Applied to FY2025's $8.7 billion of adjusted EBITDA, the impact would be roughly **$900 million to $1.3 billion**

This is a real but manageable risk — not fatal, but one that would meaningfully reduce Uber's profitability.

## Risk Two: Competition — DoorDash and Others

### The Delivery Market

The landscape of the U.S. delivery market is clear: DoorDash at **67%** versus Uber Eats at **23%**.

DoorDash keeps expanding — from delivery into groceries, alcohol, and convenience-store delivery. FY2025 revenue was **$13.7 billion**, up **28%** year over year, growing faster than Uber's delivery business.

**The question is**: can Uber Eats hold onto its 23% share in the U.S.?

**The optimistic view**: Uber One's cross-platform effect provides a defense that DoorDash cannot replicate. As long as Uber's mobility business keeps attracting users, the delivery business won't be entirely eaten away.

**The pessimistic view**: Switching costs in the delivery market are extremely low. If DoorDash continues to widen its lead, Uber Eats could be squeezed down to a 15-20% share, and losing scale effects would further deteriorate margins.

### The Mobility Market

In the short run, Lyft is not a strategic threat (its market cap is only 1/20th of Uber's). But over the medium-to-long term, the diversion of the mobility market by autonomous-driving players bears watching — this was discussed in detail in Part 03.

## Risk Three: "Noise" in GAAP Profit

Uber's FY2025 GAAP net income was **$10.1 billion** (including a large gain from revaluing equity investments), but excluding investment-gain volatility, Non-GAAP net income was roughly **$5.4 billion**.

In the prior year, FY2024, GAAP net income was **$9.8 billion**, most of which was also equity-investment revaluation. And in Q4 FY2025 alone, there was a **$1.6 billion** investment impairment.

**This doesn't affect Uber's actual operating capability, but it can be misread by the market.** When GAAP net income swings wildly due to investment impairments, it can trigger panic selling. The core metrics investors need to watch are **free cash flow and adjusted EBITDA**, not GAAP net income.

---

## Risk Four: The Macro Economy

Both mobility and delivery are discretionary consumer spending — demand contracts during recessions. But during the Fed's 2022-2023 rate-hiking cycle, Uber's bookings still grew at a double-digit rate, showing demand resilience better than expected.

## Valuation Analysis: Is a 13x P/E Actually Cheap?

### Current Valuation Snapshot

As of May 2026 data (market cap of roughly **$152 billion**):

| Valuation metric | Value | Comparison to peers |
|---------|------|-----------|
| P/E (Non-GAAP) | **~13x** | Median of tech platform companies is roughly 25x |
| P/FCF | **~15.6x** | Lower than Amazon (~25x), Meta (~22x) |
| EV/EBITDA | **~17x** | In the low end of its historical range |
| PEG | **~0.72** | Below 1, a classic "value zone" |
| FCF yield | **~6.4%** | High for a tech company |

(Sources: Uber Q1 2026 earnings, Yahoo Finance, Stock Analysis)

### What Discount Is the Market Applying to Uber?

A platform company growing 18% with $9.8 billion of FCF would "normally" trade at 20-25x P/E. Uber trades at only 13x, meaning the market is applying roughly a **35-45% discount**.

That discount reflects:
1. **Autonomous-driving risk** (the biggest factor)
2. **Regulatory risk** (driver classification)
3. **GAAP profit volatility** (investment-impairment noise)

### Discounted Free Cash Flow Estimate

| Assumption | Conservative case | Base case | Optimistic case |
|------|---------|---------|---------|
| FCF annual growth, next 5 years | 10% | 15% | 20% |
| Growth thereafter | 5% | 8% | 10% |
| Terminal growth rate | 2.5% | 3% | 3.5% |
| Discount rate | 11% | 10% | 9% |
| **Valuation range** | **~$140 billion** | **~$190 billion** | **~$250 billion** |
| **Implied share price** | **~$69** | **~$93** | **~$122** |
| vs. current share price | -8% | +24% | +63% |

**Key takeaways**:

- Under the conservative case (autonomous-driving risk materializes plus tightening regulation), the current share price is already close to fair value
- Under the base case, there's roughly **24%** upside
- Under the optimistic case (autonomous-driving threat stays contained plus the advertising business takes off), there's **63%** upside

**In one sentence: buying Uber at 13x P/E is a bet that autonomous driving won't substantively disrupt the mobility industry within 5 years. If that bet is right, the current price is likely undervalued. If it's wrong, the low valuation won't protect you much.**

## Capital Allocation: What Management Is Doing

### The $20 Billion Buyback

In August 2025, Uber announced a **$20 billion** buyback program (on top of an existing $7 billion program). At the current market cap, that's equivalent to buying back **13%** of shares outstanding.

This is a positive signal:
- It suggests management believes the stock is undervalued
- Management's incentives are aligned with shareholders (CEO Dara holds a large equity stake)
- It stands in sharp contrast to Pinduoduo's "not returning a single cent of its $420 billion in cash"

### $10 Billion+ in Autonomous-Driving Investment

Running alongside the buyback is **more than $10 billion** of investment in an autonomous-vehicle fleet.

**There's a tension here**: on one hand saying "my stock is cheap, so I'll buy it back," on the other hand spending $10 billion on a highly uncertain field.

**Supporters say**: autonomous driving is a strategic investment tied to Uber's very survival — not investing means slow death. Between the $20 billion buyback and the $10 billion AV investment, Uber's $9.8 billion in annual FCF can easily afford both.

**Critics say**: investing $10 billion in autonomous driving is effectively an admission that Uber's core moat (the human driver network) could fail. If that $10 billion were used for buybacks instead, it would deliver better near-term shareholder returns.

**Objective assessment**: this "defend on one side, return capital on the other" strategy is reasonable, but execution risk is not low. The key question is whether the $10 billion investment can produce visible returns within 3-5 years.

## Overall Assessment

### Uber's Strengths and Weaknesses at a Glance

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|------|------|
| Dominant 76% share of the mobility market | Long-term disruption risk from autonomous driving |
| $9.8 billion in annual free cash flow | Regulatory risk (driver classification) |
| Platform stickiness from Uber One's 50 million members | Delivery business far behind DoorDash |
| Fast-growing advertising business | Uncertain return on $10 billion+ AV investment |
| $20 billion buyback program | GAAP profit distorted by investment impairments |
| Strong management execution | Non-founder CEO, less mission-driven than a founder |

### Judgment Across Different Time Horizons

| Time horizon | Core judgment | Confidence |
|---------|---------|--------|
| 1-3 years | Earnings will likely keep improving, valuation has room to re-rate | Medium-high |
| 3-5 years | Depends on autonomous-driving progress and the regulatory direction | Medium |
| 5-10 years | Highly uncertain, the industry could look completely different | Low |
| 10+ years | No meaningful judgment can be made | Very low |

### What Kind of Investor Is This Suited For

- **Suited for**: investors with a 3-5 year time horizon who can accept the uncertainty brought by autonomous driving and who value free cash flow over GAAP profit
- **Not suited for**: investors seeking the certainty of "buy and hold forever" — if you need conviction that a company will still be around in 10 years, Uber cannot give you that answer

### Final Comparison with Tencent

| Dimension | Tencent | Uber |
|------|------|------|
| Certainty of the business | Extremely high (WeChat is irreplaceable) | Medium (autonomous driving is a question mark) |
| Valuation | 15x+ P/E, reasonable | 13x P/E, low but for a reason |
| Management | Founder + highly transparent + generous buybacks | Professional manager + highly transparent + generous buybacks |
| Suited investors | Long-term holders who want to "sleep well at night" | Medium-term investors who can accept uncertainty |

**Tencent is like a river whose depth you know — you can swim across it with confidence. Uber is like a river whose direction you roughly know but whose bottom you can't see clearly — you can probably make it across, but you should be prepared for possible undercurrents.**

## Closing Notes

Across four articles, we've taken Uber apart from top to bottom.

Here are a few final numbers to anchor your investment decision:

| Metric | Value | The question you need to answer |
|------|------|----------------|
| Current P/E | **~13x** | Is the autonomous-driving discount fair? |
| Current P/FCF | **~15.6x** | Can $9.8 billion of FCF keep growing? |
| PEG | **~0.72** | How long can 18% growth be sustained? |
| $20 billion buyback | **~13% of market cap** | Did management judge this correctly? |
| $10 billion+ AV investment | — | Is this a smart move or a panic move? |
| Uber One's 50 million members | **Contribute 50%+ of bookings** | Can this moat keep widening? |

**There are no standard answers to these questions. Every investor will reach a different conclusion based on their own judgment, their own time horizon, and their own risk tolerance.**

That is precisely the purpose of this series — not to tell you to "buy" or "not buy," but to lay the facts, the data, the logic, and the arguments on both sides in front of you, so you can make your own judgment.
