## Why This Part Matters Most

The first two parts settled Uber's numbers: **$9.8 billion** in annual cash generation, 76% share of the mobility market, a moat that is real and measurable. Given these fundamentals, a 13x P/E looks undervalued under any normal valuation framework — a platform growing 18% with a 6.4% FCF yield would normally trade at 20-25x.

**But the market isn't stupid.** Wall Street is applying a 35-45% discount to Uber, not because it hasn't seen these numbers, but because it's pricing in one question: **if autonomous driving matures, is there still a need for Uber's role as "middleman"?** This isn't a distant philosophical question — Waymo is already operating commercially in multiple cities including San Francisco and Phoenix, with its own ride-hailing app, and doesn't need Uber for a single dollar.

This part's job is to unpack that question clearly, rather than hand you a conclusion that pretends to be certain.

## Two Diametrically Opposed Futures

Regarding the impact of autonomous driving on Uber, the market holds two completely opposing narratives.

### The Optimistic Narrative: "Uber Becomes the Android of Autonomous Driving"

The logic runs as follows:

1. Autonomous vehicles get built, but they need help finding passengers
2. Uber owns the world's largest mobility demand network (**28 million+** rides per day)
3. Most autonomous-driving companies (other than Waymo and Tesla) lack consumer brands and customer-acquisition capability
4. Therefore, autonomous-driving companies need Uber as a distribution channel — just as app developers need Android/iOS

Facts supporting this narrative:
- Uber has signed partnership agreements with **more than 20** autonomous-driving companies
- Plans to offer autonomous-driving rides in **15 cities** by the end of 2026
- Autonomous-driving trips grew **10x** year over year in Q1 2026 (though the absolute numbers are still small)
- Uber's goal is to become "the world's largest facilitator of autonomous-driving rides" by 2029

If this narrative comes true, autonomous driving would actually be a **major positive** for Uber: no longer needing to pay drivers 72% of the fare, the platform's take rate could rise sharply from 28%, and margins could double or more.

### The Pessimistic Narrative: "Uber Gets Bypassed"

The logic runs as follows:

1. The strongest autonomous-driving companies (Waymo, Tesla) both have their own ride-hailing apps
2. They don't need Uber — they have the technology, the vehicles, the brand, and the capital
3. Once autonomous driving becomes widespread, consumers will hail rides directly through the Waymo app or the Tesla app
4. Uber's "middleman" role gets eliminated entirely — the same way physical bookstores were eliminated by Amazon

Facts supporting this narrative:
- Waymo has already developed and operates its own ride-hailing app with a good user experience
- Tesla has stated explicitly it will not run Robotaxi through Uber
- Uber has recently publicly criticized its former partner Waymo, while investing **more than $10 billion** in partnerships with Rivian, Lucid, and Nuro to build its own autonomous-vehicle fleet — this "falling out plus massive investment" itself suggests management is also worried about being bypassed
- CEO Dara said on the earnings call that mass adoption of AVs could take **10-20 years** — but Waymo's pace appears to be faster than that

## A Sober Analysis: Probability and Timeline

Both narratives are backed by facts, but both make the same mistake: **oversimplifying a complex, gradual process into a black-and-white conclusion.**

A judgment closer to reality is layered:

### Short term (2026-2028): Limited impact

- Autonomous driving currently operates only in a handful of cities, specific areas, and specific weather conditions
- Waymo's 450,000 weekly rides sounds like a lot, but Uber does **28 million rides a day** — Waymo's volume is less than **2.5%** of a single day's worth of Uber rides
- In the short term, the impact of autonomous driving on Uber's business is nearly negligible
- Uber's $9.8 billion of FCF is entirely unaffected at this stage

### Medium term (2028-2032): Coexistence of competition and cooperation

- Autonomous driving could be operating at scale in 10-20 major cities
- Uber may face both situations simultaneously:
  - Partnering with some AV companies (providing demand in exchange for vehicle supply)
  - Competing directly with Waymo/Tesla
- Uber's $10 billion+ investment in its own fleet starts producing returns (or losses)
- The key variable: **how competitive Uber's own AV fleet turns out to be**

### Long term (2032+): The landscape gets reshaped

- If autonomous driving truly matures (costs below human drivers, covering most scenarios), the mobility industry will be completely restructured
- The competitive landscape at that point will depend on: **who owns the largest demand network + who owns the lowest-cost fleet**
- Uber has an advantage in demand network, but not necessarily in fleet cost
- This is a question that currently cannot be predicted with precision

## Uber's Response Strategy

Uber's current autonomous-driving strategy can be summarized as **"hedge across multiple bets + build its own insurance."**

### 1. Partnering with 20+ AV Companies

Partners include Waymo (a relationship that is deteriorating), Baidu Apollo (Dubai), Wayve (London), Avride (Dallas), and others. The logic: don't put all your eggs in one basket.

### 2. Investing $10 Billion+ in Its Own Fleet

Partnering with Rivian, Lucid, and Nuro to buy/customize autonomous vehicles. This is a major strategic reversal — Uber sold off its own autonomous-driving R&D unit, ATG, in 2020, and is now investing $10 billion again to build its own fleet.

**Is this actually a smart move or a panic move?**

**Supporters say**: not building your own fleet means handing your fate to someone else. Waymo is already developing its own ride-hailing app, and if Uber doesn't control the supply side, it will be completely passive in the future.

**Critics say**: investing $10 billion in a field where both the technology path and the business model remain uncertain is itself a gamble. Uber's core advantage is its asset-light platform model, and building its own fleet goes "against its founding principle." Moreover, Uber isn't good at hardware and autonomous-driving technology — it tried before (ATG) and sold it off after failing.

### 3. Investing in Charging Infrastructure

Investing **$100 million** to build fast-charging stations dedicated to serving autonomous vehicles. This is a relatively small but smart bet.

### 4. The CEO's Public Statements

Dara has recently publicly criticized Waymo's pricing strategy while declaring that Uber will remain "indispensable" in the autonomous-driving era. This shift in tone from "partner" to "competitor" suggests Uber's management's awareness of the autonomous-driving threat is escalating rapidly.

## The Key Variable: Who Owns "Demand," Who Owns "Supply"

The most essential framework for understanding autonomous driving's impact on Uber is **"demand vs. supply":**

| | Currently (the human-driver era) | Future (the autonomous-driving era) |
|--|------------------|------------------|
| **Demand side** | Controlled by Uber (users open the Uber app to hail a ride) | Uber still has an advantage, but Waymo/Tesla apps are competing for it |
| **Supply side** | Fragmented (millions of independent drivers) | Concentrated (a small number of AV companies own the fleets) |
| **Uber's role** | Matchmaking platform (takes a 28% cut) | ??? |

In the human-driver era, the supply side is highly fragmented — no single driver has bargaining power, so Uber can reliably take its 28% cut.

In the autonomous-driving era, the supply side is highly concentrated — Waymo and Tesla each own tens of thousands or even hundreds of thousands of vehicles, and they have both the ability and the incentive to bypass Uber.

**This is the core structural risk Uber faces: the supply side shifts from "fragmented" to "concentrated," weakening the platform's bargaining power.**

Uber's $10 billion investment in its own fleet is, at its core, a hedge against this exact risk — if it can't keep playing the role of "matchmaker," then it should own the supply itself.

## An Analogy to Build Intuition

Picture the history of the taxi industry:

- **The 1990s**: taxi companies owned the vehicles and the drivers, controlling supply
- **The 2010s**: Uber emerged, using an algorithm to connect supply (drivers) and demand (riders), and it crushed the taxi companies
- **The 2030s**: autonomous-driving companies own the vehicles and don't need human drivers — so what is Uber's role?

Two possibilities:
1. Uber becomes "the new era's taxi dispatch center" — autonomous vehicles get orders through Uber
2. Autonomous-driving companies do their own dispatching — Uber becomes "the previous era's taxi company"

**Nobody can say for certain which of these two outcomes will happen right now.** Anyone who claims to be certain is lying.

## A Sober Judgment for Investors

Here is as objective a judgment as possible on how autonomous driving affects Uber's investment value:

### What Is Certain

1. **In the short term (within 3 years), autonomous driving will not materially affect Uber's results** — the scale is too small
2. **Uber's management is taking this threat seriously** — $10 billion+ in investment, 20+ partners, public statements
3. **The market is already pricing in the autonomous-driving risk** — this is why Uber trades at only 13x P/E

### What Is Uncertain

1. **The timeline for mass adoption of autonomous driving** — the CEO says 10-20 years, but Waymo's pace suggests it could be faster
2. **Uber's role in the autonomous-driving era** — a platform? a fleet operator? both? phased out?
3. **The return on the $10 billion+ investment in its own fleet** — it could be a stroke of genius, or it could be an expensive mistake

### A Thinking Framework

| Your investment time horizon | Autonomous-driving impact | Suggested approach |
|---------------|------------|-------------|
| 1-3 years | Almost no impact | Focus on earnings growth and valuation recovery |
| 3-5 years | Competition begins to emerge, but manageable | Track the execution of Uber's AV strategy |
| 5-10 years | Could be the decisive factor | Requires continuous tracking, be ready to revise your judgment at any time |
| 10+ years | Highly uncertain | If you need "certainty," Uber isn't for you |
