## A set of facts that will make you rethink Uber

On the day Uber went public in 2019, nobody was clapping. The stock fell 7.6% on its first day, setting the record at the time for the worst first-day IPO loss in the U.S. market. Wall Street media piled on collectively — losing **$3-4 billion a year**, buying growth with subsidies, never going to be profitable. That year, "Uber" was practically synonymous with "tech company burning cash recklessly."

Seven years later, this "cash-burning machine" generates **$9.8 billion** in annual free cash flow, holds a 76% share of the U.S. ride-hailing market across 70+ countries worldwide, and has launched a buyback program worth as much as $20 billion. The market narrative has flipped four times, while Uber's underlying business has stayed simpler than any of those narratives.

Seven years of stock price history:

| Time | Stock price | What happened |
|---------|------|-----------|
| IPO, May 2019 | **$45** | Fell 7.6% on day one, "the most unloved big IPO" |
| March 2020 | **~$14** | Pandemic crushes mobility demand, all-time low |
| February 2021 | **~$64** | Post-pandemic rebound + delivery boom |
| October 2024 | **~$82** | First sustained profitability, market re-rates the stock |
| October 2025 | **$102** (52-week high) | FCF nears $10 billion, buyback launched |
| May 2026 | **~$75** | Autonomous-driving panic + pullback |

**Over seven years, the market's narrative on Uber has flipped at least four times**: cash-burning machine → pandemic victim → delivery savior → profit machine → about to be disrupted by autonomous driving?

## What kind of business is Uber, really?

Open Uber's FY2025 annual report and you'll find **$52 billion** in revenue coming from three lines:

| Segment | FY2025 revenue | Share | YoY growth | Core logic |
|---------|------------|------|---------|---------|
| Mobility | **$29.7 billion** | 57% | +18% | Global ride-hailing, 76% U.S. share |
| Delivery | **$17.3 billion** | 33% | +25% | Uber Eats, roughly 23% U.S. share |
| Freight | **$5.1 billion** | 10% | -1% | Freight brokerage, just reached breakeven |

(Source: Uber FY2025 annual report / SEC 10-K)

**The first key insight: Uber is not a taxi company, nor a food-delivery company — it is a two-sided network platform that connects supply and demand.**

Uber doesn't own a single vehicle, doesn't employ a single driver, doesn't cook a single meal. It does exactly one thing: **use algorithms to match "people who want to go somewhere" with "drivers who want to earn money," taking a roughly 28% commission from every transaction.**

What does that mean in practice?

- **Asset-light**: no vehicle depreciation, no inventory, extremely low fixed costs
- **High operating leverage**: revenue grew 18%, but free cash flow grew 42% — profit growth far outpaces revenue growth
- **Platform economics**: the bigger the scale, the lower the unit cost, and the stronger the network effect

Anyone who understands Uber as a "taxi company" will never figure out why it makes money. Understand it instead as an "algorithmic matching platform," and everything becomes clear.

## From losses to profit: what happened?

Uber accumulated more than **$30 billion** in losses between 2019 and 2022 (including pre-IPO losses). Then in 2023, it suddenly turned profitable.

This wasn't magic — three things happened at once.

### 1. A new CEO

In 2017, founder Travis Kalanick was pushed out after a string of scandals. His successor was former Expedia CEO **Dara Khosrowshahi**.

Dara did three critical things:
- **Cut loss-making businesses**: exited China (sold to Didi), exited Southeast Asia (sold to Grab), spun off the autonomous-driving R&D unit ATG (sold to Aurora)
- **Stopped "growth at all costs"**: no longer burning cash on subsidies to grab market share, instead pursuing unit economics
- **Reshaped the corporate culture**: shifted from a "winner-take-all" aggressive culture to mature corporate management

### 2. Operating leverage kicked in

Once Uber stopped burning cash to expand, the scale effects of the platform economy began to show:

| Metric | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 | 3-year change |
|------|--------|--------|--------|---------|
| Total revenue | **$37.2B** | **$44.0B** | **$52.0B** | +40% |
| Adjusted EBITDA | **$4.7B** | **$6.4B** | **$8.7B** | +85% |
| Free cash flow | **$3.9B** | **$6.9B** | **$9.8B** | **+151%** |

(Source: Uber FY2023-2025 annual reports)

Revenue grew 40%, while free cash flow grew **151%**. That is the operating leverage of platform economics at work — once scale is large enough and cash burn has stopped, most of every additional dollar of revenue flows straight through to profit.

### 3. The advertising business takes off

Uber's advertising business has already surpassed **$2 billion** in annualized revenue, growing more than **50%** year over year. This is a business with almost zero marginal cost — merchants advertise on Uber Eats, Uber takes a cut, and margins approach 100%.

Following the precedents set by Meituan and Amazon, advertising could become one of the biggest drivers of Uber's future profit growth.

Put these numbers together and you see:

- Management actively buying back stock ($20 billion program), incentives aligned with shareholders
- Yet the stock has pulled back 27% from its high — what is the market worried about?

**The answer, in two words: autonomous driving.**

## What is the market worried about?

Since the second half of 2025, Uber's stock has fallen from a high of $102 to around $75, a pullback of roughly 27%.

The core reason is not the business results — Q1 2026 results beat expectations — but the market's fear of **autonomous driving**:

- **Waymo** (an Alphabet subsidiary) already completes more than **450,000** paid autonomous rides per week
- **Tesla** is pushing forward its Robotaxi plans
- If autonomous driving matures, Waymo and Tesla could bypass Uber and go directly to consumers — Uber's "middleman" role could be eliminated

This is the core reason Uber trades at only 13x PE: **the market is pricing in the "profit machine" of today, while applying a discount for "possibly being disrupted" tomorrow.**

Is that discount justified? That is exactly the question Part 03 of this series will dig into.
