# A Survey of Foundation Model Training Methodology, 2025-2026: From DeepSeek V4 to Kimi K2.5

## I. Introduction

2025 and 2026 have been two years of structural change in the competitive landscape of foundation models. If the theme of 2024 was "the rise of MoE and the open-source catch-up," then the theme of 2025-2026 is "accelerating divergence in architectural innovation, upheaval in post-training paradigms, and agent capability becoming the core competitive dimension."

**On the international front**, Anthropic's Claude series has continued to top the charts, with Claude Opus 4.8 sitting at the top of LMArena by a decisive margin; OpenAI's GPT series remains highly competitive; and Google's Gemini series continues to push hard on the multimodal front. The capability ceiling of top-tier closed-source models is still rising rapidly.

**On the domestic front**, the intensity of competition has far exceeded expectations. DeepSeek V4 pioneered a new "distillation replaces RL" paradigm with its dual CSA/HCA attention mechanism and OPD (Online Policy Distillation); Qwen 3 rewrote the conventional wisdom on training efficiency with minimalist RL (a mere 3,995 queries and 170 steps produced a large gain in math reasoning); Kimi K2/K2.5 built a distinctive moat in agent-capability training; GLM-5 pursued differentiated exploration in domestic-chip adaptation and asynchronous agent-RL infrastructure; MiniMax M3's MSA sparse attention achieved a 28.4x reduction in attention computation; Hunyuan TurboS boldly adopted a Mamba-Transformer hybrid architecture; and Step 3.5 Flash and MiMo-V2-Flash each carved out their own niche in the small-model race.

**The latest LMArena rankings** (as of June 2026): Claude Opus 4.8 ranks first, the GPT series follows close behind, Qwen 3.7 ranks fifth, and domestic models as a group have entered the global first tier. This ranking reflects two trends: first, top closed-source models still command a capability premium; second, domestic open-source models are now in genuine competition with the world's leading closed-source models.

**List of models covered in this article**: DeepSeek V4 Pro/Flash, Qwen 3, Kimi K2, Kimi K2.5, GLM-5, MiniMax M3, Hunyuan TurboS, Step 3.5 Flash, and MiMo-V2-Flash — nine model families in total.

**Sources**: the official technical reports and arXiv papers of the models above. The level of detail varies considerably — the technical reports for DeepSeek V4, GLM-5, and Qwen 3 are the most thorough; MiniMax M3 and Step 3.5 Flash come next; the reports for Hunyuan TurboS and MiMo-V2-Flash have a moderate information density; and the Kimi K2/K2.5 reports are the richest in detail on agent training.

### 2.2 Key Observations

Several core signals emerge from the overview table:

1. **MoE has become the overwhelming consensus**: of the nine model families, all but Hunyuan TurboS (which uses a Mamba-Transformer hybrid architecture) adopt MoE. Dense architectures have completely disappeared from the new generation of flagship models.
2. **Parameter inflation runs alongside restraint in active parameters**: total parameters span an enormous range from 196B to 1.6T, but active parameters are generally kept between 11B and 56B, reflecting a consistent pursuit of "large capacity, low inference cost."
3. **Training data volume has reached the 15T-36T scale**: Qwen 3 leads with 36T tokens, with DeepSeek V4 close behind at 33T. The competition over data scale has entered the "tens of trillions of tokens" era.
4. **Every model brings its own distinctive attention-mechanism innovation**: from CSA/HCA to MSA, DSA, and MFA, attention-mechanism innovation is the technical dimension showing the clearest divergence in this round of competition.

## III. Pretraining

### 3.1 Architectural Evolution

#### 3.1.1 MoE Has Become the Absolute Mainstream

Of the nine model families covered in this article, eight adopt an MoE architecture — a qualitative shift from 2024, when only a handful of models (DeepSeek V3, Qwen 2.5 MoE, etc.) led the way with early attempts. MoE's core advantage is that a small active-parameter footprint (11B-56B) can leverage an enormous total-parameter capacity (196B-1.6T), striking an optimal balance between inference cost and model capability.

Noteworthy differences in the details:

| Model | Total Experts | Shared Experts | Active Experts | Special Design |
|------|---------|---------|-----------|---------|
| DeepSeek V4 Pro | 384 routed | 1 shared | Top-6 | — |
| DeepSeek V4 Flash | 256 routed | 1 shared | Top-6 | — |
| Qwen 3 | 128 routed | None | Top-8 | Removed shared experts |
| Kimi K2 | 384 routed | 1 shared | Top-8 | First layer dense, remaining 60 layers MoE |
| GLM-5 | 256 routed | — | — | MLA-256+Muon Split |
| MiniMax M3 | 128 routed | 1 shared | Top-4 | — |
| Step 3.5 Flash | 288 routed | 1 shared | Top-8 | 3 dense layers + 42 MoE layers |
| MiMo-V2-Flash | 256 routed | None | Top-8 | No shared experts |

An interesting point of divergence is **whether to keep shared experts**: Qwen 3 and MiMo-V2-Flash chose to remove shared experts, on the reasoning that increasing the number of routed experts is more effective than keeping a small number of shared ones; whereas DeepSeek V4, Kimi K2, MiniMax M3, and Step 3.5 Flash each kept one shared expert. This design choice has not yet been settled.

**Hunyuan TurboS's outlier approach** deserves special discussion. It uses a 128-layer Mamba-Transformer hybrid architecture: 57 Mamba2 layers + 7 attention layers + 64 FFN/MoE layers, composed of interleaved AMF (Attention-Mamba-FFN) and MF (Mamba-FFN) blocks. Across all 128 layers, only 7 are attention layers — a mere 5.5% share. The core bet behind this aggressive design is that Mamba2's O(n) linear complexity can deliver a fundamental efficiency advantage in long-sequence scenarios, at the cost of needing a very small number of attention layers to compensate for Mamba's shortcomings in precise positional retrieval. Judging from its 7th-place finish on LMSYS Arena with a score of 1,356, this architectural choice is viable, but whether it outperforms a pure Transformer+MoE design still requires more evidence.

#### 3.1.2 Attention-Mechanism Innovation: A Hundred Flowers Blooming

In this round of model competition, attention-mechanism innovation is the most exciting technical dimension. Each lab has approached the problem from a different angle and proposed its own distinctive sparse-attention scheme.

**DeepSeek V4's dual CSA+HCA mechanism**

Building on MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention, pioneered in DeepSeek V2), DeepSeek V4 introduces two complementary attention mechanisms:

- **CSA (Compressed Sparse Attention)**: every m=4 adjacent KV pairs are compressed into one (via a learnable linear layer), and then a lightweight "Lightning Indexer" performs top-k sparse selection on the compressed KVs (k=1024 for V4 Pro). This means that, in a 1M-token context, the number of tokens actually participating in the attention computation is compressed from the millions down to the thousands.
- **HCA (Hierarchical Compressed Attention)**: uses a much higher compression ratio (m'=128) but performs no sparse selection at all — instead applying full dense attention over all the compressed KVs. This preserves the completeness of global information.

CSA and HCA are used in alternation across the Transformer's layers, forming a complementary pattern of "precise local selection plus coarse-grained global coverage." The inference-efficiency gains from this design are dramatic: V4 Pro's KV cache is only 10% of V3's, and its FLOPs are only 27% of V3.2's.

**MiniMax M3's MSA (Mixed Sparse Attention)**

MSA uses a two-branch design:
- **Index branch**: a lightweight selector quickly determines which KV blocks are relevant to the current query
- **Main branch**: performs precise block-sparse attention over the selected KV blocks

Specific parameters: block size 128, with each query selecting 16 blocks. At a 1M-token context length, this reduces attention computation by 28.4x, speeds up prefill by 9x, and speeds up decoding by 15x. The accompanying GPU kernel optimizations include an exponent-free top-k (2.5-5.1x faster than torch.topk) and a KV-outer-loop kernel.

**GLM-5's DSA (Dynamic Sparse Attention)**

What makes DSA unique is its extremely low adaptation cost: migrating from full attention to sparse attention requires only 20B tokens of continued training (compared with the 943.7B tokens DeepSeek V4 spent training from scratch). DSA uses deterministic top-k selection, avoiding the training instability that randomness can introduce.

**Step 3.5 Flash's MFA+SWA hybrid**

Step 3.5 Flash combines sliding-window attention (SWA) with global attention at a 3:1 ratio (3 SWA layers to 1 global layer in every group of 4). The SWA window size is 512 tokens. What's distinctive here is **head-level gated attention** — each attention head can learn its own gating weight to determine the mixing ratio between SWA and global attention.

**MiMo-V2-Flash's SWA+GA hybrid**

MiMo-V2-Flash also combines SWA with global attention, but at an even more extreme ratio: 5:1 (39 SWA layers to 9 GA layers across 48 layers). The SWA window is only 128 tokens — the smallest window of any model here. To prevent the attention collapse that such a tiny window can cause, MiMo introduces a **learnable sink bias**: a learnable bias term is added to specific positions (e.g., the BOS token), letting the model dump "junk attention" onto the sink token and avoid degeneration of attention weights.

**Hunyuan TurboS's extreme design**

Of its 128 layers, only 7 are attention (GQA, 64 query heads / 8 KV heads), with the rest made up entirely of Mamba2 and FFN/MoE. Mamba2 uses a configuration of d_state=128 and chunk_size=128, delivering O(n) linear complexity. This is the design with the lowest proportion of attention layers among all the models covered here.

**Summary of attention-mechanism innovations**

| Model | Scheme Name | Core Idea | Compression/Sparsity Ratio | Adaptation Cost |
|------|---------|---------|-------------|---------|
| DeepSeek V4 | CSA+HCA | Compression + top-k selection / high compression + dense | KV cache only 10% of V3's | Trained from scratch |
| MiniMax M3 | MSA | Index + block-sparse dual branches | 28.4x reduction in attention computation | Trained from scratch |
| GLM-5 | DSA | Deterministic top-k | — | Only 20B tokens of adaptation |
| Step 3.5 Flash | MFA+SWA 3:1 | Head-level gated mixing | — | Trained from scratch |
| MiMo-V2-Flash | SWA+GA 5:1 | Tiny window + sink bias | — | Trained from scratch |
| Hunyuan TurboS | Mamba2+GQA | Linear complexity + minimal attention | O(n) linear | Trained from scratch |

#### 3.1.3 mHC: Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections

The **mHC (manifold-constrained Hyper-Connection)** introduced by DeepSeek V4 is a fundamental redesign of the residual connection. The problem with traditional residual connections (y = x + f(x)) is that, as depth increases, the gradient contributions of individual layers to the final output can become unbalanced.

The core idea of mHC:
1. Replace the residual connection with a **doubly stochastic matrix** (where every row and every column sums to 1), used to control the mixing weights of each layer's inputs and outputs
2. The doubly-stochastic constraint is enforced via the **Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm** (20 iterations) — a learnable matrix is alternately row-normalized and column-normalized until it converges onto the manifold of doubly stochastic matrices
3. The matrix parameters are dynamically parameterized (rather than fixed values) and can adapt over the course of training

The elegance of this design lies in the fact that the doubly stochastic matrix guarantees a kind of "conservation" of information flow — no single layer's contribution gets excessively amplified or suppressed. Empirical results show that mHC adds only 6.7% extra compute time, while significantly improving both training stability and performance in deep networks.

#### 3.1.4 MTP (Multi-Token Prediction)

MTP has been widely adopted in this round of models:

- **GLM-5**: 3 shared MTP layers, with an average accepted length of 2.76 at inference time
- **Step 3.5 Flash**: a 3-head MTP, introducing a **Fast-MTP position-aware loss** — each prediction head uses a different loss weight depending on its predicted position (the 1st, 2nd, or 3rd future token)
- **MiMo-V2-Flash**: MTP speculative decoding reaches an accepted length of 3.6, achieving a 2.6x inference speedup

MTP's value is twofold: during training it provides a richer gradient signal (predicting multiple future tokens at each position), and during inference it significantly boosts throughput via speculative decoding.

#### 3.1.5 AFD: Attention-FFN Decoupled Parallelism

**AFD (Attention-FFN Decoupling)**, proposed by Step 3.5 Flash, is an engineering-oriented architectural innovation. In a traditional Transformer, the attention layer and the FFN layer execute serially (attention first, then FFN). AFD decouples the two so they execute in parallel: attention and FFN are computed simultaneously, with the results merged at the layer output. This reduces per-layer serial latency and allows better use of GPU parallel-compute capacity at inference time.

#### 3.2.2 Synthetic Data at Scale

Synthetic data has moved from "auxiliary supplement" to "core component." The most representative examples:

- **Kimi K2's knowledge-rewriting strategy**: raw web text is rewritten by a large model into a more structured, more training-friendly format — a strategy used at scale as early as the pretraining stage
- **Kimi K2's agent data-synthesis pipeline**: starting from 3,000+ GitHub MCP (Model Context Protocol) tools, it synthesizes 20,000+ tool definitions and then generates multi-turn agent interaction trajectories. This is the largest agent-training data synthesis pipeline disclosed publicly to date
- **Qwen 3's reasoning-enhancement stage**: 5T tokens are carved out of its 36T total dataset specifically for reasoning enhancement, containing large amounts of synthetic math and code reasoning data

#### 3.2.3 Data-Rewriting Strategy

A notable feature of Kimi K2's data engineering is **Knowledge Rewriting**. The core idea: raw internet text is often full of noise — ads, navigation bars, irrelevant links — and using it directly for training is inefficient. Kimi K2 uses an already-trained model to "rewrite" this text — preserving the core knowledge content while reorganizing it into a format better suited for language-model learning. The trade-off between the cost of this strategy (the inference compute required for rewriting) and its benefit (improved training efficiency) is a question worth studying further.

### 3.3 Training Strategy

#### 3.3.1 Long-Context Training

Long-context capability has become table stakes, and models generally adopt a multi-stage context-extension strategy:

| Model | Extension Path | Final Context Length |
|------|---------|-------------|
| DeepSeek V4 | 4K → 1M (multi-stage) | 1M |
| Qwen 3 | Three stages (30T general → 5T reasoning enhancement → long context) | — |
| Kimi K2 | Two-stage pretraining + 400B annealing + 60B long context | — |
| GLM-5 | Five-stage pretraining | — |
| MiniMax M3 | — | 1M |
| Hunyuan TurboS | Two-stage long-context extension | 256K |
| MiMo-V2-Flash | Three stages (22T general → 4T code enhancement → 1T long context) | — |

The common pattern is to first fully train foundational capability on short sequences, then gradually extend through a dedicated long-context stage. The reasoning behind this strategy: training on long sequences is far more computationally expensive than on short ones (due to self-attention's quadratic complexity), so it is more economical to build foundational capability on short sequences early on.

#### 3.3.2 FP8/FP4 Mixed Precision

Low-precision training has been pushed even further in this round of models:

- **DeepSeek V4**: introduces **FP4 quantization-aware training** for the first time, dropping the MoE expert weights and the QK path of the CSA indexer down to FP4 precision. This is the first reported use of FP4 during pretraining in the industry, distinct from quantization at inference time — using low precision during training means the model adapts to quantization error from the very start
- **Other models**: FP8 mixed precision is already common practice and is no longer reported as a standalone technical highlight

#### 3.3.3 Training Stability

Training stability is a core engineering challenge in large-scale pretraining. This round of models shows particularly notable innovation on this front:

**Kimi K2's MuonClip — zero loss spikes**

MuonClip is a stability constraint added on top of the Muon optimizer: a threshold of τ=100 is set on the QK inner product of the attention layers, and values exceeding it are clipped. This simple yet elegant design allowed Kimi K2 to achieve **zero loss spikes** across its entire 15.5T-token pretraining run — a milestone result. By contrast, most large-scale training runs experience anywhere from a few to dozens of loss spikes, each of which means a degradation in training state and wasted recovery time.

**DeepSeek V4's foresight routing + SwiGLU clamping**

DeepSeek V4 employs a dual safeguard for stability:
1. **Foresight Routing**: decouples the update of the MoE router from the update of the model backbone. Under normal training, the router and the backbone update in sync; when a potential loss-spike signal is detected, the router's update is paused (frozen) and only the backbone continues updating. This adds roughly 20% compute overhead, but since it only activates when there is a spike risk, the actual average overhead is far below 20%
2. **SwiGLU clamping**: clamps the output of the SwiGLU activation function to the range [-10, 10], preventing extreme activation values from propagating

**Step 3.5 Flash's Polar Express**

Step 3.5 Flash made specialized precision optimizations to the Muon optimizer (called Polar Express), experiencing only a single loss spike across its 17.2T-token training run.

**Comparison of stability techniques**

| Model | Stability Scheme | Number of Loss Spikes | Extra Overhead |
|------|-----------|--------------|---------|
| Kimi K2 | MuonClip (QK-Clip τ=100) | 0 | Very low |
| Step 3.5 Flash | Polar Express | 1 | — |
| DeepSeek V4 | Foresight routing + SwiGLU clamping | — | ~20% (peak) |

#### 3.3.4 Optimizer: Muon Replaces AdamW

The adoption of the Muon optimizer is a notable trend in this round of models:

| Model | Optimizer | Special Configuration |
|------|-------|---------|
| DeepSeek V4 | Muon | 10 Newton-Schulz iterations (fast for the first 8 steps, precise for the last 2), hybrid bucket allocation |
| Kimi K2 | MuonClip | Muon + QK-Clip stability constraint |
| GLM-5 | Muon Split | MLA-256 head splitting |
| Step 3.5 Flash | Muon+Polar Express | Precision optimization |

Muon's core advantage is that it does not rely on first-/second-order momentum estimates (the core of AdamW); instead it orthogonalizes the gradient matrix via Newton-Schulz iteration, which in theory makes better use of the gradient's geometric structure. DeepSeek V4's implementation is especially refined: the first 8 Newton-Schulz iterations use a fast but lower-precision approach to accelerate convergence, while the last 2 steps switch to a high-precision mode to ensure the final quality of orthogonalization; hybrid bucket allocation assigns parameter matrices to different compute buckets based on their size, optimizing parallel efficiency.

#### 3.4.2 Communication Optimization

The core communication bottleneck in large-scale MoE training is the All-to-All communication between experts. Each model takes a different approach to addressing it:

- **DeepSeek V4**'s hybrid bucket allocation and expert-parallelism optimization are important sources of its training efficiency
- **GLM-5**'s adaptation work across domestic chips (7 hardware platforms) faces a more complex communication challenge — the interconnect topology and communication bandwidth vary enormously across chips, requiring communication strategies to be optimized separately for each platform
- **MiMo-V2-Flash**'s R3 (Rollout Routing Replay) technique indirectly solves a communication-related problem in MoE training: during the RL stage, rollout generation and the training update use different model versions, causing inconsistent routing decisions. R3 eliminates this inconsistency by replaying the rollout-stage routing decisions during training

## IV. Post-Training

### 4.1 SFT Strategy

#### 4.1.1 Comparison of Thinking-Mode Designs

"Thinking mode" — where the model performs internal reasoning before producing its final answer — has become a standard feature of 2025-2026 models. But the design philosophies differ noticeably across labs:

**Qwen 3's /think and /no_think**

Qwen 3 uses the simplest binary design: the user adds a `/think` or `/no_think` tag to the prompt to control whether the model performs internal reasoning. Implementing this relies on a "thinking-mode fusion" stage during post-training — training data with and without thinking are mixed together, so that the same model can switch modes based on the instruction.

An important finding from Qwen 3 is that **the thinking budget is an emergent capability** — after RL training, the model automatically learns to allocate a thinking process of different lengths based on problem difficulty, with no need for an explicit length-control mechanism.

**DeepSeek V4's three reasoning intensities**

DeepSeek V4 offers finer-grained gradient control:
- **Non-think**: no internal reasoning, generates the answer directly (suited to simple questions)
- **Think High**: standard-depth internal reasoning
- **Think Max**: maximum-depth internal reasoning (suited to extremely difficult math/programming problems)

The three intensities are triggered by different system prompts, and the model learns to distinguish between the three modes during the OPD distillation stage.

**GLM-5's three modes**

GLM-5 likewise offers three reasoning modes, but its design philosophy leans more toward "scenario adaptation" than "intensity gradation" — providing different thinking strategies for different task types (e.g., math reasoning, code generation, general conversation).

**Hunyuan TurboS's adaptive CoT**

Hunyuan TurboS takes the most aggressive approach: **adaptive long/short CoT** — the model automatically decides whether to use long-chain or short-chain thinking based on problem difficulty, with no manual switching by the user. Empirical results show this strategy lets Hunyuan TurboS match DeepSeek-R1's reasoning performance while using only 52.8% of its token count — meaning inference cost is nearly halved while performance is maintained.

**Summary comparison of thinking modes**

| Model | Number of Modes | Control Method | What's Distinctive |
|------|-------|---------|---------|
| Qwen 3 | 2 | /think /no_think tags | Thinking budget emerges naturally |
| DeepSeek V4 | 3 | Non-think/Think High/Think Max | Three-level intensity gradient |
| GLM-5 | 3 | Scenario adaptation | — |
| Hunyuan TurboS | Adaptive | No manual switching needed | Only 52.8% of R1's token count |

#### 4.1.2 Multi-Round Deliberative Learning

**Multi-round Deliberative Learning**, proposed by Hunyuan TurboS, is a distinctive SFT strategy carried out in three steps:
1. **Critique stage**: the model critiques its own answer to identify weaknesses
2. **Weakness deliberation**: for the weaknesses identified, the model generates an improved answer
3. **Iterative SFT**: the (original answer, critique, improvement) triples are used as training data for iterative SFT

The essence of this strategy is to have the model learn from its own mistakes — similar to the "error notebook" method used in human learning.

### 4.2 Reinforcement Learning

#### 4.2.1 GRPO Remains the Mainstream Algorithm

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, pioneered by DeepSeek) remains the most widely adopted RL algorithm in this round of models. Hunyuan TurboS explicitly reports using two-stage GRPO: 300,000 data points in the reasoning stage plus 160,000 in the general stage.

But what's more noteworthy are the improvements and alternatives each lab has built on top of GRPO.

#### 4.2.2 DeepSeek V4's OPD — Distillation Fully Replacing RL

**OPD (Online Policy Distillation)** is DeepSeek V4's major innovation in the post-training paradigm, with a core idea of **fully replacing traditional RL with distillation**.

OPD's workflow:
1. **Independent training of domain experts**: expert models are trained separately for different domains (math, code, general, etc.), with each expert model going through the full SFT+GRPO pipeline
2. **Online policy distillation merging**: rather than simple model merging (e.g., parameter averaging), it distills the full-vocabulary logit distribution using **reverse KL divergence** — the student model (the final V4) generates samples under its own policy distribution and then matches the logit distributions of 10+ teacher models

The deeper logic behind this design: traditional RL's reward signal is scalar (a single number measuring how good an answer is), whereas distillation's "reward signal" is a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary — several orders of magnitude richer in information. This explains why OPD can achieve better results with fewer training steps.

Even more elegantly, DeepSeek V4 also introduces a **Generative Reward Model (GRM)**: whereas a traditional reward model is discriminative (input an answer, output a score), a GRM is generative (it produces evaluative text and then extracts a score from that text). Moreover, DeepSeek V4 **applies RL optimization to the GRM itself** — using RL to improve the reward model's judgment quality, forming a nested optimization loop.

#### 4.2.3 Qwen 3's Minimalist RL

Qwen 3's RL results are among the most surprising findings of this round:

- Used only **3,995 queries** (fewer than 4,000 questions)
- Trained for only **170 steps of GRPO**
- Raised the AIME math-competition benchmark from 70.1 to 85.1 (+15 points)

This result challenges the conventional wisdom that "RL requires large amounts of data and long training." The Qwen 3 team's explanation is: if pretraining and SFT have already built a sufficiently strong foundational capability, RL's role is more about "activation" than "teaching" — a small amount of high-quality RL signal is enough to trigger reasoning capabilities the model already possesses but has not fully unlocked.

Qwen 3's post-training follows a four-stage pipeline:
1. **Long-CoT cold start**: SFT on long-chain-of-thought data to establish the habit of thinking
2. **Reasoning RL**: the minimalist GRPO described above (3,995 queries, 170 steps)
3. **Thinking-mode fusion**: mixing /think and /no_think training data
4. **General RL**: RL on a broader set of tasks

#### 4.2.4 GLM-5's IcePop

**IcePop** is GLM-5's solution to a long-overlooked problem in RL: **train-inference distribution mismatch**.

The nature of the problem: during RL training, the model samples from its own distribution (on-policy), but the reward model used for training was trained on a different distribution (an off-policy evaluation). As RL training progresses, the gap between the model's distribution and the reward model's training distribution grows larger, making the reward signal increasingly unreliable.

IcePop's solution:
1. **Explicitly model the distribution mismatch**: quantify the gap between the current policy and the reward model's training distribution
2. **Pop-operator clipping**: when the gap exceeds a threshold, clip the influence of the reward signal
3. **Remove KL regularization**: traditional RL uses KL-divergence regularization to limit the magnitude of policy updates; IcePop argues this instead hampers effective exploration, and removes it outright

#### 4.2.5 Step 3.5 Flash's MIS-PO

**MIS-PO (Multi-level Importance Sampling Policy Optimization)** is a discrete, filter-based RL method proposed by Step 3.5 Flash. Its core innovation is **two-level filtering**:

1. **Trajectory-level filtering**: at the level of complete answers, filters out trajectories whose reward signal indicates unacceptably low quality
2. **Token-level filtering**: at the token level, further filters out anomalous tokens based on importance-sampling weights

The benefit of this two-level filtering: it prevents a handful of anomalous tokens or low-quality trajectories from having an outsized effect on the policy update, making RL training more stable.

#### 4.2.6 Hunyuan TurboS's Two-Stage GRPO

Hunyuan TurboS uses standard two-stage GRPO:
- **Reasoning stage**: 300,000 reasoning-related data points (math, code, logical reasoning)
- **General stage**: 160,000 general-task data points

This design reflects a "reasoning first, general second" post-training paradigm — first building strong thinking ability on reasoning tasks, then using the general stage to ensure general conversational ability is not lost.

### 4.3 Distillation

Distillation's role in this round of models has undergone a qualitative shift — upgrading from an "optional efficiency optimization" to a "core training paradigm."

#### 4.3.1 MiMo-V2-Flash's MOPD (Multi-Teacher Online Distillation)

**MOPD (Multi-teacher Online Policy Distillation)** is the core post-training method of MiMo-V2-Flash. Compared with DeepSeek V4's OPD, MOPD has several distinctive features:

1. **Multi-teacher architecture**: different domains use different expert teacher models, with each teacher providing the highest-quality guidance in its own domain
2. **Token-level reward signal**: traditional RL's reward is trajectory-level (one score per complete answer); MOPD instead uses the teacher model's token-level logits as the reward signal, giving 1-2 orders of magnitude finer information granularity
3. **Reverse KL + clipped importance sampling + ORM outcome reward**: a blend of three signals — reverse-KL distillation provides a distribution-matching signal, clipped importance sampling prevents excessive distribution shift, and the ORM (Outcome Reward Model) outcome reward provides supervision on the correctness of the final answer

A key engineering challenge for MOPD is: in RL training of MoE models, rollout generation and parameter updates use different versions of the model, causing inconsistent MoE routing decisions. MiMo-V2-Flash solves this via the **R3 (Rollout Routing Replay)** technique: replaying the rollout-stage routing decisions during the parameter-update stage to ensure consistency of the training signal.

#### 4.3.2 Qwen 3's Strong-to-Weak Distillation

For training its small models, Qwen 3 employs an extremely efficient strategy: using the logit distribution of the large model (235B) as the teacher signal to distill the small model. The key figure: it takes only **1/10 the GPU training time of the large model** to obtain a small model near the large model's level.

The practical significance of this result is profound: it shows that, given a strong teacher model, the marginal cost of training smaller models in the same family is extremely low. This explains why Qwen 3 could simultaneously release multiple model sizes across both its Dense and MoE product lines.

#### 4.3.3 DeepSeek V4's OPD

As described in Section 4.2.2, OPD's core is reverse-KL, full-vocabulary logit distillation using 10+ teacher models. In the context of distillation, what makes OPD unique is that it is "online" — the student model generates samples under its own distribution (rather than using samples generated by the teacher), which avoids the distribution-shift problem present in traditional offline distillation.

#### 4.3.4 GLM-5's Cross-Stage Distillation

GLM-5 introduces a **cross-stage distillation anti-forgetting** mechanism into its multi-stage post-training. The background of the problem: post-training is typically divided into multiple stages (SFT → RL → alignment, etc.), and training in a later stage can cause the capabilities established in an earlier stage to degrade (catastrophic forgetting). GLM-5's solution is to keep the previous stage's model as a "teacher" at every stage, using a distillation signal to constrain the current stage's training so it does not stray too far from the previous stage's knowledge.

#### 4.3.5 Comparison of Distillation Techniques

| Model | Distillation Method | Teacher Source | Distillation Granularity | Core Value |
|------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| DeepSeek V4 | OPD | 10+ domain experts | Full-vocabulary logit | Replaces RL |
| MiMo-V2-Flash | MOPD | Multi-domain teachers | Token-level logit + ORM | Multi-signal fusion |
| Qwen 3 | Strong-to-weak distillation | 235B large model | Logit | 1/10 the GPU time to train the small model |
| GLM-5 | Cross-stage distillation | Previous-stage model | — | Prevents catastrophic forgetting |

### 4.4 Agent-Capability Training

Agent capability (i.e., a model's ability to use tools, interact with environments, and complete complex multi-step tasks) has risen in this round of models from an "add-on feature" to a "core competitive dimension." Kimi K2/K2.5 has invested most deeply in this direction.

#### 4.4.1 Kimi K2's Large-Scale Agent Data-Synthesis Pipeline

Kimi K2 has built the most complete agent-training data-synthesis pipeline disclosed publicly to date:

1. **Tool collection**: extracting real tool definitions from 3,000+ GitHub MCP tool libraries
2. **Tool expansion**: synthesizing 20,000+ tool definitions based on real tools — expanding coverage by varying parameters, combining functionality, and simulating new tools
3. **Trajectory generation**: using an existing strong model to generate multi-turn interaction trajectories over the synthetic tools — covering complex scenarios such as tool invocation, result parsing, error handling, and multi-step reasoning
4. **Quality filtering**: applying multi-dimensional quality filtering to the generated trajectories to retain only high-quality training data

The key insight behind this pipeline: the bottleneck in training agent capability is not the algorithm (RL algorithms like GRPO are already mature enough), but the data — high-quality agent-interaction data is extremely scarce and must be manufactured at scale through synthesis.

#### 4.4.2 Kimi K2.5's Agent Swarm and PARL Framework

Building on K2, Kimi K2.5 pushes agent capability further, introducing two key innovations:

**Agent Swarm architecture**

Agent Swarm is a multi-agent collaboration architecture:
- **Orchestrator**: responsible for task decomposition and sub-agent scheduling
- **Sub-agents**: each focused on tool use in a specific domain (e.g., code execution, file operations, search)
- **Decoupled design**: the orchestrator is trainable while the sub-agents are frozen

The key advantage of this architecture: training the orchestrator does not require simultaneously training all the sub-agents, substantially lowering training complexity. At the same time, new sub-agents can be plugged in without retraining the orchestrator.

**PARL (Parallel Agent Reinforcement Learning) framework**

PARL addresses the efficiency problem in agent RL training: traditional agent RL requires serially executing tool calls and environment interactions, resulting in extremely low GPU utilization (a large amount of time is spent waiting for the environment to return results). PARL parallelizes the rollout process across multiple agents, overlapping waiting time with compute time, significantly boosting training throughput.

Measured effect: latency reduced by 3-4.5x.

**Toggle heuristic**

Kimi K2.5 also proposes the Toggle heuristic strategy: at inference time, dynamically deciding whether to enable thinking mode based on task characteristics. This strategy achieves a 25-30% reduction in tokens with almost no loss in performance.

**Visual agent capability**

Kimi K2.5's visual capability is based on the **MoonViT-3D visual encoder**: it processes 4-frame spatiotemporal volumes (treating 4 consecutive image/video frames as a single 3D input), achieving 4x compression along the temporal dimension. A surprising finding is **zero-shot visual SFT**: doing SFT with only text data is enough to activate visual reasoning ability, with no need for visually annotated data — indicating the cross-modal representations established during pretraining are already strong enough.

Further cross-modal RL-enhancement experiments show that visual RL not only improves performance on visual tasks but also boosts pure-text tasks in return — MMLU-Pro +1.7, GPQA +2.1. This hints at a potential positive transfer effect from cross-modal training signals.

#### 4.4.3 GLM-5's Asynchronous Agent-RL Infrastructure (Slime)

GLM-5 has built dedicated infrastructure for agent-RL training called Slime:
- **1,000+ concurrent rollouts**: running more than 1,000 agent-environment interactions simultaneously
- **TITO gateway**: a Token-In-Token-Out gateway that manages communication between agents and environments
- **DP-aware routing**: optimizing request routing based on the data-parallelism (DP) topology

The core problem Slime solves is agent-RL training efficiency: because agents need to interact with real or simulated environments, every step incurs I/O latency, and traditional synchronous RL yields extremely low GPU utilization in agent scenarios. Slime raises GPU utilization to an acceptable level through asynchronous design and large-scale concurrency.

#### 4.4.4 MiniMax M2's Forge RL System

Although MiniMax M3's technical report focuses mainly on architectural innovation, the **Forge RL system** developed for its predecessor, M2, offers an engineering reference for agent-RL training. Forge is a general-purpose RL training platform that supports flexible combinations of multiple reward signals and training algorithms.

## V. Inference Efficiency

Inference efficiency directly determines a model's deployment cost and user experience. This round of models shows innovation on this dimension along several fronts:

### 5.1 DeepSeek V4's KV-Cache Compression

The inference-efficiency gains from DeepSeek V4's dual CSA+HCA attention mechanism are comprehensive:
- **KV cache**: only 10% of V3's. In a 1M-token context scenario, this means memory requirements shrink from hundreds of GB down to tens of GB
- **FLOPs**: V4 Pro's is only 27% of V3.2's
- This lets V4 cut inference cost dramatically while matching or even exceeding V3's performance

### 5.2 MSA's Reduction in Attention Computation

MiniMax M3's MSA achieves the following at a 1M-token context length:
- A **28.4x** reduction in attention computation
- A **9x** speedup in prefill
- A **15x** speedup in decoding

The accompanying GPU kernel implementations are also worth noting:
- **Exponent-free top-k**: the conventional torch.topk requires computing exponentials and sorting over all elements; the exponent-free top-k algorithm designed by MiniMax bypasses the exponential computation and runs 2.5-5.1x faster than torch.topk
- **KV-outer-loop kernel**: treats KV as the outer loop (rather than the conventional query-outer-loop), making better use of GPU memory-access patterns

### 5.3 MTP Speculative Decoding

MTP's value at inference time is mainly expressed through speculative-decoding acceleration:

| Model | Number of MTP Heads | Average Accepted Length | Speedup |
|------|--------|------------|-------|
| MiMo-V2-Flash | Multiple heads | 3.6 | 2.6x |
| GLM-5 | 3 shared heads | 2.76 | — |
| Step 3.5 Flash | 3 heads | — | — |

MiMo-V2-Flash's average accepted length of 3.6 means that of every roughly 3.6 tokens generated speculatively, an average of 3.6 are accepted (matching what autoregressive generation would produce), thereby delivering a 2.6x boost in inference throughput. This figure ranks among the best currently disclosed in public reports.

### 5.4 Hunyuan TurboS's Mamba Linear Complexity

Hunyuan TurboS's Mamba2 layers deliver O(n) linear complexity, which in theory offers a fundamental advantage in long-sequence scenarios: as sequence length grows from 4K to 256K, a Transformer's attention computation grows by roughly 4,096x (quadratic), whereas Mamba's grows by only 64x (linear). Note, however, that Hunyuan TurboS still retains 7 attention layers (GQA), so its overall complexity is not strictly linear.

### 5.5 Summary of Inference Efficiency

| Model | Core Optimization | Key Metric |
|------|-----------|---------|

| MiniMax M3 | MSA sparse attention | 28.4x reduction in attention computation |

## VII. Trends and Implications

### 7.1 Six Established Trends

#### Trend One: MoE Becomes the Only Architectural Choice

Eight of the nine models adopt MoE, and the sole exception (Hunyuan TurboS's Mamba-Transformer hybrid) itself contains MoE expert layers. Dense architectures have completely exited the stage among flagship models.

The logic behind MoE's victory is clear: under inference-cost constraints (API pricing pressure, edge-deployment demands), MoE delivers capability comparable to a large Dense model (with hundreds of billions of active parameters) using far fewer active parameters (11B-56B). When DeepSeek V4 achieves the performance of a 1.6T-total-parameter model with only 49B active parameters, it becomes very hard for any team to justify training a 1.6T Dense model.

An important corollary of this trend: **MoE engineering capability (routing stability, expert load balancing, All-to-All communication optimization) is becoming a core competitive strength for foundation-model teams.**

#### Trend Two: Sparse Attention Blooms in a Hundred Forms

CSA/HCA, MSA, DSA, MFA, SWA+GA — every major model has proposed its own distinctive sparse-attention scheme. This phenomenon shows that there is now consensus about the efficiency bottleneck of standard full attention in long-context scenarios, but the optimal alternative has not yet converged.

The design philosophies behind these schemes differ fundamentally:
- **The compression school** (CSA/HCA): compress the KV first, then either select or use all of it
- **The selection school** (MSA, DSA): no compression, directly select the most relevant KV
- **The windowing school** (SWA+GA, MFA): use a sliding window to cover local context and a small number of global layers to cover long-range context
- **The linear school** (Mamba2): replace attention entirely with a state-space model

Each of these approaches has its own trade-offs, and they are unlikely to converge on a single paradigm in the near term.

#### Trend Three: The Muon Optimizer Replaces AdamW

DeepSeek V4, Kimi K2, GLM-5, Step 3.5 Flash — four models explicitly adopt Muon or a variant of it. AdamW has dominated the deep-learning optimizer landscape for the past decade; Muon is the first alternative to have proven its superiority in large-scale pretraining.

Muon's core advantage lies in orthogonalizing the gradient via Newton-Schulz iteration, making better use of the gradient's geometric structure, especially standing out in large-batch-size training. The customized improvements each lab has built on top of Muon (MuonClip, Muon Split, Polar Express) show that Muon's basic framework has been accepted, and the competitive focus has shifted to specific implementation optimizations.

#### Trend Four: Distillation Is Replacing Traditional RL

DeepSeek V4's OPD and MiMo-V2-Flash's MOPD represent a trend that could reshape the post-training paradigm: **using distillation to replace (or substantially reduce) traditional RL**.

The underlying logic of this trend:
- RL's reward signal is scalar (a single score), with low information density
- Distillation's "reward signal" is a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary, with information density several orders of magnitude higher
- RL training is unstable (reward hacking, distribution shift, etc.), whereas distillation training is more stable
- RL requires large amounts of rollout compute, whereas distillation is more compute-efficient

But distillation has one fundamental limitation: it requires a teacher model stronger than the student. When training "the strongest model," where does the teacher come from? DeepSeek V4's answer is "domain experts" — expert models pushed to the extreme in a specific domain that, even if weaker overall than the final model, can still provide an effective teaching signal within their own domain.

#### Trend Five: Thinking Mode Becomes a Standard Feature

Every major model supports some form of thinking mode. The difference lies in the control method:
- Manual control (Qwen 3's /think /no_think)
- Multi-level control (DeepSeek V4's three intensities)
- Fully adaptive (Hunyuan TurboS)

The essence of this trend: users have different needs for the trade-off between inference cost (token count) and reasoning quality. Simple questions don't need long-chain thinking (which would waste tokens); complex questions need deep reasoning (otherwise quality suffers). The design of thinking mode directly affects user experience and API cost.

From a technical-implementation perspective, Qwen 3's discovery that "the thinking budget emerges naturally" is an important signal — a sufficiently well-trained model can automatically learn to allocate its thinking resources without an explicit length-control mechanism. This hints that thinking mode may trend toward being fully adaptive in the future.

#### Trend Six: Agent Capability Becomes a Core Competitive Dimension

Kimi K2/K2.5's agent data-synthesis pipeline (3,000+ MCP tools → 20,000+ synthetic tools), GLM-5's Slime asynchronous agent-RL infrastructure (1,000+ concurrent rollouts), and MiniMax M2's Forge RL system all show that multiple teams have poured substantial engineering resources into agent training.

The importance of agent capability is pulled by demand at the application layer: foundation models are evolving from "answering questions" to "completing tasks," which requires using tools (search, code execution, API calls, etc.), interacting with environments (browsers, file systems, etc.), and handling multi-step tasks (decompose → execute → verify → correct). These capabilities cannot be acquired through pure language modeling alone; they require dedicated training methods and data.

Kimi K2's strong performance on agent benchmarks such as SWE-bench (and Kimi K2.5's extension of agent capability into the visual domain) validates this direction of investment. DeepSeek V4's scores of 80.6% on SWE-bench Verified, 3,206 on Codeforces, and 93.5% on LiveCodeBench likewise reflect how intense the competition in agent/coding capability has become.

### 7.2 Open Questions

#### Question One: Will Sparse Attention Converge on a Single Paradigm?

Current sparse-attention schemes embody at least five different design philosophies (compression, selection, windowing, linear, hybrid). Will they eventually converge on a single optimal scheme, the way MoE unified model architecture?

The case **for "probably not"**: different schemes optimize for different objectives — CSA/HCA optimizes KV-cache size, MSA optimizes computational volume, SWA optimizes implementation simplicity, and Mamba optimizes theoretical complexity. There are fundamental trade-offs among these objectives.

The case **for "probably yes"**: the market will ultimately select whichever scheme has the highest overall efficiency, just as the Transformer settled the competition among RNNs, CNNs, and attention. DeepSeek V4's CSA+HCA currently appears to strike the best overall balance between "KV-cache compression and computational efficiency," and could become the de facto standard.

#### Question Two: Can Distillation Fully Replace RL?

DeepSeek V4's OPD has already fully replaced traditional RL with distillation in practice. But this raises a fundamental question: if training the strongest model also depends on distillation, where does the "teacher" come from?

Possible answers:
1. **The domain-expert route** (already taken by DeepSeek V4): train multiple domain experts, each pushed to the extreme in its own domain, then merge via distillation. The teacher does not need to outperform the student along every dimension — it only needs to provide an effective signal on a particular dimension
2. **The self-distillation route**: the model uses its own past versions as teachers, improving continuously through iterative self-improvement
3. **The RL + distillation hybrid route**: RL is used to explore the boundary of capability (finding new knowledge the teacher model lacks), while distillation is used to efficiently propagate existing knowledge

Qwen 3's minimalist-RL result (3,995 queries, 170 steps) hints at another possibility: perhaps a small amount of high-quality RL is already sufficient, with distillation handling the bulk of the remaining work. The future post-training paradigm may be a combination of "a little RL exploration plus a lot of distillation propagation."

#### Question Three: Is the Mamba-Transformer Hybrid the Future?

Hunyuan TurboS, with only 7 of its 128 layers devoted to attention (5.5%), has demonstrated the viability of the Mamba-Transformer hybrid architecture, and its 7th-place finish on LMSYS Arena with a score of 1,356 shows its performance can compete with pure Transformers.

But several facts are worth noting:
1. Hunyuan TurboS is the **only** model among the nine covered in this article to adopt the Mamba-Transformer hybrid — the other eight teams all chose pure Transformer+MoE
2. Mamba still has theoretical limitations on tasks such as precise positional retrieval (e.g., "what is the 327th token"), which is also why Hunyuan TurboS retains 7 attention layers
3. Mamba's linear-complexity advantage is not pronounced in short-sequence scenarios and only shows up in long-sequence scenarios (>64K)

The Mamba-Transformer hybrid is very likely a valuable alternative route, especially for long-context and low-latency scenarios. But whether it can replace pure Transformer+MoE as the mainstream is not something current evidence is sufficient to conclude.
