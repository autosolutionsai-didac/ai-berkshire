# One Formula for "How Much Should You Buy"

The most common mistake in investing isn't picking the wrong stock — it's picking the right stock but sizing the position wrong.

You like a stock. Buy too little, and a rally barely registers. Buy too much, and a drop leaves you reeling. So how much should you actually buy? Over sixty years ago, a physicist gave a precise answer.

---

## The Kelly Formula: Four Variables, One Division

Every investment has only four variables: the probability of winning, the probability of losing, how much you gain if you win, and how much you lose if you lose. The Kelly formula combines them into one optimal position size:

> **Optimal position size = win probability ÷ loss magnitude − loss probability ÷ gain magnitude**

Intuitively: **the more you stand to lose, the less you should buy; the more you stand to gain, the more you can buy.** The formula finds the optimal balance between the two.

Three examples:

**Example one: a good opportunity — high odds of winning, losses you can absorb.**

Take a stock where you judge there's a 60% probability it doubles and a 40% probability it falls 50%.

> Position size = 0.6 ÷ 0.5 − 0.4 ÷ 1.0 = 1.2 − 0.4 = **80%**

High win rate, large upside, limited downside — Kelly says go heavy.

**Example two: an average opportunity — a slight edge, but upside and downside are roughly symmetric.**

A 55% probability of gaining 30% and a 45% probability of losing 30%.

> Position size = 0.55 ÷ 0.3 − 0.45 ÷ 0.3 = 1.83 − 1.50 = **33%**

With only a slight edge in win rate, the position size should shrink dramatically.

**Example three: no edge — don't touch it.**

A 50% probability of gaining 20% and a 50% probability of losing 20%.

> Position size = 0.5 ÷ 0.2 − 0.5 ÷ 0.2 = 2.5 − 2.5 = **0%**

A coin-flip opportunity has an optimal position size of zero — without an informational edge, you shouldn't bet at all. If the calculation comes out negative, stay even further away.

The logic is clear: **an opportunity truly worth a heavy position needs all three at once — a high win rate, large profit potential, and limited loss magnitude. Missing any one of the three, the position size should come down.**

## What Happens If You Buy Too Much? Not More Profit — Inevitable Ruin

This is the most counterintuitive part of the Kelly formula. Many people think, "If I'm bullish, I should buy more." But the math says that once you exceed the optimal position size, the more you buy, the less you earn over the long run — until you lose everything.

![Kelly curve](../assets/kelly/fig1-kelly-curve.png)

| Position strategy | Long-run growth rate | Volatility | One-line summary |
|---------|-----------|------|-----------|
| Half Kelly | 75% of optimal | 50% of optimal | Slightly less profit, but far steadier |
| Full Kelly | 100% (theoretical optimum) | 100% | Fastest growth, but extreme swings |
| 1.5x Kelly | 75% of optimal | 150% of optimal | Same return as half Kelly, but triple the volatility |
| **2x Kelly** | **Zero** | 200% of optimal | **All for nothing** |
| **Beyond 2x** | **Negative** | Higher still | **Inevitable long-run ruin** |

Look closely at the 1.5x row: the growth rate is identical to half Kelly, but the volatility is triple. Buying more doesn't earn more — it just means enduring three times the pain.

2x Kelly is even more extreme — every single trade is profitable, yet the long-run growth rate is zero. **Beyond 2x, even if every trade wins, you will inevitably end up losing everything.**

This isn't theoretical. In 1998, Long-Term Capital Management, running 25x leverage, lost $4.6 billion in four months. In 2021, the Archegos fund saw $36 billion wiped out in two days. They weren't wrong about their calls — their position sizes were too large.

## Why Losing 50% Is Far Worse Than You Think

Overbetting is lethal because losses and gains are mathematically asymmetric:

![Loss asymmetry](../assets/kelly/fig2-loss-asymmetry.png)

| Loss | Gain needed to break even |
|------|-----------|
| -10% | +11% |
| -20% | +25% |
| -30% | +43% |
| -50% | **+100% (must double)** |
| -75% | **+300% (must quadruple)** |

Lose 10%, and gaining 11% gets you back to even. But lose 50%, and you need to double your money to break even. That's because investing is multiplicative, not additive — losing 50% means multiplying by 0.5, and gaining 50% means multiplying by 1.5; 0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75, which still falls short of 1.

**The essence of the Kelly formula is avoiding large losses.** Not losing money is the best way to make money.

## The Formula Is Simple — the Hard Part Is Where the Four Numbers Come From

In a casino, the win probability and payout odds are written into the rules and can be calculated precisely. In stock investing, however, nobody hands you these four parameters — you have to estimate them yourself.

This is the real difficulty of the Kelly formula, and also its deepest insight: **the quality of your estimates for the four parameters depends entirely on how deeply you understand the company.**

**Gain magnitude — how much can you make if you win?** You need to know what the company is actually worth and how much of a discount the current price represents. That requires understanding its business model, competitive landscape, and growth runway. If you don't understand the business, you can't estimate its value, and you won't know how much upside there is.

**Loss magnitude — how much can you lose if you lose?** You need to know what happens to the company in a worst-case scenario. Is it a short-term earnings wobble, or is the business model being disrupted? Is cash flow healthy enough to weather it, or is the balance sheet so fragile it shatters on impact? If you don't understand the business, you can't tell "temporary difficulty" apart from "permanent destruction."

**Win probability — how likely are you to win?** You need to judge whether your own valuation holds up and whether the market's reason for undervaluing it is actually valid. That requires understanding industry trends, management competence, and the depth of the moat. If you don't understand the business, your "60% win probability" may just be wishful thinking.

**The loss probability is simply 1 minus the win probability** — it doesn't need to be estimated separately, but it's a reminder that even your most confident judgment can be wrong.

All four parameters point to the same thing: **whether you truly understand the company.**

So what the Kelly formula ultimately points to isn't a math problem — it's the most fundamental question in investing: **buying a stock means buying a company.** The deeper your understanding of the company, the more accurate your parameter estimates, and the more rational your position sizing. If you know nothing about the company and every parameter is a guess, the number the formula spits out is meaningless.

This also explains why truly great investors dare to go heavy: not because they're bold, but because they've spent enormous amounts of time researching and understand the four parameters far better than everyone else. In their eyes, the volatility that panics the market isn't risk at all — it's just the price swinging around.

---

## Two Practical Rules of Discipline

Once you understand the logic of the formula, two rules of discipline are enough in practice:

**First, cut it in half.** Even when you're highly confident, use only half of what you consider the reasonable position size. The reason is simple: your estimate of your own judgment is almost always inflated. Half Kelly sacrifices only 25% of the growth rate while cutting volatility by 50% — an extremely favorable trade. Leave enough room for error, because an investing career is long enough that you will make mistakes.

**Second, set a hard cap.** No single stock should ever exceed 30% of total assets. No matter how confident you are, this is an iron rule. The math above has already proven it: exceeding the Kelly limit doesn't mean earning more — it means heading toward ruin.

---

## In Closing

The Kelly formula uses mathematics to prove something investors should already know intuitively:

**For a company you don't understand, the optimal position size is zero.** When every parameter is a guess, the resulting position size means nothing.

**Only a company you truly understand earns the right to talk about position sizing.** The deeper the understanding, the more accurate the estimate, and the more rational the position.

Back to the simplest statement of all: buying a stock means buying a company. The Kelly formula merely re-proves this point with mathematics — **your right to place a bet comes from your understanding of the company.**
