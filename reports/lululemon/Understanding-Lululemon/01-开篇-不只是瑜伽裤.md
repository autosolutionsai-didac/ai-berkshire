## A Company That's Hard to Read

Two and a half years, $46 billion in market cap evaporated to $31 billion — is this a normal brand-collapse story, or a mispriced opportunity the market got wrong?

The stock price took this path:

- December 2023: **$511** all-time high — the "next Nike" narrative peaks
- Mid-2024: around **$340** — growth begins to slow, the market is still hesitant
- Early 2025: **$250** — North America posts consecutive negative growth, confidence shaken
- January 2026: CEO Calvin McDonald departs — the last straw
- May 2026: **~$125** — down **75%** from the peak

Now market cap is only $15 billion. But open the financial statements, and you'll find a set of numbers that doesn't match this stock chart at all:

| Metric | FY2025 (through Feb 2026) | Scale Assessment |
|------|---------------------|---------|
| Total revenue | **$11.1 billion** (+5%) | Growth slowing but still positive |
| Net income | **$1.58 billion** | Down from peak, but still a massive profit |
| Gross margin | **58.4%** | Top-tier globally among consumer goods |
| ROIC | **30.6%** | Every $1 invested returns $0.30 |
| China growth rate | **+29%** | The only high-speed growth engine globally |
| P/E (TTM) | **~9x** | vs. a 10-year average of 41x — currently just 22% of that average |
| Buybacks | **Over $4.8 billion** cumulatively over 5 years | Shares outstanding down 9.4% |

Put it all together — **a global brand with a 58% gross margin, 30% ROIC, and $1.58 billion in annual profit, trading at just 9x earnings, 78% below its 10-year average**.

If you think this is just "it's fallen a lot" — you might be missing the real crisis behind it.
If you think "the brand is finished" — you might be missing the other story the financial data is telling.

What this series aims to do is **lay out both sides so you can judge for yourself**.

## What Kind of Business Is lululemon, Really?

Most people's impression of lululemon stops at "the company that sells yoga pants." This is the most common misunderstanding, and also the one that most distorts judgment.

Open up the revenue breakdown:

| Channel | Share of Revenue | Characteristics |
|------|---------|------|
| Company-operated stores | **~55%** | Roughly **811** stores globally, high sales productivity per square foot |
| E-commerce (owned) | **~39%** | Exceeded $1 billion in a single quarter |
| Wholesale/other | **~6%** | Very small share, intentionally kept minimal by the company |

**DTC (Direct-to-Consumer) accounts for over 90% of revenue.** This means: the brand narrative is fully self-controlled, customer data is owned, and gross margin is far higher than under a wholesale model.

Now look at the regional breakdown:

| Region | Share of Revenue | FY2025 Growth |
|------|---------|------------|
| Americas | **72%** | **-1%** (same-store sales -3%) |
| Mainland China | **~16%** | **+29%** |
| Rest of international | **~12%** | **+17%** |

Data sources: lululemon FY2025 annual report; China Daily

**lululemon's real business is not "selling yoga pants for a markup" — it's a premium athletic lifestyle brand company built on technical fabric as its medium, community culture as its moat, and the DTC model as its backbone.**

Its business model is closer to **Hermès** (brand premium + full-price selling + direct control) than to Gap (wholesale-driven + discount-clearance).

This difference means: lululemon's **58% gross margin** has nothing to do with the phrase "selling pants" — it's in the tier of consumer goods company whose financial texture is second only to luxury goods.

## Three Common Misconceptions About lululemon

### Misconception One: "Just another sportswear brand"

There are hundreds, if not thousands, of sportswear brands in the market. What's the fundamental difference between lululemon and them?

One number says it all: **a 95% full-price sell-through rate**.

In the apparel industry, achieving a 70% full-price sell-through rate is already considered excellent. Nike typically runs 60%-70%. lululemon sells 95% of its products at full price — meaning almost no discounts, almost no leftover inventory, almost no brand dilution.

This is not the financial profile of a sportswear brand. This is the financial profile of a **luxury good**.

A pair of Align leggings retails for **$98-$128**, more than **50%** pricier than Nike's comparable products. Consumers aren't unaware there are cheaper options — they know, and still choose lululemon. This pricing power comes from two things:

1. **Product strength**: the feel of Nulu fabric is genuinely different, and most people who've worn it agree
2. **Identity affirmation**: wearing lululemon signals "I care about physical and mental wellness" — the same logic as drinking Moutai or using an iPhone

**Counterargument**: pricing power isn't eternal. Under Armour once had a similar brand premium, and it later fell from $150 to $7. Once a brand's "coolness" fades, the premium can evaporate quickly. And lululemon's appeal among the 18-25 age group is already being eaten away by Alo Yoga.

### Misconception Two: "Growth has already peaked"

Looking at the top-line number, FY2025 revenue grew just 5% — not exactly exciting. But break it down:

- **Americas (72% of revenue)**: **-1%**, weak for 7 consecutive quarters
- **China (16% of revenue)**: **+29%**, Q4 same-store growth of +30%
- **Rest of international (12% of revenue)**: **+17%**

**lululemon isn't "peaking" — it's "the US engine stalling while the China engine is just starting up."**

China currently has only **170 stores**, and brand awareness is only in the **low teens as a percentage**. For reference, Nike has **over 6,000** stores in China, with brand awareness above **90%**. If lululemon's awareness in China rises from 15% to 30%, China revenue could at least double.

In 2026, lululemon will also enter six new markets: **Greece, Austria, Poland, Hungary, Romania, and India**.

Data sources: lululemon official press releases; Chain Store Age

**Counterargument**: the China market carries geopolitical risk. When consumer nationalism rises, foreign brands are the first to be hit. Domestic brands (Particle Fever, MAIA ACTIVE) are rising, priced 30%-50% below lululemon, with the quality gap narrowing. Betting all of the growth thesis on China is a risky strategy.

### Misconception Three: "9x P/E = cheap, time to buy"

Mathematically this isn't wrong — **9x P/E is the lowest in lululemon's history as a public company**, 78% below its 10-year average of 41x.

But a low P/E doesn't equal cheap. A low P/E can have three possible explanations:

| Possibility | Explanation | If this is the case |
|--------|------|---------------|
| Market is overly pessimistic | Nothing wrong with the brand, just a short-term pile-up of crises | An excellent buying opportunity |
| Permanent growth slowdown | North America won't recover, China is the only engine | Fair valuation, not much of a bargain |
| Irreversible brand decline | Falls from grace like Under Armour | Could get even cheaper |

**Which of these does the current 9x P/E actually reflect?** This is the core question of this series, and it will be explored in detail in Part 04.
