## Part One: The Confusion of Our Era

### On the domestic front

**The employment problem**: The unemployment rate for people aged 16 to 24 has reached roughly 20%. At its core, this reflects a crisis of confidence among private enterprises. China has roughly 700-800 million people in its workforce, of which 80-90% of jobs are provided by non-state-owned enterprises, with only about 10% coming from state-owned enterprises.

**The consumer confidence crisis**: Real estate once accounted for 70% of Chinese household wealth, and still accounts for roughly 60% today. A sharp decline in real estate and stock market prices inevitably affects people's consumer confidence and expectations for the future.

**Supply side vs. demand side**: Economic policy in the past focused mainly on the supply side, but the current problem lies primarily on the demand side, producing deflationary pressure. The popular domestic term "involution" (juan) actually refers to "extreme competition in a deflationary environment."

**Export dependence**: After more than 40 years of development, China now accounts for more than 30% of global manufacturing value-added, but its own consumption absorbs only about half of that. This means that roughly half of the goods China produces must be sold to other countries, with developed nations as its largest customers.

### On the international front

**A fundamental questioning of America's role**: The biggest variable of the past five or six years has been that the United States has begun to fundamentally question its own role in international society. Since World War II, the United States has played the role of "anchor" of the global order — maintaining peace, promoting international trade, and safeguarding the flow of capital.

The United States bears roughly 80% of global military spending, and as the world's buyer of last resort, currency provider, and consumer market, it has played a stabilizing role in the order. But the American public now widely believes the costs have outweighed the benefits, and that China — having ridden the coattails of the US-led order — now poses a fundamental challenge in return. This has led the United States to reassess its role in the international order.

**The fundamental question**: How will the international order evolve going forward? Who will invest in and maintain the public goods of international trade? What challenges will Chinese industry face?

## Part Two: Reflections on the Confusion

### Historical background

This confusion is not unique to China. Looking back over 500 years of world history, every country that has undergone an industrial takeoff and entered the middle-income stage has gone through an "intermediate consolidation period" — a "Stage 2.5." **China today is in Stage 2.5.**

Every country that has undergone an industrial takeoff — Germany, Japan, the countries of South America and Southeast Asia — has faced the same challenges China faces today. Some have successfully crossed this threshold; others remain stuck in the middle-income trap to this day.

### The essence of the problem

**Defining modernization**: At its core, modernization is the automatic, compounding, sustained economic growth produced by the combination of a market economy and modern technology.

Compounding economic growth is a "terrifying mathematical concept." Compared with the early days of Reform and Opening Up, China's real economy has grown tens or even hundreds of times over. Yet even as the economy has compounded, **social governance, the human psyche, and political institutions have not undergone compounding change to the same degree.**

Human nature has not fundamentally changed in the 200,000 years since Homo sapiens emerged, yet our economy has gone through three great leaps: from hunter-gatherer civilization to agricultural civilization, and then to modern technological civilization.

**Where the gap comes from**: Once the early industrial-takeoff phase ends, an enormous gap inevitably opens up between the reality of compounding economic development and the slow pace of change in society, psychology, and political governance. This gap is what triggers all manner of problems — and it is something every country experiences at this stage.

### Historical case studies

**The time pattern of industrialization**: Thirty to forty years of compounding growth is enough to produce enormous economic change in an economy, especially during the takeoff phase:

- Japan: from the Meiji Restoration in 1868 to its victory over Russia in 1905 — more than 30 years
- Germany: from unification to World War I — thirty to forty years
- The United States: from after the Civil War to becoming the world's largest economy in the 1890s — likewise more than 30 years
- South America: from after World War II to falling into the middle-income trap in the 1980s and 90s — also more than 30 years

The problems that arise at this stage typically pose a comprehensive challenge to society. Some societies manage to organize themselves and get through this stage; others require a much longer consolidation period; and some go astray and bring about serious tragedies. **Both World War I and World War II were, to some degree, connected to this dynamic.**

**A key turning point**: War itself did not solve this problem — what actually solved it was the transformation in governance that came after the war.

### Shifts in mindset

#### Example one: the concept of land

In Civilization 2.0 — the agricultural age — land and population were the most important determinants of economic scale. Agricultural economies faced a "ceiling" (the Malthusian trap): once population growth outstripped the land's carrying capacity, the land could no longer support more people. Territorial expansion was always the most important demand of communities, ethnic groups, and nations.

**But in Civilization 3.0 — the age of technology — as the economy enters a sustainable, compounding growth phase, the driver of economic growth is no longer land and population, but the size of the market and the degree to which factors of production circulate freely.**

This clash between old and new mindsets was the most important cause of both world wars. When World War I broke out, most parties assumed the war would be over within weeks, but the deep-rooted mentality of fighting over territory instead produced 50-60 million deaths and led to the collapse of every empire that took part.

**The outcome of the war ran counter to its aims**: After World War II, Germany and Japan — the two chief instigators of both wars — actually achieved, after their defeat, the territory and living space they had sought. What they had failed to achieve through war, they obtained through the peace that followed defeat.

The most important event after World War II: the United States became the first country in human history to return, free of charge, all the territory it had won in victory to its original owners. In exchange, the United States built an international system of trade, commodity exchange, and capital flows founded on American principles.

**In the age of Civilization 3.0, land is no longer the most important factor in economic growth. It has been replaced by the size of markets and the free circulation of economic factors — including technology, labor, and capital.**

#### Example two: the real economy vs. the virtual economy

Over the past several years, government policy has often drawn a distinction between the "real economy" and the "virtual economy," emphasizing a shift "from virtual back to real." That distinction was valid during the transition from agriculture to industry, but once an economy reaches Stage 2.5 and maturity, the distinction no longer holds — it is a false concept.

**The gaming example**: Video games are seen as the archetypal virtual economy, yet on the Russia-Ukraine battlefield, what determines victory or defeat today is no longer tanks or machine guns, or even unintelligent missiles, but smart drones — and the people piloting those drones are gamers. There are roughly one to two million combat drones on the Russia-Ukraine battlefield, and the "soldiers" operating them are mostly gamers.

**The Nvidia example**: In the 31 years since its founding in 1993, Nvidia has never manufactured a single wafer — all of its chips are made by TSMC. It is, in effect, a software design company, and in that sense carries the characteristics of a "virtual economy" business.

Nvidia's market capitalization now exceeds the combined market value of all publicly listed companies in Germany — indeed, it exceeds the combined stock market value of both Germany and Italy, and it exceeds the total stock market value of more than 100 countries. At today's share price, only five countries in the world have a total stock market value greater than Nvidia's market cap.

**Conclusion**: The virtual economy and the real economy have become inseparable, and the distinction between them is no longer necessary. Many old ideas are still profoundly shaping how we judge today's economy.

#### Example three: the function of government

On the domestic front, China's economy has reached $18 trillion in size, with more than 100 million enterprises, making billions to tens of billions of dollars' worth of economic decisions every single day. A complexity and scale of this magnitude is far beyond what any small group of people could hope to influence, plan, or direct.

The fundamental principle of a market economy is that all major economic decisions must be made independently and separately by entrepreneurs with a personal stake in the outcome, operating in a fully competitive environment.

On the international front, half of what China produces is sold around the world, and China has become the first- or second-largest trading partner of 120 countries. Those 120 countries together account for more than 80% of the world's economic output outside China. The lives of the billions of people in those countries are profoundly affected by a billion private decisions made in China.

**Many decisions made by the Chinese government now have broad and profound effects globally, bound up with the livelihoods and interests of at least several billion people.**

As most countries transition from an agricultural economy to a modern technological one, the function of government gradually shifts from commanding and directing to being republican, consultative, supportive, and service-oriented. This shift in the function of government is not only a practical requirement of China's current stage of development, but is also determined by the very nature of China's economy.

## Part Three: The Middle-Income Trap and International Relations

### The essence of a 3.0 economy

A 3.0 economy is able to grow continuously and spontaneously mainly because all the economic factors within it can be exchanged and circulated freely. Every act of free trade, every free exchange, produces a 1+1>2 effect — and the exchange of knowledge can even produce a 1+1>4 effect.

**Every truly modernized, sustainably growing 3.0 economy shares one crucial trait: full, unobstructed exchange and circulation of every factor within the economy, with no bottlenecks.**

### China's two key problems

#### Problem one: consumption and savings

Personal consumption accounts for only 40% of China's overall economy, and that share has been declining in recent years. Meanwhile, the savings rate has risen from 40% to roughly 50%.

The vast majority of these savings sit within the banking system, which is dominated by state-owned banks. Can a state-owned banking system fully channel these savings back into economic circulation? The answer is no — there is no successful precedent for this, anywhere, at any point in history.

Getting savings into a system that genuinely benefits economic growth requires a modern capital market and an efficient financial system to promote the effective allocation and circulation of capital.

#### The history of financial innovation

Venice — from the High Middle Ages to the Napoleonic era, a span of more than a thousand years — was the longest-lasting republic in human history. Between the years 1000 and 1500, despite having a population of only around a hundred thousand or so, it monopolized the most important trade routes connecting Asia and Europe.

**Venice's greatest achievement**: it was the first to invent crucial pieces of the modern financial system — double-entry bookkeeping, along with joint-stock ownership, insurance, and modern banking.

After the Netherlands declared independence in 1581, it fought a 70-year war of independence and, in the 17th century, rose in one leap to become the foremost maritime trading empire. The Dutch invented the publicly held limited-liability joint-stock company — the world's earliest public company was the Dutch East India Company. The Netherlands also established a central bank and a stock exchange.

**Britain's breakthrough**: the Glorious Revolution of 1688 achieved two things:

1. In governance, it adopted a republican, constitutional-monarchy system in which royal power was checked by various forces
2. It completed an institutional merger: it "acquired" the Dutch financial system

William III and his wife Mary II became England's joint monarchs, and William III transplanted the entire Dutch financial system onto English soil.

This merger brought Britain a complete, modern financial system. **What a capital market provides is not just capital — more importantly, it provides a system of credit and trust.**

The function of a trust system: entrepreneurs need entrepreneurial credibility, investors need investor credibility, and intermediary institutions need institutional credibility. Ultimately, this system can pool the small savings of ordinary savers who know nothing about business into a massive force for investment.

The entire process builds a complete system of trust, together with the legal institutions, dispute-resolution mechanisms, customary practices, and mutual trust that go along with it.

#### Problem two: Hong Kong's potential

**A parallel opportunity for China and Britain**: Britain left China a gift — Hong Kong. Hong Kong possesses every element of a modern capital market: a complete set of institutions, laws, historical traditions, dispute-resolution mechanisms, trusted intermediary institutions, and the confidence of international investors.

But these advantages have not yet been truly put to use.

**The difference between Britain and China**: the Dutch-British union was a merger of equals, whereas China's relationship to Hong Kong looks more like an acquisition. What is acquired is not always cherished as much as it should be — and that is a very significant difference.

If Hong Kong's advantages could truly be put to use, it could serve as an important seed for reworking China's capital markets. The Hong Kong-Shanghai Stock Connect was an important innovation, but it is only a beginning.

**A warning**: some of what has happened in recent years has already threatened the very foundation of Hong Kong's existence as an independent financial market. A system of trust has this characteristic: it takes a very long time to build up, but it can be broken in a very short time. Hong Kong's markets and its system need to be cherished and cared for — but that requires first understanding their importance.

If this is not corrected in time, the consequences will be incalculable. China's economy still falls far short of its true underlying growth potential.

### The key to China's economic growth

At present, China can only rely on policy stimulus, but stimulus is not very sustainable. Stimulus only works if it produces sustainable growth. If it fails to generate sustainable growth, the country will be forced to rely on a fresh round of stimulus every year.

**The essence of "Chinese-style modernization"**: While it is true that "Chinese-style modernization" is a valid concept (every country has its own particularities), at bottom it is still modernization, and so it shares a great deal in common with modernization elsewhere. "We must advance modernization on the basis of what is universal. Only when the universal and the particular reinforce each other do we get genuine practice."

What is universal is the consensus that people have formed over the past several hundred years by drawing lessons from failure and success. **Common sense is the scarcest form of knowledge, because it is usually formed only after paying a price for violating it.**

The modern market economy has now been operating for four or five hundred years, and some of the consensus it has produced no longer needs to be debated or questioned — still less should it be casually criticized or rejected.

**The core idea of a market economy**: although human nature is fundamentally self-interested, the great achievement of a market economy is that, through the division of labor and free competition, it converts individuals' pursuit of private gain into a public good that benefits society as a whole — achieving the optimal allocation of social resources, driving continuous economic growth, and benefiting every stratum of society.

### Essential institutional guarantees

In a market economy, the vast majority of resource-allocation decisions must be made by private actors. **The success of China's market economy has been achieved precisely through a process in which the government has continually ceded power, continually stepped back, and shifted its function from commanding to serving.**

The guarantees that must be provided:

1. **Personal and property safety**: entrepreneurs must first have a guarantee of personal safety — without a guarantee of personal safety, no one can build a successful enterprise
2. **Procedural justice**: resolving legal disputes requires procedural justice; the difference between "rule by law" and "rule of law" lies largely in whether government power is checked by the law
3. **Capital markets**: **a sound, complete capital market is the single most important guarantee for the free circulation of economic factors**

### The key indicator

Every country that enters an intermediate consolidation period after an industrial takeoff has encountered the same problems: some have successfully crossed the threshold, some have gone through tragedy, and some are still struggling. There is no fixed template that can simply be copied to achieve modernization.

But there is one clear KPI (key performance indicator): **achieving genuine modernization — that is, China being able to generate sustainable economic growth through spontaneous, endogenous forces.**

The single biggest driver of this is **the share of personal consumption in GDP.** This is the most original, spontaneous, and sustainable engine of economic growth.

- Personal consumption accounts for 40% of China's GDP
- In India, the figure is 60%
- In the United States, it exceeds 70%
- Once China reaches this stage, its growth, too, can become sustainable

China has: some of the world's best entrepreneurs, some of the world's best engineers, the largest, most unified market of consumer demand and supply, trustworthy investors, and an appeal to global professional institutions across the entire chain of trust. **These conditions provide enormous potential for achieving spontaneous, sustainable economic growth.**

### Where the breakthrough could come from

To reignite the engine of economic growth from where things stand today — the economy is a chain of interlocking links, involving many nodes:

- Entrepreneurial spirit
- Consumer confidence
- Positive incentives within the bureaucracy
- Foreign investor confidence
- Improvement in US-China relations
- A better international trade environment
- Making use of Hong Kong's capital markets

**Every one of these nodes is both the "chicken" and the "egg" — each is a chicken that can also lay eggs.** Any single node can trigger a chain reaction, because they all interact with one another. Igniting any one of them can light up the entire economic chain.

But right now, every node on the chain has run into problems — and that is the confusion we face in this era.

Since September of this year, we've at least seen a significant shift in policy. As long as we hold to the principle that practice is the sole criterion for testing truth, keep experimenting and correcting course, and persevere, we will eventually ignite one of these nodes. And once one is ignited, every node will pull the others along, because all the nodes on the chain are interconnected.

In the early days of Reform and Opening Up, who could have predicted that a few dozen farmers signing a blood pact to adopt the household responsibility system would ignite forty years of sweeping reform in China? Within a single year, it solved China's food-and-clothing problem. The reforms in the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone rapidly ignited a nationwide wave of reform. **History has shown that many major changes need not be — and in fact cannot be — planned in advance.**

China's potential remains enormous. The problems we face are the same ones every country that has undergone an industrial takeoff has faced — there is nothing special about them. To a great extent, these problems stem from the huge gap between our deep-rooted mindsets — formed during the agricultural age or even earlier — and the economic reality produced by the enormous, compounding growth that has since taken place.

Within that gap, we need to re-examine our old mindsets and test which ones are right and which are wrong. **In the end, we must hold to the principle that practice is the sole criterion for testing truth, and use the results of practice to test our ideas and our actions.**

## Part Four: How a Global Value Investor Should Respond to the Challenges of This Era

### A basic stance

First, the macro environment is an objective fact — all we can do is accept it. It is at the micro level that we can actually make a difference. This is a value investor's basic stance.

**The world exists objectively; it will not change because of our wishes, our imagination, or our subjective judgments.**

Our approach to investing is to "take the world the way it is, not what we wish it to be, not what we want it to be. It is what it is — take it."

On that premise, we can make a difference at the micro level, in specific companies.

### The essence of wealth

Amid all this macro confusion, can you still genuinely hold these companies with conviction? That is the central question.

**Defining wealth**: wealth is the share of the entire economy's purchasing power that belongs to you.

#### Historical case studies

**British aristocrats**: many aristocratic families who originally owned land still hold onto a great deal of land and grand castles today — in the past, this made them the wealthiest people around. Yet today, aristocrats who own nothing but land and castles are no longer wealthy; they have even become relatively poor, since maintaining these castles requires a great deal of labor, and the value of human labor has changed enormously.

The aristocrats who remain wealthy are wealthy because they have other investments, not because they still rely solely on their original land and castles. Many aristocrats now generate income by opening their castles to public visitors — turning them into parks and charging five pounds a head.

**The example of the "ten-thousand-yuan household"**: in the early days of Reform and Opening Up, having ten thousand yuan was a remarkable thing, and a "ten-thousand-yuan household" was considered among the wealthiest people around. Suppose we had put those ten thousand yuan in the bank back then — with principal and interest, would it make you rich today? Clearly not. Many people today earn more than that in a single month's salary.

**Conclusion**: land, cash, and real estate can none of them serve as lasting wealth.

#### Wealth in the modern economy

In the age of agricultural civilization, output per unit barely grew at all, and there was a "ceiling" on total economic output. An individual's share of the economy's purchasing power was relatively fixed, and was realized through land, population, and property.

Once an economy enters a phase of sustained, cumulative growth, it may fluctuate along the way, but over the long run it moves in one direction — up. If your wealth is static, it will gradually shrink relative to a growing economy.

Over the past forty-plus years, China's nominal GDP has grown by hundreds, even thousands of times over. The "ten-thousand-yuan households" of that era are no longer wealthy today. In the United States, "millionaire" used to be a remarkable status, but a millionaire back then was roughly equivalent to a billionaire today.

**Static wealth held in the form of cash is not wealth that grows on a sustainable, cumulative basis.**

Once an economy enters an age of sustained, cumulative growth, **true wealth should be measured by the share of purchasing power you hold within the entire economy.** And your effective wealth is the share of purchasing power you hold within the economy in which you actually wish to consume.

#### The fundamental purpose of investing

**The fundamental purpose of investing is to preserve and increase your purchasing power.**

The standard for measuring how wealthy someone is is their share within the economy, not an absolute number. One person is wealthier than another because they hold a larger share of purchasing power within the economy.

Ten thousand yuan today no longer carries the meaning it once did for a "ten-thousand-yuan household." True wealth is your share of the overall economy. As long as you maintain your share, you have preserved your wealth — even if the entire economy's "pie" shrinks due to war or some other factor, your wealth has not actually decreased. And if your share rises, your wealth is still growing.

But once we enter modern civilization, the "pie" grows in waves, on a sustained, cumulative basis. This sustained growth is the most fundamental, most defining feature of the modern economy.

### The state of global economic development today

Of today's global population of 8 billion:

- Roughly 10-15% have already entered a stage of endogenous, self-sustaining growth that does not depend on outside forces
- Roughly 50% are in an intermediate transitional state (China among them)
- The rest are still in the early stage of transitioning from an agricultural economy toward an industrial takeoff

**This trend is a process that has unfolded continuously over several centuries and cannot be reversed by anyone's will — it is a transformation of civilizational paradigm.**

### The mission of the value investor

**As a value investor, you need to understand what value is, and what wealth truly means — the thing to pursue, protect, and grow — and that is your share of purchasing power within the economy.**

For a global value investor like Himalaya Capital, our responsibility as a fiduciary is to preserve and increase our share of purchasing power on a global scale. Concretely, that means finding, on behalf of our investors, the most dynamic and creative companies within the world's most dynamic economies, and holding their shares in order to preserve and grow our purchasing power.

In this way, when the overall economy grows, your wealth naturally grows along with it; and if your share increases, it means your growth has outpaced the average. Even if the overall economy shrinks for whatever reason, as long as your share rises, your wealth is still increasing.

Once you hold this view of wealth, you will better understand what it means to say: **the macro is something we must accept; it is the micro where we can actually make a difference.**

With this understanding, you can hold the most creative, outstanding businesses with equanimity, without being shaken by fluctuations in the macro environment.

### A sense of urgency

Once this civilizational paradigm shift is underway, the world economy will keep growing — a trend that no country's will can alter. Countries that get stuck at the middle-income stage and fail to cross it will see their relative share of the world economy steadily decline.

**The example of South America**: at the end of the 19th century, both Brazil and Argentina were among the most promising developing countries, yet despite repeated attempts, neither managed to cross the "middle-income trap." Both got another chance after World War II, but growth stalled again after the 1980s. Meanwhile, the world economy as a whole kept growing, and these two countries' share of the global economy kept declining. Both countries once ranked among the world's leading economies; today, that standing is nowhere to be found. **This is exactly why we must maintain a certain sense of urgency.**

### Choices for individual investors

**As a global investor, you need to invest in whatever economies you consider most dynamic, but you also need to pay attention to your own actual needs, and preserve your purchasing power wherever it is that you need to spend it.**

As a global investor, Himalaya Capital's goal is to select, from the most dynamic economies worldwide, the most dynamic, creative, and competitive enterprises, and to hold shares in them.

But for an individual investor, you need to preserve your purchasing power within the economy where you are willing, and need, to spend it — that is your true wealth. Many Chinese investors have most of their purchasing-power needs in China, and may not need purchasing power in Europe or South America at all.

### The history of value investing

Value investing was born precisely in a period when the economy and the macro environment as a whole were extremely turbulent and full of confusion.

**Ben Graham** was the first person to fully articulate the idea of value investing, and he was Buffett's teacher.

- He began investing in 1926; his first three years coincided with the "Roaring Twenties," during which he made many speculative trades
- During the Great Depression of 1929-1932, his investment partnership lost as much as 70% of its book value
- Chastened by this experience, he began to genuinely practice value investing
- From 1932 to 1935 he successfully recovered his earlier losses
- In 1936 he founded a new closed-end fund, which ran until 1956, delivering extraordinary returns over those 20 years
- In 1949 he published The Intelligent Investor, laying out for the first time the three most important ideas of value investing in full

**The backdrop of Graham's era**: US unemployment reached 25%, and the economy shrank by a third to a half. People broadly felt there was no future, as if the world were coming to an end. Not long after, the world was plunged into a global war launched by fascism, which killed upward of a hundred million people and destroyed most of the world's industrial base. **It was against this backdrop that he built an extraordinary investment record.**

Looking back at our own era compared with the thirty years in which Graham founded and practiced value investing, **it is precisely in such a turbulent, confusing macro environment that value investing shows its advantages and comes into its own.**

**Keynes** was also an outstanding value investor. From 1921 until his death in 1946, Keynes managed the endowment of King's College, Cambridge, building an outstanding investment record over 25 years. Keynes's Britain was on the front line of World War II, while Graham's America was in the rear, far from the fighting — which makes Keynes's record all the more meaningful.

**John Templeton** played an important role in advancing value investing and spreading it to other countries.

- In 1939, in the middle of the war, many American stocks fell below one dollar a share
- Holding to the principle that "cheap is king," Templeton bought 100 shares of every stock trading below one dollar, putting in a total of ten thousand dollars
- He sold four years later; of the 104 stocks, 100 had risen sharply
- In 1954 he founded the Templeton Fund and began spreading value investing to other countries
- By 1992, after 38 years and through every kind of market change, the fund had delivered returns of more than tenfold

**Li Lu's own experience**: I founded Himalaya Fund in 1997. Before that, in 1993, I bought my first stock, starting with cheap companies. Right at the fund's founding in 1997, we went through the Asian Financial Crisis. Markets across Asia's major economies fell by more than 70% across the board, with the worst hit falling more than 90%. Taken together, those years turned out to be a period of very high returns — the market was littered with gold.

### The six principles of value investing

#### 1. The essential nature of a stock

A stock is not merely a piece of tradeable paper; it represents partial ownership of a company. This matters a great deal in a continuously growing economy — equity ownership is what safeguards your purchasing power.

#### 2. The role of Mr. Market

The market is made up of individuals, and human nature seeks short-term gain, tending to treat stocks as short-term trading chips. You can picture the market as "Mr. Market" — a neurotic character whose function is not to tell you what something is truly worth, but simply to offer you a price at which to buy or sell.

These prices are often far below or far above value. **For a value investor, Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you.**

#### 3. Margin of safety

The future is hard to predict, because you may not fully understand a company, and you cannot clearly foresee its future. But if you buy at a low enough price and leave yourself an ample margin of safety, your investment will feel more secure, and you'll be better able to hold it for the long term.

When the market presents an extreme mispricing to the downside — offering a huge margin of safety — can you seize that opportunity? To a large extent, that determines whether you can truly create wealth.

#### 4. Circle of competence

Long-term investment returns come, in large part, from the value that outstanding companies create through their long-term operating performance. Outstanding companies can keep growing their intrinsic value — which lines up perfectly with a defining feature of the modern economy itself: a company's intrinsic value can keep compounding indefinitely, alongside the economy's own cumulative growth.

These high-quality companies sustain a return on capital that is higher than the industry average and their competitors' over the long run. So investing in this kind of company means your wealth compounds faster than the market average. But picking out and understanding such companies is not easy, which is why investors need to build their own circle of competence — knowing clearly what they understand and what they don't, and where the boundary of that circle lies — and invest only in high-quality companies within that circle, companies they can genuinely understand, and hold them for the long term.

#### 5. Fish where the fish are

This is Mr. Munger's contribution. Investors don't need to spend excessive effort studying macroeconomics, and they don't need to master the global economy inside and out. **What you need to know is which lake has fish in it — where competition is thin, and where you happen to have deep knowledge — and build your circle of competence there.**

Minnesota has more than ten thousand lakes. Investors don't need to understand every company, don't need to master every macroeconomic parameter or government policy, and certainly don't need to accurately forecast the next ten years. The key is finding the one "lake" where you can actually catch fish.

Insufficient competition is one very important reason mispricing occurs.

#### 6. A new definition of wealth

Wealth is your share of purchasing power within the economy. **The goal of value investing is to preserve and grow wealth by holding shares in the most dynamic companies, within the most dynamic economies.**

This is the lesson and contribution that Himalaya Fund has distilled from thirty years of practice.

### What practice has taught me

My own thirty years can be read as a running commentary on these six principles. When I first arrived in the United States, I owned nothing — I had negative net worth. That I can stand here today and share this experience with you is truly thanks to the practice of value investing. **The ideas of value investing can be put into practice — and practiced successfully, over the long term.**

I hope that, like Mr. Buffett and Mr. Munger, I will still have another thirty years ahead of me to keep practicing value investing. I feel just as much passion for this field today as I did thirty years ago. It is a way of breathing and growing together with your era, and that is what makes it so compelling.
