# Tencent Music Entertainment — Business Model and Moat Analysis

**Analysis dimension**: Business model, moat, and user value | **Analytical framework**: Duan Yongping's investment lens
**Research date**: June 20, 2026 | **Ticker**: NYSE: TME / HKEX: 1698.HK

---

## One-line conclusion

TME has completed its transition from a "live-streaming tipping company" to a "music subscription company," and revenue quality has improved markedly — but its moat is not "exclusive copyright" (that was broken up by antitrust enforcement); it is a combination of "scale + ecosystem + inertia." That moat is enough to keep out small players, but not an attacker at the scale of ByteDance. Duan Yongping would ask: is your moat that others don't want to come in, or that they can't come in? TME's answer increasingly looks like the former is turning into the latter.

## II. The Nature of the Business Model: A Structural Shift Between Two Engines

### 2.1 Year-by-Year Breakdown of Revenue Structure

| Year | Total revenue (RMB 100M) | Online music revenue | Share | Social entertainment revenue | Share | YoY growth |
|------|--------------|------------|------|------------|------|---------|
| 2019 | 25.40 billion | ~7.6 billion | ~30% | ~17.8 billion | ~70% | — |
| 2022 | 28.38 billion | ~17.0 billion | ~60% | ~11.4 billion | ~40% | -1.5% |
| 2023 | 27.76 billion | 17.33 billion | 62% | 10.43 billion | 38% | -2.2% |
| 2024 | 28.40 billion | 21.74 billion | 76% | 6.66 billion | 24% | +2.3% |
| 2025 | 32.90 billion | 26.73 billion | 81% | 6.18 billion | 19% | +15.8% |
| Q1 2026 | 7.90 billion | 6.51 billion | 82% | 1.38 billion | 18% | +7.3% |

*Data source: TME annual reports (20-F/6-K)*

**Online music revenue breakdown (full-year 2025)**:

| Sub-item | Revenue (RMB 100M) | YoY growth | Share of online music |
|------|------------|---------|------------|
| Subscription revenue | 17.66 billion | +16.0% | 66% |
| Non-subscription revenue (advertising + sublicensing, etc.) | 9.07 billion | +39.2% | 34% |

**Key judgment**: This was not an "active strategic pivot" but "the right transition completed under duress." The root causes of the social entertainment business's collapse were: (1) regulation restricting minors from tipping; (2) Douyin/Kuaishou pulling away live-streaming users; (3) natural user attrition at WeSing (Quanmin K-Song) and Kugou Live. TME's shift toward online music subscriptions happened because the back door caught fire and it had to run out the front. The outcome is good, but the process shouldn't be romanticized.

### 2.2 The Essence of the Business Model

At its core, TME's business model is a **music content distribution platform** connecting two sides: upstream are record labels and musicians (content suppliers), downstream are users (consumers).

**Three layers of monetization**:

| Layer | Model | Representative products | Margin | Sustainability |
|------|------|---------|--------|---------|
| Layer 1 | Membership subscriptions | QQ Music Green Diamond/SVIP | High (gross margin ~45%) | ★★★★ |
| Layer 2 | Advertising + sublicensing | Ad monetization of free users | Medium | ★★★ |
| Layer 3 | Social entertainment (tipping) | WeSing / Kugou Live | Low and unstable | ★★ |

**Duan Yongping's key question: Why do users pay?**

The real reason users pay is not "this particular song is here" (other platforms have it too), but rather:
1. **Sunk cost of playlists and favorites** — years of accumulated playlists, liked songs, and listening history that users are reluctant to abandon
2. **Personalization of algorithmic recommendations** — the longer you use it, the more accurate the recommendations get; switching platforms means "retraining" from scratch
3. **Embedded social relationships** — music links shared among WeChat/QQ friends default to opening QQ Music
4. **Habitual inertia** — "this is just what I've always used"

This is not the strongest moat (unlike Kweichow Moutai's "must-have" status), but it does create sufficient inertia.

### 2.3 SVIP Strategy: The Current Core Growth Driver

| Metric | Data | Source |
|------|------|------|
| Number of SVIP users | Surpassed 20 million | TME 2025 annual report |
| SVIP penetration rate | ~16% (of 127.4 million paying users) | Estimate |
| SVIP monthly fee | RMB 28-30 (regular membership RMB 15-18) | QQ Music official site |
| SVIP price premium | 60-100% | Calculation |

SVIP is essentially "squeezing another layer" out of the existing paying user base. That's not a bad thing — Spotify is doing the same thing (HiFi Plan). But the ceiling is clear: 20 million / 127.4 million = 16% penetration, with management targeting 20-25%, leaving room for roughly 4-11 million more users. At RMB 30/month for SVIP vs. RMB 15/month for regular membership, every 5-percentage-point rise in SVIP penetration adds roughly RMB 1.1 billion in annualized revenue.

**Counter-check**: Raising SVIP prices is extremely difficult in the current consumption environment. ARPPU in Q4 2025 was already flat quarter-over-quarter at RMB 11.9, and growth momentum is fading. Douyin's Qishui Music's free model (watch an 80-second ad for a day of membership, now simply given away on open) is eroding the willingness-to-pay mindset from the bottom up.

## III. Platform Flywheel Effect

### 3.1 Flywheel Model

```
Licensed content → attracts users → converts to paying users → increases revenue → funds more licensing
    ↑                                              |
    └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Verification of each stage of the flywheel**:

| Stage | Operating status | Evidence | Risk point |
|------|---------|------|--------|
| Licensing → users | ⚠️ Weakening | MAU has declined for 16 straight quarters, to 528 million (end of 2025) | After exclusive licensing was broken up, copyright no longer serves as an exclusive user-acquisition tool |
| Users → paying | ✅ Still functioning | Paying ratio rose from ~15% in 2022 to 24.1% (end of 2025) | Growth rate has slowed from 8.3% to 5.3%, a clear deceleration |
| Paying → revenue | ✅ Healthy | Subscription revenue of RMB 17.66 billion, +16% | Limited room left for ARPPU growth |
| Revenue → licensing | ⚠️ Changing | Cut exclusive-licensing procurement budget by 30% in 2024, shifting support toward original content | Licensing cost remains the largest expense item |

**The flywheel's real problem**: the "licensing → users" link is failing. After the 2021 antitrust order broke up exclusive licensing, users no longer come "because only here can you find this song." The flywheel has shifted from being "licensing-driven" to "inertia-driven plus ecosystem-driven." The problem with inertia-driven momentum is that it eventually stops spinning.

### 3.2 Flywheel Score: ★★★

The flywheel exists but is decelerating. The critical "licensing → users" link has weakened significantly since exclusive licensing was dismantled. Whether alternative drivers (algorithmic recommendations, social sharing, SVIP perks) can fill the gap is the key thing to watch over the next 2-3 years.

## IV. Verifying the Moat, Piece by Piece

### 4.1 Brand Moat

| Brand | Positioning | MAU (Q4 2024 estimate) | User profile |
|------|------|------------------|---------|
| QQ Music | Flagship, young white-collar | ~250 million | Tier 1-2 cities, ages 18-35 |
| Kugou Music | Mass market | ~200 million | Lower-tier markets, all ages |
| Kuwo Music | Differentiated supplement | ~60 million | In-car / long-form audio |
| WeSing (Quanmin K-Song) | Karaoke and social singing | ~80 million (estimate) | All ages |

**Is brand a moat?** Not entirely. QQ Music has brand recognition, but it isn't a brand like "BMW" or "Moutai" that commands a price premium. Users choose QQ Music mostly because of the Tencent ecosystem (WeChat/QQ entry points) and historical inertia, not brand loyalty. Kugou has brand presence in lower-tier markets, but its brand power is weaker still.

**Brand score: ★★★** — has recognition, but doesn't constitute an independent competitive barrier.

### 4.2 Switching Costs

| Source of switching cost | Strength | Notes |
|------------|------|------|
| Playlists / favorites | ★★★ | Years of accumulated playlists aren't easy to migrate, but technically can be exported |
| Listening history / recommendation algorithm | ★★★★ | This is the strongest switching cost — the longer you use it, the more accurate the recommendations, and a new platform has to be "retaught" from scratch |
| Social relationships | ★★★ | WeChat-shared links default to QQ Music; Moments listening leaderboards |
| Paid membership | ★★ | Users who've already paid for annual plans won't switch in the short term, but will leave if they don't renew |
| Downloaded songs | ★★ | Offline downloads live on the device; switching platforms means re-downloading |

**Is switching cost a moat?** It's one of TME's most tangible moats, but not as strong as it might seem. Migrating playlists isn't technically hard (third-party tools exist), recommendation algorithms are being built by everyone (Qishui Music, backed by ByteDance, has stronger algorithmic capability), and social-relationship embedding is a genuine advantage — music sharing within the WeChat ecosystem points almost 100% to QQ Music.

**How Duan Yongping might see it**: "Switching cost is one thing; users being too lazy to switch and users not wanting to switch are two different things. TME's users are mostly the former — too lazy to switch. Once there's a strong enough reason (say, Qishui being free plus better recommendations), even lazy people will move."

**Switching cost score: ★★★** — switching costs exist but aren't deep enough, especially under the impact of Qishui Music's free model.

### 4.3 Network Effects

| Type of network effect | Present? | Strength | Notes |
|------------|--------|------|------|
| Direct network effect (social entertainment) | Yes | ★★ | WeSing's social nature carries some network effect, but this business is shrinking continuously |
| Indirect network effect (creators-listeners) | Yes | ★★★ | More listeners → more musicians join → more content → more listeners |
| Data network effect (recommendation algorithm) | Yes | ★★★ | More user behavior data → more precise recommendations → better experience → more usage |

**Are network effects a moat?** Partly, but overstated. The network effect in social entertainment (live-streaming/karaoke) is real, but that business has shrunk from 70% of revenue to 18%, and the network effect is self-reinforcingly decaying along with user attrition. The network effect in online music is comparatively weak — one person listening to music doesn't require others to be listening on the same platform.

**Network effect score: ★★** — moderate strength within a shrinking social-entertainment segment, nearly absent in the core music-subscription business.

### 4.4 Scale Effects

| Dimension of scale advantage | Strength | Notes |
|------------|------|------|
| Licensing procurement scale | ★★★★ | The purchasing volume from 127.4 million paying users confers bargaining power, and licensing cost is spread over more heads |
| Amortization of R&D spend | ★★★ | R&D costs for AI recommendation and audio technology are spread across 500+ million users |
| Bandwidth / server costs | ★★★ | Scale brings cost advantages in CDN and other infrastructure |
| Channel reach | ★★★★ | The three apps (QQ Music/Kugou/Kuwo) cover distinct user segments, which a single competitor would find hard to replicate |

**Are scale effects a moat?** This is TME's most important structural advantage. With 127.4 million paying users, licensing costs can be amortized much more broadly. Assuming annual licensing costs of RMB 15 billion, spread across 127.4 million users that's RMB 117 per person per year, or under RMB 10 per month, while ARPPU on the revenue side is already RMB 11.9. If a competitor had only half of TME's paying users, it would have to pay more for the same catalog — or buy a narrower one.

**But the flip side is**: Qishui Music doesn't need the full catalog — it only needs the hit songs. Music consumption within the Douyin ecosystem is dominated by breakout hits, and the amortization advantage of a long-tail catalog is partially dissolved in the short-video era.

**Scale effect score: ★★★★** — this is TME's strongest moat, but it is being eroded by the "free + algorithm" model.

### 4.5 Technology Barriers

| Technology area | TME's positioning | Competitiveness |
|---------|---------|--------|
| AI recommendation algorithm | Self-developed, but weaker than ByteDance's | ★★★ |
| AI music generation | Tianqin Lab, MUSE model | ★★★ |
| Audio technology | High-fidelity codec, spatial audio | ★★★ |
| Content recognition / copyright protection | Industry-leading | ★★★★ |

**Is technology a moat?** Not an independent one. TME's technical capabilities lead within the music industry, but aren't outstanding across the broader internet industry. ByteDance's recommendation algorithms and overall AI capability outmatch TME's. Technology is more a "necessary condition" than a "sufficient condition."

**Technology barrier score: ★★★** — has an accumulated base, but doesn't constitute a decisive barrier.

### 4.6 Composite Moat Assessment

| Moat type | Score | Trend |
|-----------|------|------|
| Brand | ★★★ | → Stable |
| Switching costs | ★★★ | ↓ Weakened by Qishui Music's free model |
| Network effects | ★★ | ↓ Declining as social entertainment shrinks |
| Scale effects | ★★★★ | → Still the strongest barrier |
| Technology barriers | ★★★ | → Stable but not decisive |
| **Composite** | **★★★** | **↓ The overall moat is getting shallower, and the pace is worth watching closely** |

## V. User / Creator Value

### 5.1 Value Created for Listeners

| User need | Degree to which TME meets it | Degree of differentiation |
|---------|------------|----------|
| Massive catalog | ★★★★★ | ★★ (platforms are converging) |
| Personalized recommendations | ★★★★ | ★★★ (QQ Music's recommendations are decent, but not as good as NetEase Cloud Music's) |
| Lossless / high-fidelity audio | ★★★★★ | ★★★★ (an exclusive SVIP perk) |
| Social interaction | ★★★ | ★★★ (boosted by the WeChat ecosystem) |
| Live performances | ★★★★ | ★★★★ (TME Live's exclusive resources) |

**The counterpoint**: on the core user experience — "discovering good music" — NetEase Cloud Music's community atmosphere and recommendation algorithm have long been considered superior by users. TME's advantage lies in being "comprehensive" rather than "refined."

### 5.2 Value Created for Musicians

| Value dimension | What TME provides | Comparison with competitors |
|---------|--------|------------|
| Distribution reach | 500+ million MAU across three apps | Largest in the industry |
| Copyright income | Revenue share by play count | Industry standard |
| Original-content support | Yuanli Program 2.0 (RMB 200 million annual investment) | NetEase Cloud Music has a similar program |
| Data analytics | Open platform provides play-count data | Industry standard |
| Performance opportunities | TME Live, TMEA Awards | A differentiated advantage |

**Key judgment**: TME's value to musicians is chiefly "the largest distribution scale." But that doesn't constitute exclusive value — musicians typically distribute on multiple platforms simultaneously. After exclusive licensing was broken up, TME's binding power over musicians has been declining.

### 5.3 Value Created for Hosts/Streamers

The social entertainment business continues to shrink, and the streamer ecosystem is being siphoned off by Douyin, Kuaishou, WeChat Channels, and similar platforms. In 2025, social entertainment revenue was only RMB 6.18 billion (18.8% of total), down year-over-year. It fell a further 11% in Q1 2026.

**Duan Yongping's view**: "If your users, creators, or streamers can leave for a competitor at any time, and the experience there is about the same or even better, then the value you're creating is replaceable. Replaceable value isn't worth much."

### User Value Score: ★★★

TME offers users the most comprehensive music catalog in the industry and the convenience of the Tencent social ecosystem, but it isn't the best on core experience (recommendations, community). The value it creates for creators and streamers is replaceable.

## VI. Business Matrix and Synergies

### 6.1 Business Matrix

| Segment | Product | Positioning | Revenue contribution | Growth status |
|---------|------|------|---------|---------|
| Online music | QQ Music | Flagship / tier 1-2 | ~60% of online music | ✅ Growing |
| Online music | Kugou Music | Lower-tier / mass market | ~30% of online music | ⚠️ Slowing growth |
| Online music | Kuwo Music | In-car / long-form audio | ~10% of online music | ⚠️ Flat |
| Social entertainment | WeSing (Quanmin K-Song) | Karaoke and social singing | Majority of social entertainment | ❌ Declining |
| Social entertainment | Kugou Live | Live-streaming tipping | Minority of social entertainment | ❌ Rapidly declining |
| New business | TME Live | Online/offline performances | Contributes to non-subscription revenue | ✅ Growing |
| Pending integration | Ximalaya | Long-form audio / podcasts | Acquisition pending completion | ⏳ Being integrated |

### 6.2 Three-App Synergy

Synergy among the three apps (QQ Music/Kugou/Kuwo) is a structural advantage unique to TME:

- **Complementary user coverage**: QQ Music captures young white-collar workers, Kugou captures the lower-tier market, Kuwo captures in-car scenarios — together covering 500+ million MAU
- **Amortized licensing costs**: one license serves all three apps, lowering per-app acquisition cost relative to competitors
- **Data sharing**: user-behavior data from the three apps is pooled, strengthening AI recommendation capability
- **Internal traffic allocation**: Kugou users are funneled to QQ Music via short-form video

**But the flip side of synergy**: there's also internal friction among the three apps. QQ Music and Kugou Music overlap significantly in tier 1-2 cities, and ad placements pushing the same song are effectively competing for the same users. The management overhead of running three apps independently isn't small either.

### 6.3 The Ximalaya Acquisition: A RMB 9 Billion Strategic Bet

| Metric | Data |
|------|------|
| Acquisition price | $1.26 billion (~RMB 9 billion) |
| Deal structure | Cash + equity |
| Ximalaya MAU | ~300 million |
| Content assets | 5.2 million audiobooks, 240,000 podcasts |
| In-car penetration | Integrated with 80+ automakers |

**Duan Yongping's view**: "Spending RMB 9 billion on Ximalaya — what exactly did they buy? A long-form-audio company that still hasn't made much money. If it could make money, it would have already — why does it need you to buy it first? Management has to prove that 1+1>2, but most acquisitions ultimately prove 1+1<1.5."

### Business Synergy Score: ★★★

Three-app synergy is a real structural advantage, but the integration risk of the Ximalaya acquisition is substantial. The continued shrinkage of social entertainment is weakening the overall value of the matrix.

## VII. Assessing TME Against Duan Yongping's "Good Business" Standard

Duan Yongping's core standard: "A good business is one that is differentiated and profitable."

### 7.1 Differentiation

| Question | Answer | Score |
|------|------|------|
| What is unique about the product? | Three-app coverage plus the Tencent social ecosystem are unique, but the music content itself has no differentiation | ★★★ |
| Can users tell you apart from competitors? | They can distinguish the brand, but the core experience is converging | ★★ |
| Can competitors replicate you? | The three-app matrix isn't replicable in the short term, but a single app can be | ★★★ |

**Differentiation score: ★★★** — differentiation comes from the ecosystem and scale, not the product itself.

### 7.2 Pricing Power

| Dimension | Performance | Score |
|------|------|------|
| Ability to raise prices | Raised regular membership from RMB 13 to RMB 15/month in 2023; user acceptance was reasonably good | ★★★ |
| Room for further increases | Current ARPPU is RMB 11.9/month, versus Spotify's roughly $10/month (~RMB 72) — theoretically a large gap to close | ★★★★ |
| Willingness to raise prices | ARPPU growth stalled starting Q4 2025; management has paused price increases amid a downward consumption environment | ★★ |
| Constraint from competitor pricing | Qishui Music is free, NetEase Cloud Music's pricing is similar | ★★ |

**Pricing power judgment**: TME has some pricing power, but it's constrained. Chinese consumers' sensitivity to paying for digital content is far higher than in Europe or the US. Spotify's $10/month pricing in Western markets isn't replicable in China — the ceiling Chinese users can accept is roughly RMB 20-25/month. Qishui Music's free strategy is eroding the willingness-to-pay mindset from the bottom, further limiting room for price increases.

**Pricing power score: ★★★** — present but constrained, nowhere near the "raise prices whenever it wants" level of Moutai or Apple.

### 7.3 Sustainable Competitive Advantage

**Duan Yongping's "ten-year test"**: will TME still be around in 10 years?

| Factor | Judgment |
|------|------|
| Demand for music consumption | Will definitely still exist in 10 years — humanity's need to listen to music won't disappear |
| TME as a platform | Very likely still around, but its market share may fall from 66% to 50-55% |
| Subscription model | Likely still the mainstream, but the threat from the free + ad-supported model shouldn't be ignored |
| Moat strength | May weaken further — the algorithmic recommendation gap will narrow, and licensing will become more of a public resource |

**Duan Yongping would probably say**: "TME isn't a business you can 'set and forget and it just keeps getting more valuable.' It needs continuous investment, continuous competition, and continuous spending on licenses. That kind of business can work, but it's not the best kind of business. The best business is like Moutai — put it away for 10 years without touching it, and it becomes more valuable on its own. Leave TME alone for 10 years, and it might get eaten by Qishui Music."

**Sustainable competitive advantage score: ★★★** — sustainable, but requires ongoing investment to maintain; not a "sit back and collect" type of business.

## VIII. Non-Consensus Views and Counter-Checks

### 8.1 Market Consensus vs. My Judgment

| Market consensus | Counter-check |
|---------|---------|
| "TME is still strong even after the licensing moat was broken up" | Fact: MAU has declined for 16 straight quarters to 528 million, and Qishui Music's MAU has already reached 140 million. The consequences of the licensing moat's collapse are materializing — it's just that profit growth is temporarily masking the user attrition |
| "There's still room for the paying ratio to rise" | Going from 15% to 24% is already a large increase. The ceiling for Chinese users' willingness to pay may be around 30% (compared with Spotify's global ~45%), and the incremental headroom is narrowing |
| "SVIP is a new growth engine" | SVIP has 20 million users at 16% penetration, with a 20-25% target. Marginal contribution is diminishing: most of the hardcore users willing to pay RMB 30/month have already converted |
| "Qishui Music's catalog is incomplete, so the threat is limited" | Qishui Music doesn't need the full catalog — Douyin users' music consumption is dominated by breakout hits. 82% of Qishui's users come from Douyin, a traffic channel TME doesn't have |
| "Social entertainment will stabilize after bottoming out" | It's still falling at an 11% pace in Q1 2026, with no bottom in sight. And once social-entertainment users leave, they don't come back |

### 8.2 The Biggest Risk: Halting User-Data Disclosure

Starting in Q1 2026, TME stopped disclosing quarterly MAU, paying-user count, and ARPPU, switching instead to disclosing only year-end paying-user counts annually.

**This is an extremely bad signal.** A company whose users are growing does not stop disclosing user data. The only reasonable explanation for halting disclosure is that the numbers don't look good, and management doesn't want the market watching this metric closely every quarter.

Duan Yongping has repeatedly emphasized: "Management's integrity is the most important thing. The data they don't want you to see is usually exactly the data you most need to see."

## IX. Overall Assessment by Dimension

| Assessment item | Score | One-line judgment |
|--------|------|----------|
| Clarity of business model | ★★★★ | The subscription + advertising model is clear, and revenue quality has improved markedly |
| Flywheel effect | ★★★ | Exists but is decelerating; the licensing→users link is failing |
| Moat strength | ★★★ | Scale is the strongest barrier, but the overall moat is getting shallower |
| User value | ★★★ | Comprehensive rather than refined; the core experience is replaceable |
| Business synergy | ★★★ | Three-app synergy is real but comes with friction; Ximalaya integration risk is large |
| Differentiation | ★★★ | Comes from the ecosystem, not the product itself |
| Pricing power | ★★★ | Present but constrained, far below the "good business" standard |
| Sustainability | ★★★ | Requires ongoing investment to maintain; not a "sit back and collect" type |

### Overall Score: ★★★ (3.0/5)

**One-line conclusion**: TME's business model has become healthier after the transition, and subscription revenue's 81% share is a positive signal — but its moat is "scale + inertia" rather than "irreplaceable," and its pricing power is constrained by the market and by competition, falling short of Duan Yongping's highest "good business" standard. This is a "decent" business, not a "great" one. TME will very likely still be around in ten years, but its market share and bargaining power may by then have declined significantly.
