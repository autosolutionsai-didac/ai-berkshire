# Ximalaya and risk — the ambitions and costs of an audio empire

> "Understanding TME" series · Part 04 · Acquisition and risk
> Reading time: ~8 minutes

---

## A $2.9 billion acquisition, and the three layers of logic behind it

On May 12, 2026, Tencent Music announced it had received regulatory approval to acquire Ximalaya for approximately **$2.9 billion** (roughly RMB 21.0 billion). This is one of the largest acquisitions in China's internet sector in recent years.

But this deal wasn't a simple "buy it because you want it" — regulators attached **five restrictive conditions** before clearing it.

This piece answers two questions: first, does the strategic logic for acquiring Ximalaya hold up? Second, beyond Ximalaya, what other risks does Tencent Music face?

---

## I. The Ximalaya acquisition: breaking down the strategic logic

### Why buy it?

First, some numbers to understand Ximalaya's scale:

| Metric | Ximalaya (2025 data, est.) | Notes |
|------|------------------------|------|
| MAU | ~280-300 million | China's largest audio platform |
| Content types | Audiobooks + podcasts + paid knowledge content + radio drama | Full coverage of non-music audio |
| Revenue scale | ~RMB 6.0-7.0 billion (est.) | ~RMB 6.1 billion at the time of its 2023 IPO prospectus |
| Profitability | Near break-even (est.) | Long-standing losses, narrowing sharply in recent years |

Tencent Music's logic for buying Ximalaya has three layers:

**Layer one: upgrading from a "music platform" to an "audio platform."**

Music subscriptions have a ceiling — Chinese users are willing to pay RMB 15-25 a month for music, and that's about it. But add in audiobooks, podcasts, and paid knowledge content, and the same user's spending ceiling can be raised significantly.

Analogy: Spotify started pushing hard into podcasts in 2019, and podcast listeners now account for over 40% of its total MAU. That strategy helped lift Spotify's ARPPU from roughly $5 in the pure-music era to a higher level, because advertising revenue opened up a second growth curve.

**Layer two: hedging against Qishui Music's competition.**

What Qishui Music can compete on is "pure music" — it has no audiobooks, no podcasts. If Tencent Music becomes an integrated "music + audiobooks + podcasts" audio platform, **the axis of competition shifts from "who has more songs at a lower price" to "whose audio content is richer"** — the latter is much harder for Qishui to catch up on in a short time.

**Layer three: putting cash to work at a high return.**

Tencent Music has RMB 38.0 billion of cash on its books. Just letting it sit and earn interest yields 2-3% annually. Spending $2.9 billion on an audio platform with RMB 6.0-7.0 billion in annual revenue that's near break-even — if it can achieve a 10-15% profit margin after integration — would add roughly RMB 0.6-1.0 billion in incremental profit each year. **Using 20% of the cash pile to buy an incremental profit engine makes mathematical sense.**

### Counterpoint: the acquisition's risks shouldn't be dismissed

**First, the $2.9 billion price isn't cheap.** Ximalaya was valued at roughly RMB 25.0-30.0 billion ($3.5-4.0 billion) at its 2023 IPO, and its public-market valuation shrank significantly by 2024. At $2.9 billion, Tencent Music is paying close to that IPO valuation — did it overpay?

**Second, integration is difficult.** Music subscriptions and audiobooks/podcasts are two completely different business models — the former relies on copyright procurement plus algorithmic recommendation, the latter on original content production plus community operations. The gap between the two in team culture and operating logic is large, and failed integrations are common in the internet industry.

**Third, regulators attached five restrictive conditions.** These conditions may limit the room for synergy between Tencent Music and Ximalaya. While the specific conditions haven't been fully disclosed, the fact that approval came "with conditions" itself signals that regulators are worried the deal could produce monopolistic effects — and the restrictions may be precisely what limits the most valuable synergy scenarios.

## II. Integration risk: lessons from history

Internet-sector acquisitions don't have a great track record of successful integration. A few reference points:

| Acquisition | Consideration | Outcome |
|--------|------|------|
| Tencent's acquisition of China Literature | Controlling stake | Success — China Literature has kept growing within Tencent's ecosystem |
| Baidu's acquisition of YY Live | $3.6 billion | Failure — continued user attrition, business eventually marginalized |
| Alibaba's acquisition of Youku | ~$4.0 billion | Controversial — persistent losses for many years |
| Spotify's acquisition of Gimlet (podcasts) | $230 million | Success — podcasts became Spotify's second growth curve |

Tencent has successful experience in content integration (China Literature), but also several middling outcomes. The integration difficulty with Ximalaya lies in:

1. **Ximalaya's core assets are its creator ecosystem and user community** — these "soft assets" are easily lost during M&A.
2. **Audiobook copyrights and music copyrights are two entirely different systems** — integrating the supply chain will take time.
3. **The five regulatory restrictions may block the most direct synergies** — for example, prohibiting forced bundling or exclusive distribution.

**A reasonable expectation**: integration will take 18-24 months before initial results show. In an optimistic scenario, Ximalaya turns profitable within the Tencent Music system and content synergies boost overall profit by 10-15%. In a pessimistic scenario, integration friction causes user attrition and creators to leave, and the $2.9 billion investment takes more than five years to pay back.

## III. Other risks: it's not just Qishui Music

Beyond Qishui Music and the Ximalaya integration, Tencent Music faces several other risks worth taking seriously.

### Risk one: Chinese ADR delisting

**This isn't unique to Tencent Music, but it's real.**

Tencent Music is dual-listed on NYSE in the US and HKEX in Hong Kong. If deteriorating US-China relations force Chinese ADRs to delist from US exchanges, the short-term impact on the share price could be severe — something already demonstrated once during the panic selling of Chinese ADRs in 2021-2022.

Mitigating factors:
- Tencent Music is already secondary-listed in Hong Kong — even if the US listing is delisted, the Hong Kong shares remain tradable
- It has passed PCAOB audit compliance inspections
- Investors holding Hong Kong shares would not be directly affected

**But a US delisting would still cause liquidity loss and further valuation compression.** Currently, average daily US trading volume far exceeds Hong Kong's — if the US channel closes, a wave of forced-selling institutional money could push the price down further in the short term.

### Risk two: AI music disruption

In 2025-2026, AI music-generation technology (such as Suno, Udio, etc.) has advanced rapidly. AI can already generate reasonably good songs — melody, arrangement, even lyrics can all be produced by AI.

If AI drives the cost of music creation toward zero, what does that mean for Tencent Music?

**The upside**: copyright procurement costs could fall — if AI-generated music floods the market, record labels' bargaining power weakens.

**The downside**: the scarcity of music is diluted. When everyone can "compose" with AI, why would users pay to listen to someone else's songs? Over the long run, this could fundamentally change the value basis of music subscriptions.

**Current assessment**: this is a 5-10 year risk. In the near term, AI music's quality, emotional expressiveness, and cultural significance remain far below human-created work. But in 10 years? Uncertain.

### Risk three: narrower management disclosure

Starting Q1 2026, Tencent Music announced it would **no longer disclose user data (MAU, paying-user count, etc.) on a quarterly basis.**

This is a signal worth being wary of.

The charitable reading: management believes MAU is no longer an indicator that reflects the company's value — because user quality (paid-penetration, ARPPU) matters more than user quantity. Ceasing disclosure is meant to steer the market toward the right metrics.

The uncharitable reading: MAU may keep accelerating its decline, and management doesn't want the market to see that number every quarter. Halting disclosure is a form of "information management" — selectively showing the market good data while hiding bad data.

**Both readings have merit, but as an investor, less information is never a good thing.** The less information there is, the larger the "uncertainty discount" the market applies — this may be one of the reasons behind the 68% 52-week crash.

### Risk four: the associated risk from Spotify's stake

Spotify holds roughly 9% of Tencent Music's equity. In 2025, Spotify recognized a fair-value loss of roughly €975 million — meaning Spotify wrote down its Tencent Music stake substantially.

This doesn't affect Tencent Music's fundamentals directly, but there's an implicit risk: **if Spotify decides to liquidate that 9% stake, it would create massive short-term selling pressure.** 9% corresponds to roughly $1.2 billion in market value — a concentrated sale could pile further pressure onto an already-depressed stock price.

## IV. Risk summary table

| Risk | Severity | Probability | Timeframe |
|------|---------|------|---------|
| Large-scale commercialization of Qishui Music | High | Medium | 1-3 years |
| Ximalaya integration failure | Medium-high | Medium | 2-3 years |
| Chinese ADR delisting | High (but cushioned by HK listing) | Low | Uncertain |
| AI music disruption | Medium | Medium-low | 5-10 years |
| Narrower disclosure | Medium-low (but affects valuation) | Already happened | Ongoing |
| Spotify liquidating its stake | Medium (short-term shock) | Medium-low | 1-2 years |

**The two biggest risks are Qishui Music and the Ximalaya integration.** The former determines how much "offensive room" Tencent Music has (whether it can keep raising paid-penetration and ARPPU); the latter determines whether the $2.9 billion investment adds to profit or goes to waste.

Chinese ADR delisting risk, while high-impact, has relatively low probability — and the Hong Kong secondary listing provides a cushion. AI disruption is a more distant risk.

---

## V. Summary

The strategic logic behind the Ximalaya acquisition is clear — upgrading from a music platform to an audio platform, hedging against Qishui's competition, and putting cash to work as an investment. But the $2.9 billion price isn't cheap, integration difficulty is high, and regulatory restrictions are real — **whether this deal succeeds or fails may take 2-3 years to become clear.**

Among the other risks, Chinese ADR delisting and AI disruption are "low probability, high impact" risks, while narrower disclosure and Spotify's stake liquidation are "medium probability, medium impact" risks.

**Taken together, Tencent Music currently faces not one fatal risk, but the accumulation of several medium-level risks.** No single one of these would "kill" the company on its own, but stacked together — Qishui stealing users, Ximalaya consuming resources, information shrinking, geopolitics adding friction — it's no wonder the market has priced it at roughly a 6x cash-adjusted PE, a "fear price."
