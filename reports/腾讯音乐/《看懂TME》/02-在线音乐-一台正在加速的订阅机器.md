# Online Music — A Subscription Machine That's Accelerating

> "Understanding TME" series · Article 02 · Core business
> Reading time: ~9 minutes

---

## A set of numbers that will make you rethink how Tencent Music makes money

First, a table showing the shift in revenue structure:

| Year | Online music revenue (billion yuan) | Share | Social entertainment revenue (billion yuan) | Share |
|------|-------------------|------|-------------------|------|
| 2019 | ~8.0 (est.) | ~30% | ~19.0 (est.) | ~70% |
| 2021 | ~12.0 (est.) | ~38% | 19.2 | ~62% |
| 2023 | 17.7 | ~58% | 12.9 | ~42% |
| 2024 | 21.7 | 76% | 6.7 | 24% |
| 2025 | **26.7** | **81%** | **6.2** | **19%** |

Over 6 years, online music went from three-tenths of revenue to eight-tenths. This isn't "one segment grew" — **the entire company swapped engines.**

Even more important is the change in margins. Social entertainment is essentially livestream-tipping revenue sharing — a user tips 100 yuan, the platform keeps 30-40 yuan, the streamer keeps 60-70 yuan, so gross margin is inherently low. Online music subscriptions are different — a user pays an 18-yuan monthly fee, licensing costs run about 50-55% of that, and nearly all the rest is pure profit.

This explains why total revenue rose only 15.8%, yet gross margin climbed steadily from 30% in 2021 to 44.2% in 2025 — **the quality of earnings is improving.**

## I. Super VIP: the key variable currently unfolding

If you could only track one metric for Tencent Music, it wouldn't be MAU, and it wouldn't be total revenue — it would be **Super VIP** membership (hereafter "Super VIP").

### What is Super VIP?

Tencent Music's membership system has two tiers:

| Type | Monthly fee | Core benefits | User base |
|------|------|---------|---------|
| Regular member | 15-18 yuan/month | Lossless audio, ad-free, downloads | 127.4 million total paying users |
| **Super VIP** | **28-30 yuan/month** | Regular benefits + Dolby Atmos + car mode + multi-device + exclusive content | **20 million+** |

Super VIP members pay 28-30 yuan a month, 1.5-2x what regular members pay. Currently there are 20 million+ such users, with penetration of roughly 16% (of total paying users).

### Why does Super VIP matter so much?

Let's run the numbers.

Currently, 127.4 million paying users pay an average of 11.9 yuan a month (ARPPU). If Super VIP penetration rises from 16% to 25%:

| Scenario | Super VIP users | Effect on ARPPU |
|------|----------|-------------|
| Current (16% penetration) | 20 million | ARPPU 11.9 yuan/month |
| Target (25% penetration) | 32 million | ARPPU projected to rise to 13-14 yuan/month (est.) |

Every 1 percentage point increase in Super VIP penetration corresponds to roughly 1.27 million additional Super VIP users, each paying an extra 12-15 yuan a month. **This amounts to a growth engine that requires no new users at all — purely from getting existing users to spend more.**

### How far can Super VIP go?

The logic supporting Super VIP's expansion is clear:

1. **In-car scenario** — China has over 300 million registered vehicles, and in-car music is a basic need; Super VIP's multi-device benefit connects directly to this scenario
2. **Smart speaker/headphone scenario** — features like Dolby Atmos and spatial audio require Super VIP, and hardware penetration is rising
3. **Exclusive content** — some exclusive content and early-listening rights are reserved for Super VIP users only, creating differentiation

**Counterargument**: Super VIP's price point of 28-30 yuan/month is no longer cheap in the Chinese market — iQIYI's continuous membership is 25 yuan/month, Bilibili's premium membership is 25 yuan/month, and a Qishui Music monthly subscription is only 8 yuan. Against a backdrop of a weaker economic environment, the pool of users willing to pay 28+ yuan for music may be smaller than expected. Super VIP penetration could hit a ceiling around 20-25%.

## II. Pay rate: how far is the road from 24% to 40%?

### Current state

| Region/platform | Pay rate | Notes |
|----------|--------|------|
| Tencent Music (China) | **24%** | 2025 data, rising year over year |
| Spotify (global) | ~39% | Global average |
| Mature overseas markets, average | 40-50% | Europe, U.S., Japan, Korea |

China's online music pay rate of 24% is only half that of mature overseas markets. This is the data bulls most love to cite — "there's still room to double."

### What's driving the pay rate up?

**First, the habit has already taken root.** In 2019, China's online music pay rate was under 10%. Going from 10% to 24% took just 6 years. That indicates users' willingness to pay is rising systemically, not a passing fad.

**Second, free content is shrinking.** After the 2021 antitrust action against exclusive music licensing, rights holders have if anything pushed toward all platforms operating on properly licensed content. The space for free listening is narrowing — shuffled playback, time-limited previews, low audio quality, and other restrictions are "pushing" free users toward paying.

**Third, younger users are more willing to pay.** Those born after 1995 and after 2000 are markedly more accepting of "paying for content" than those born in the 1980s. As the user base generationally turns over, the pay rate naturally rises.

### Where is the ceiling for the pay rate?

The data suggest the path from 24% to 35% is highly likely to be achieved. But going from 35% to above 40% starts to get harder.

There are two reasons:

1. **The peculiarity of the Chinese market** — free alternatives will always exist. Short-video platforms (Douyin, Kuaishou) offer massive music-consumption scenarios, and users don't necessarily need a paid music-subscription platform. This is competition Spotify doesn't face overseas.

2. **Qishui Music's low-price strategy** — an 8-yuan monthly subscription vs. an 18-yuan one; the diversion effect on price-sensitive users is real.

**Reasonable expectation**: reaching a 30-35% pay rate within 3-5 years is a high-probability event. Reaching 40% will take longer, and requires that Qishui Music not grab a large share of paying users.

## III. ARPPU: how much is each user willing to pay per month?

### Current state

| Platform | ARPPU (monthly) | Yuan equivalent |
|------|-----------|----------|
| Tencent Music | 11.9 yuan | 11.9 yuan |
| Spotify | ~$5.3 | ~38 yuan |
| iQIYI | ~20 yuan (est.) | 20 yuan |
| Bilibili | ~15 yuan (est.) | 15 yuan |

Tencent Music's ARPPU is only about a third of Spotify's. But a direct comparison with Spotify isn't reasonable — the two operate in entirely different economic conditions, spending power, and competitive landscapes.

**A more meaningful comparison is with similar domestic platforms.** iQIYI's video membership runs about 20 yuan/month, Bilibili's premium membership about 15 yuan/month. Tencent Music's 11.9 yuan/month is on the low side among domestic content subscriptions.

### Paths for ARPPU to rise

**Path one: rising Super VIP penetration.** This is the clearest path — it requires no price increase, just more people upgrading to Super VIP. Every additional Super VIP user nudges the blended ARPPU up a bit.

**Path two: raising the price of the basic membership.** Tencent Music raised the basic membership from 13 to 15 yuan in 2023, with some plans reaching 18 yuan. Against iQIYI's 25 yuan, there's theoretically still room to raise prices. But the pace of any increase depends on the competitive environment — with Qishui Music's 8-yuan subscription sitting right there, a large, abrupt price hike could push users toward the competitor.

**Path three: increasing ad revenue.** Free users don't pay subscription fees, but they can contribute ad revenue. Tencent Music's current ad-monetization efficiency is relatively low, leaving room for improvement.

### The ceiling for ARPPU

Taking domestic spending power and the competitive landscape together, ARPPU's reasonable ceiling is probably around **20-25 yuan/month**.

The reasoning: assume that 3-5 years from now, Super VIP penetration reaches 25% (paying 28-30 yuan/month) and regular-member ARPPU reaches about 15 yuan (after a modest price increase) → blended ARPPU of about 18-19 yuan. If regular members also go through a round of price increases, it could reach 20-22 yuan.

This ceiling implies: **from the current 11.9 yuan to a ceiling of 20-25 yuan, there's still 70-110% of room to grow.** But this won't happen in one step — it may take 5-8 years to gradually play out.

**Counterargument**: ARPPU improvement presupposes a stable competitive landscape. If Qishui Music significantly expands its paying-user base over the next 2-3 years (currently dominated by its low 8-yuan monthly plan), Tencent Music could be forced to "compete on price" rather than "raise prices." In that scenario, ARPPU growth would be much slower than expected.

## IV. The growth model: putting the three variables together

Subscription revenue = number of paying users x ARPPU

Combining the three variables analyzed above:

| Scenario | Paying users (hundred million) | ARPPU (yuan/month) | Annualized subscription revenue (billion yuan) | vs. 2025 |
|------|-------------|-------------|-------------------|----------|
| **2025 actual** | 1.274 | 11.9 | ~18.2 | Baseline |
| Neutral (3 years out) | 1.5 (pay rate 30%+) | 14-15 | ~25.2-27.0 | **+39-48%** |
| Optimistic (5 years out) | 1.8 (pay rate 35%+) | 18-20 | ~38.9-43.2 | **+114-137%** |
| Pessimistic (3 years out) | 1.2 (Qishui diversion) | 12-13 | ~17.3-18.7 | **-5% to +3%** |

**Under the neutral scenario, pure subscription revenue has 40-50% growth potential within 3 years.** This requires no miracle — just the pay rate and ARPPU continuing along their current trend.

The optimistic scenario more than doubles, but requires large-scale Super VIP penetration plus a competitive landscape that remains favorable.

The pessimistic scenario is one in which Qishui Music grabs a large share of paying users — in that case, Tencent Music's subscription revenue could stagnate or even decline slightly.

## V. An overlooked fact: licensing costs are falling

In 2021, China's State Administration for Market Regulation required Tencent Music to give up exclusive music licensing. At the time, the market panicked — "without exclusive licensing, doesn't Tencent Music's moat collapse?"

But something counterintuitive happened afterward: **once exclusive licensing was scrapped, Tencent Music's licensing costs actually fell.**

The reason: in the exclusive-licensing era, record labels knew you had no choice but to buy — so prices naturally kept climbing in negotiations. After the shift to non-exclusive licensing, Tencent Music could run a "is it worth buying" cost-benefit calculation on every single track — dropping the ones that weren't worth it, since competitors were buying the same songs anyway. Licensing procurement went from an "arms race" to a "careful calculation."

Result: gross margin climbed steadily from 30.0% in 2021 to 44.2% in 2025, and continued rising to 44.9% in Q1 2026.

| Year | Gross margin | Trend |
|------|--------|------|
| 2021 | 30.0% | Late in the exclusive-licensing era |
| 2022 | 33.5% | First year of non-exclusivity |
| 2023 | 38.4% | Continued improvement |
| 2024 | 42.3% | Accelerating improvement |
| 2025 | 44.2% | New high |
| Q1 2026 | 44.9% | New high again |

**Counterargument**: part of the decline in licensing costs may be temporary — a "dividend period" after exclusive contracts expired. If record labels jointly raise prices in the future (similar to what Spotify faces overseas), gross margin could give back some of these gains. But at least for now, the trend remains favorable.

## VI. Summary: the caliber of the subscription machine

Summarizing this article's analysis in a few points:

1. **The revenue structure transformation is complete** — online music accounts for 81%, and the drag from social entertainment has nearly disappeared
2. **Super VIP is the clearest growth engine** — 20 million users, 16% penetration, and every 1 percentage point of increase translates into real incremental revenue
3. **Reaching a 30-35% pay rate from 24% is a high-probability event**, while 40% will take longer
4. **The ARPPU ceiling sits at 20-25 yuan** — 70-110% of room above the current 11.9 yuan, though it will take 5-8 years to gradually get there
5. **Licensing costs are falling** — gross margin has risen for four consecutive years, and this is structural rather than incidental

**Absent a competitor disrupting things**, Tencent Music is a slow but reliably compounding subscription growth machine — paying users increasing, per-user revenue rising, costs falling. Put those three together, and profit growth can be sustained for many years.

But the problem is — **someone is disrupting things.**
