# Tencent Music: Profit Up 25%, Stock Down 68%

> Reading time: about 8 minutes
> This is the first of a three-part series on Tencent Music

---

## A confusing set of numbers

Start with the data:

- FY2025 Non-GAAP net profit of **9.6 billion yuan**, up **25%** year-over-year
- Over the same period, the stock fell from **$26.70** to **$8.47**, a decline of **68%**
- Current market cap is about **93 billion yuan**, with cash and equivalents on the balance sheet of **38 billion yuan**, and net cash (after interest-bearing debt) of about **34.5 billion yuan**
- PE (price-to-earnings ratio) net of cash is just **6x** — the equivalent of paying 6 dollars for a business that earns 1 dollar a year

A company with rapidly growing profit and cash overflowing its balance sheet has nonetheless seen its stock price fall by two-thirds.

This isn't the story of some obscure small-cap stock. This is **Tencent Music Entertainment Group** — China's largest online music platform, home to the QQ Music, Kugou, and Kuwo brands, with a combined paying-user base of more than **127 million**.

What exactly happened? Before we can answer "why did it fall," we need to answer a more basic question first: **what kind of company is Tencent Music, really?**

Because today's Tencent Music and the Tencent Music of three years ago are, for all practical purposes, two completely different companies.

---

## A quiet transfusion

If your mental image of Tencent Music is still "livestream tipping," that picture is out of date.

Look at how the revenue mix has shifted:

| Year | Share of social entertainment revenue | Share of online music revenue |
|------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 2019 | **72%** | 28% |
| 2021 | **58%** | 42% |
| 2023 | 32% | **68%** |
| 2026 Q1 | 17.5% | **82.5%** |

The conclusion from this table is clear: **Tencent Music is no longer a livestreaming company — it's a subscription company.**

In 2019, social entertainment (primarily livestream tipping) contributed seven-tenths of revenue. By the first quarter of 2026, that share had shrunk to under one-fifth, while online music (subscriptions plus advertising) accounted for more than four-fifths.

This transition wasn't forced by the market — livestream tipping carries much lower gross margins than subscriptions, and it faces the double pressure of regulation and user fatigue, making it a shrinking business by nature. Tencent Music chose to proactively cut it loose, giving up short-term revenue in exchange for long-term earnings quality.

How has that worked out? Look at the gross margin:

| Year | Gross margin |
|------|-------|
| 2021 | **30.1%** |
| 2023 | **36.7%** |
| 2026 Q1 | **44.9%** |

In four years, gross margin has jumped from 30% to nearly 45%. A gain of this magnitude is quite rare among mature internet companies.

Two forces are driving it: first, after the 2021 antitrust crackdown eliminated exclusive licensing rights, content costs fell significantly; second, the rising share of high-margin subscription revenue has structurally improved earnings quality.

## Subscription's "second growth curve": SVIP

Tencent Music's paying-user count reached **127 million** by the end of 2025, for a payment rate of about **24%**. That figure still has room to grow relative to global music-streaming peers — Spotify's payment rate is about **39%**, far higher than Tencent Music's, though Spotify doesn't have as large a free-user base as Tencent Music does.

But what really deserves attention isn't total paying users — it's **SVIP (Super VIP)**.

SVIP costs **28-30 yuan** a month, twice the price of a standard membership (about 15 yuan). It offers lossless audio, premium mastered tracks, in-car scenarios, and multi-device sync for smart devices, among other perks. As of the latest data, SVIP users number more than **20 million**, for a penetration rate of about **16%**, and management's target is to raise that to **25%**.

What does this mean? A simple calculation:

- Current ARPPU (average monthly revenue per paying user) is about **11.9 yuan**
- If SVIP penetration rises from 16% to 25%, ARPPU could approach **15 yuan**
- With paying-user count flat, the ARPPU increase alone could drive **26%** growth in subscription revenue

This is why management remains confident about revenue growth even as user growth slows — **the room to raise prices is more certain than the room to add new users.**

Of course, the counter-argument is equally clear: the high SVIP-penetration assumption rests on the premise that "users are willing to pay for audio quality." But China's music market has been free for so long that no one truly knows where the ceiling on users' willingness to pay actually sits. And with Qishui Music (Soda Music) disrupting the market with an 8-yuan monthly pass, the resistance to price increases will only grow stronger.

## Ximalaya: a $2.9 billion gamble

In June 2025, Tencent Music announced it would acquire Ximalaya — China's largest online audio platform — for roughly **$2.9 billion** (in cash plus stock consideration).

The logic behind the deal is straightforward: music and audio (podcasts, audiobooks, paid knowledge content) are adjacent categories with heavily overlapping user scenarios (commuting, exercising, before bed). After the merger, the combined company could:
- Expand the content ecosystem, extending from "listening to music" to "listening to everything"
- Cross-sell memberships to raise ARPU (average revenue per user)
- Share technology and recommendation-algorithm resources

On May 12, 2026, the deal received antitrust approval — but with **5 extremely stringent conditions attached**:

1. **No price increases**: audio-service prices may not be raised for two years
2. **No exclusivity**: the platform may not require content providers to sign exclusive agreements
3. **No bundling**: music and audio memberships may not be forcibly bundled together
4. **No restrictions on creators**: content creators may not be restricted from publishing on other platforms
5. **Keep free content available**: a certain proportion of free listening content must be maintained

In other words, the regulator approved the deal but blocked off nearly every lever that could produce synergies. You can't raise prices to monetize, you can't lock in content exclusively, and you can't cross-sell through bundling — so where exactly are the acquisition's synergies supposed to come from?

The optimistic view: most of these restrictions carry a time window (two years), and brand integration, technology sharing, and user-data connectivity are not covered by the restrictions. The pessimistic view: by the time these restrictions expire in two years, the market environment and competitive landscape may look completely different — waiting that long to talk about synergy could mean the opportunity has already passed.

## Cash: the most underappreciated fact

Tencent Music holds **about 38 billion yuan in cash and equivalents** on its balance sheet (including cash, term deposits, and short-term investments); after subtracting 3.5 billion yuan in notes payable, net cash comes to about **34.5 billion yuan**. This isn't a "paper asset" — it's real money.

**Net cash of 34.5 billion yuan equals 37% of the current market cap.**

That means when you buy Tencent Music at a market cap of 93 billion yuan and subtract the 34.5 billion in net cash, you're effectively paying only about 58.5 billion yuan for the business itself. And in 2025 that business generated roughly **10.2 billion yuan** in operating cash flow; after a modest amount of capital expenditure, free cash flow (FCF — the cash a business generates from operations that is left over and freely disposable after capital spending) came to about **9.8 billion yuan**.

Paying 58.5 billion yuan for a business generating 9.8 billion yuan in annual cash flow implies a return of about **17%**.

The company is in fact returning cash to shareholders:
- Cumulative share buybacks of **$2.5-2.6 billion**
- 2025 dividend of **$368 million**

But the 38-billion cash pile also raises a question: why not buy back more? If management truly believed the stock were severely undervalued, the most rational move would be aggressive buybacks. The current pace of buybacks, relative to the size of the cash reserves, isn't exactly aggressive.

One explanation is that the Ximalaya acquisition requires cash to be set aside (total consideration of about $2.9 billion, of which the cash portion is $1.26 billion, or roughly 9.1 billion yuan). Another explanation is that management's confidence in the business outlook may not be as strong as the valuation numbers would suggest.

## So, what exactly is Tencent Music?

To sum up the picture of this company today:

- **A music platform transitioning from livestream tipping to subscription services**, with the transition roughly 80% complete
- **Margins at an all-time high**, and still improving
- **Extremely flush with cash** — net cash of 34.5 billion yuan equals 37% of market cap, and free cash flow generation is nearly 10 billion yuan a year
- **Expanding into audio through the Ximalaya acquisition**, though regulatory conditions constrain near-term synergy potential
- **An SVIP strategy that provides a pricing-power logic independent of user growth**, though the ceiling for that is unknown

On the fundamentals, this is a company with strong earning power, a healthy balance sheet, and a business transition headed in the right direction.

**And yet the market is pricing it at 6x cash-adjusted PE.**

Why would the market price such a profitable company as though it's "about to blow up"?

The answer is hidden in three words: **Qishui Music (Soda Music).**

And in the three heart-stopping crashes behind which lies the real question of what the market is actually afraid of.
