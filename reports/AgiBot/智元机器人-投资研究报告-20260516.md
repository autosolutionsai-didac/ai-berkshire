## II. Business essence analysis

### One-sentence definition

**AGIBOT's business essence is: selling general-purpose humanoid robot hardware to industrial and commercial customers, while simultaneously attempting to build an embodied-intelligence software platform and developer ecosystem.**

At the current stage, more than 90% of revenue comes from hardware sales, while the platform and ecosystem are still in the investment phase.

### Business model canvas

| Dimension | Description |
|------|------|
| Customers | Automakers (BYD, NIO), electronics manufacturers (Longcheer Technology), auto-parts suppliers (Joyson Electronics), telecom operators (China Mobile), research institutes and universities |
| Value proposition | Replace human labor in "dirty, dangerous, and heavy" factory jobs with humanoid robots, lowering labor costs |
| Revenue model | One-time hardware sales (98,000-620,000 yuan per unit), with some project-based solutions |
| Cost structure | Asset-light OEM model — no self-built factories; contract manufacturing by Junsheng Intelligent, Lens Technology, and Ningbo Huaxiang; heavy R&D investment (75% of employees are in R&D) |
| Key resources | Zhihui Jun's personal brand influence, full-stack technical team, supply-chain alliance (40+ listed-company partners) |
| Channels | Primarily direct sales; overseas expansion beginning in 2026 (Hong Kong as the beachhead, targeting overseas revenue share above 30%) |

### Revenue structure breakdown

| Product line | Positioning | Price range | 2025 shipment share (estimate) |
|--------|------|---------|---------------------|
| Yuanzheng series (A1/A2/A3) | Full-size industrial-grade humanoid robots | 168,000-620,000 yuan | ~34% (approximately 1,742 units) |
| Lingxi series (X1/X2) | Half-size interaction/service robots | 98,000-109,000 yuan | ~36% (approximately 1,846 units) |
| Jingling series (G1/G2) | Research and education robots | 450,000 yuan | ~27% (approximately 1,412 units) |

**Key observations**:
- The Lingxi series has the highest shipment volume but the lowest unit price (roughly 100,000 yuan), while the Yuanzheng series has the highest unit price but does not lead in volume
- Weighted by revenue, the Jingling G1 (450,000 yuan x 1,412 units, approximately 640 million yuan) may contribute more than 60% of total revenue — implying that **research and education scenarios may currently be the largest source of revenue**, rather than industrial manufacturing
- This is a key information blind spot: the company's promotional focus is on industrial scenarios, but the actual revenue mix may be dominated by research and education

|------|------------|--------------|------------|
| 2025 revenue | 1.05 billion yuan | 2.001 billion yuan | 1.708 billion yuan |

**Basis for estimate**: The Yuanzheng A2's full-unit cost is approximately 200,000-300,000 yuan against a selling price of 600,000-700,000 yuan, implying a hardware gross margin of roughly 40-50%; but the Lingxi series sells for only 98,000-109,000 yuan, so its gross margin may be lower. Taking the overall product mix and heavy R&D spending together, the company's overall net margin is very likely a substantial loss.

### Operating leverage analysis

AGIBOT uses an OEM contract-manufacturing model (no self-built factories), so fixed assets are relatively light, but R&D expenses are high (a 1,000+ person team, 75% of whom are in R&D). This implies:
- **Upside**: scaling up does not require large capital expenditure on new factories, so revenue growth can convert to profit improvement relatively quickly
- **Downside**: limited quality-control leverage over contract manufacturers; quality control will be tested at large shipment volumes
- **Core variable**: if annual shipments grow from 5,000 to 50,000 units (10x), R&D expenses stay roughly flat, and the incremental gross profit may be enough to cover R&D costs and reach breakeven

### Follow-up question: what's good about this business?

**In one sentence**: this is a business of "replacing repetitive labor with general-purpose robots," with a potentially enormous addressable market (trillion-yuan scale), but currently at a very early stage — the return on investment of a single robot has not yet been fully validated for customers, and the drivers of repeat purchases remain unclear.

**Why do customers pay?**
- Currently: most customers are purchasing for "strategic positioning" and "technology exploration" purposes, rather than a clear ROI calculation
- Industrial scenarios: replacing 3D jobs (dirty, dangerous, demeaning), but the cost advantage of humanoid robots over dedicated automation equipment has not yet been proven
- Research and education: meets the experimental needs of research institutions and universities, a market with a limited ceiling

## III. Moat assessment

| Moat type | Score | Verification analysis |
|-----------|------|---------|
| Brand/pricing power | Three stars | Zhihui Jun's personal brand brings extremely strong media exposure and customer attention, but the brand is attached to the individual rather than the company; pricing power is weak — product prices are already declining (the Yuanzheng A2 Youth edition is priced at 168,000 yuan, targeting under 200,000 yuan), and an industry price war has already begun |
| Switching costs | One star | Extremely low. Humanoid robots remain at the stage of general-purpose hardware, so the cost for customers to switch brands is very low. No software ecosystem lock-in has formed |
| Network effects | Two stars | The open-source ecosystem (the fully open-sourced Lingxi X1, the AgiBot World dataset) has potential for network effects — the more developers, the richer the data, and the better the models. But this is still early-stage and has not yet formed a closed loop |
| Scale effects | Three stars | The world's largest shipment volume brings supply-chain bargaining power; the ecosystem alliance of 40+ listed companies has created some manufacturing scale advantage. The 2026 capacity target is 40,000+ units; if achieved, costs will fall further |
| Technology/patent barriers | Three stars | Full-stack in-house development (hardware + AI models + operating system + datasets), 317 patents, industry-leading. But humanoid robotics technology is diffusing quickly — 140+ Chinese companies have entered the space, and a clear trend toward technological homogenization is emerging |

### Moat trend judgment

**Past 3 years (2023-2025)**: the moat has been **widening** — from zero to the world's largest shipment volume, establishing first-mover advantage and a supply-chain alliance.

**Forecast for the next 5 years (2026-2030)**: the moat faces **narrowing risk** —
- Tesla's Optimus begins mass production in 2026, and with its brand, capital, and manufacturing scale could quickly change the competitive landscape
- Unitree Robotics is already profitable and has a more focused technology roadmap; its competitiveness should not be underestimated
- Intense competition among 140+ Chinese companies will compress profit margins
- If the open-source ecosystem fails to quickly form a closed loop, the first-mover advantage may be diluted

### Follow-up question: will this moat still be there in 10 years? What could destroy it?

**Factors most likely to destroy the moat**:
1. **Tesla's manufacturing scale** — once Optimus achieves mass production at scale (targeting 20,000 units/week by 2027), prices will be driven extremely low, and pure hardware companies will be unable to compete
2. **Open-sourcing of AI foundation models** — if embodied-intelligence foundation models become open-source infrastructure (similar to the trend in the LLM field), AGIBOT's software barrier will be flattened
3. **Failure of the underlying customer-demand thesis** — if factories find that dedicated automation equipment is more economical than general-purpose humanoid robots, the fundamental premise of the entire sector would be overturned

### Bias self-check

- **Narrative bias**: Zhihui Jun's personal story is too compelling (Huawei "Genius Youth" -> Bilibili influencer with millions of followers -> star entrepreneur), which can easily lead an analyst to be swayed by the narrative and overlook the fundamentals
- **Anchoring effect**: numbers like the 15-billion-yuan valuation and "17x growth" easily become anchors that cause people to overlook the scale of losses and valuation-bubble risk
- **Survivorship bias**: what we see is a top-tier company like AGIBOT that has raised funds smoothly; we do not see the dozens of humanoid robotics startups that have already failed
- **Herding effect**: with Tencent, Hillhouse, and Sequoia all having invested, it's easy to fall into the illusion that "smart money can't be wrong" — but these institutions invested at low primary-market prices, and secondary-market investors face a completely different risk-reward profile

### The bear case

1. **"Volume without profit"** — so what if shipment volume is the world's largest? Unitree Robotics can earn 600 million yuan in profit on 1.7 billion yuan in revenue, while AGIBOT may be losing more than 500 million yuan on 1.05 billion yuan in revenue, suggesting the business model may have a fundamental problem
2. **"Related-party order inflation"** — Longcheer Technology invested in AGIBOT before placing an order for nearly a thousand units, and BYD is both a shareholder and a customer; the genuine commercial motivation behind these orders is questionable
3. **"A capital game"** — investing in 15 companies and forming 17 joint ventures in a single year, plus acquiring a listed company, looks more like financial engineering than product refinement
4. **"Inflated shipment data"** — Unitree has publicly questioned whether AGIBOT is mixing quadruped and other robot forms into its humanoid-robot shipment statistics
5. **"A low ceiling"** — the largest current application scenarios are entertainment/commercial performances and data collection, rather than genuine industrial-productivity replacement

### Follow-up question: where am I most likely to be wrong? Why wouldn't smart people buy?

**Most likely error**: being dazzled by the two numbers "17x revenue growth" and "world's largest shipment volume," while overlooking the possibility that behind them lies loss-making growth and a false prosperity driven by related-party transactions.

**Why smart people wouldn't buy**: until the IPO prospectus discloses audited financial data, it is impossible to determine whether this company is "the next DJI" or "another Royole." Paying HKD 40-50 billion for a loss-making company does not offer favorable odds.

## VI. Industry and civilizational trends

### Is humanoid robotics a "civilizational-scale paradigm shift"?

**Judgment: Yes, but likely on a timescale of 20-30 years, not 3-5 years.**

The ultimate vision of humanoid robotics — a "general-purpose physical labor force" — would, if realized, have an impact comparable to the steam engine, electricity, and the internet. But history tells us that there is often a long "valley of death" between technical feasibility and large-scale commercialization.

### Historical technology-revolution analogies

| Technology revolution | Period of technical feasibility | Period of mass commercialization | Transition time | Where humanoid robotics currently stands |
|---------|-----------|-------------|---------|------------------|
| Steam engine | 1712 (Newcomen) | 1780s (after Watt's improvements) | ~70 years | — |
| Electricity | 1882 (Edison's power station) | 1920s (factory electrification) | ~40 years | — |
| Internet | 1969 (ARPANET) | 1995 (commercial browsers) | ~26 years | — |
| Autonomous driving | 2004 (DARPA Grand Challenge) | **Still not achieved at scale as of 2026** | 20+ years and ongoing | Warning: humanoid robotics may follow a similar path |
| **Humanoid robotics** | 2023 (year of mass-production launch) | **To be determined** | **To be determined** | Comparable to autonomous driving circa 2016 (moving from technology-demonstration period to commercialization-validation period) |

**Key analogy**: humanoid robotics today most closely resembles autonomous driving in 2016 — technology demonstrations were exciting and capital poured in frantically, but the gap between "demonstration" and "genuinely replacing human labor" turned out to be much larger than imagined. Autonomous driving has taken 10+ years and still has not achieved large-scale commercialization at Level 4; humanoid robotics may face a similar timeline.

```

Reducers (Green Harmonic Drive)              AGIBOT                  Automotive manufacturing
Motors (Wolong Electric)               UBTech                     3C electronics
```

**AGIBOT sits in the midstream, finished-unit position**, the segment of the value chain with the most intense competition and the hardest differentiation. Upstream components have technical barriers (reducers, motors), downstream applications have scenario know-how, while finished-unit manufacturers face being squeezed from both sides.

### Technology-path risk

- **The electric-drive approach has become the industry consensus** (Boston Dynamics abandoned hydraulics for electric drive), so near-term path risk is relatively low
- **Long-term risk**: newer drive technologies (artificial muscle, flexible materials) could disrupt the electric-drive approach, but the time window is 10+ years out
- **The bigger risk is on the AI side**: the technology path for embodied-intelligence foundation models has not yet converged — will end-to-end learning (Tesla's approach) or a layered architecture (AGIBOT's GO model approach) ultimately win out? Uncertain

### Follow-up question: looking back in 20 years, will AGIBOT be "the Standard Oil of this era" or "a flash-in-the-pan 3Com"?

**The more likely outcome falls between the two**: the humanoid robotics sector is very likely to prove viable as a business (not a 3Com-style outcome), but the eventual winner may not be today's first mover. Historically, the early leaders of technology revolutions (Yahoo, Nokia, BlackBerry) have rarely turned out to be the ultimate winners. AGIBOT needs to prove it can not only "get off to a fast start" but also "make it to the finish line" — the evidence for that is currently insufficient.

### Reverse DCF: what does the IPO target valuation imply?

The IPO target valuation of HKD 45 billion (approximately 40.9 billion yuan), based on 2025 revenue of 1.05 billion yuan:

**Implied assumptions**:
- To justify a 25x P/S multiple (in line with UBTech/Unitree), 2026 revenue would need to reach **40.9bn/25 ≈ 1.64 billion yuan** (56% growth)
- To justify a 15x P/S multiple (a mature tech company), 2026 revenue would need to reach **40.9bn/15 ≈ 2.73 billion yuan** (160% growth)
- If valued on profitability (assuming profitability within 3 years), 2028 net profit would need to reach **40.9bn/50 ≈ 820 million yuan** (a 50x P/E)

**Conclusion**: the IPO target valuation requires 2026 revenue of at least 2.5-3.0 billion yuan, and requires profitability within 2-3 years. This is not impossible (management's stated target is "multiples of growth"), but the certainty is low.
