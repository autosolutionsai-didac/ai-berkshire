# A Hotel Business That Doesn't Own a Single Hotel

> Understanding Booking series · Part 02 · Business Model
> Reading time: about 10 minutes

## The Underlying Logic of This Business

A small town in Italy, an independent hotel with 30 rooms. Half empty in the off-season, the owner has no money for global marketing and can't afford multilingual customer service staff.

Booking.com shows up. It puts those 30 rooms in front of 200 million monthly active travelers worldwide. The owner doesn't have to lift a finger — just set prices and room availability in the system, and orders flow in automatically. The cost: a commission of **15-18%** on every booking.

Is that expensive? Absolutely. But does this hotel have a choice? Millions of small and midsize hotels around the world don't have one either.

**This is the core of Booking's business model: it doesn't own a single room, yet millions of hotels worldwide can't do without it.**

## Commission Structure: What Justifies 15-18%?

### Comparing Commission Rates Across the Industry

| Platform | Average commission/fee | Charged to |
|------|-------------|-------|
| **Booking.com** | **15-18%** | Hotels |
| **Expedia** | **15-20%** | Hotels |
| **Airbnb** | **3% (host) + 14% (guest)** | Both sides |
| **Trip.com** | **10-15%** | Hotels |

Sources: public policies of each platform, Skift industry reports

Booking's commission rate sits on the medium-to-high end of the industry. Hotels naturally want to pay less — major chains like Marriott and Hilton have kept pushing a "book direct" movement, using loyalty points and best-price guarantees to steer users around the OTAs.

**But the key fact is: large hotel chains account for only about 30% of global accommodation supply. The remaining 70% is independent hotels and alternative accommodations.** These small and midsize suppliers have no brand power, no loyalty program, and no global distribution capability — **30-50%** of their bookings come from Booking.com.

For that 70% of supply, Booking isn't "an optional marketing channel" — it's "a survival necessity." That's the fundamental reason the **15-18%** commission rate holds.

**The counterargument:** The commission rate is not set in stone. The EU is already discussing a cap on OTA commissions. If major markets legislate a 10% commission ceiling, Booking's revenue would take a direct hit. In addition, Google Hotels' free booking links are giving hotels a low-cost alternative acquisition channel, which could erode OTA commission pricing power over the long run.

## From Agency to Merchant: Booking's Most Important Strategic Shift

Understanding Booking's business model requires understanding two key terms: the **agency model** and the **merchant model**.

### Agency Model

The traditional approach: users pick a hotel on Booking, then pay at the hotel's front desk when they arrive. Booking collects commission from the hotel afterward.

- Advantage: high user trust (pay on arrival)
- Disadvantage: Booking doesn't get the payment data, can't control the user experience, and holds no "float"

### Merchant Model

The new approach: users prepay directly through Booking. Booking collects the payment first, then settles with the hotel later.

- Advantages: (1) it captures payment data, laying the groundwork for personalized recommendations; (2) it gains "float" — the user's money sits in Booking's account first; (3) a better user experience (flexible cancellation, locked-in exchange rates, etc.)
- Disadvantage: it takes on more payment risk and handles more refund processing

### Progress of the Transition

| Year | Merchant revenue share | Agency revenue share |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| 2018 | ~30% | ~70% |
| 2022 | ~50% | ~50% |
| 2024 | 60% | 40% |
| **2025** | **66%** | **30%** |

Source: Booking Holdings 10-K annual report

**Over seven years, the merchant revenue share doubled from 30% to 66% — this is Booking's single most important strategic initiative of the past decade.**

Why does it matter? Because the merchant model transforms Booking from "the middleman who helps hotels sell rooms" into "the infrastructure that controls the entire transaction flow." This shift corresponds to:

1. **Deeper data advantage** — Booking knows when you book, how much you spend, your cancellation rate, and whether you bundle in a flight
2. **Float advantage** — the cash sitting idle between prepayment and settlement resembles the model insurance companies use
3. **The technical foundation for Connected Trip** — only by controlling the payment layer can Booking string together lodging + flights + car rental + attraction tickets on a single platform

## Network Effects: Booking's Core Moat

### How the Two-Sided Network Effect Operates

```
More hotels come on board → users get more choice → more users show up
→ hotels get more bookings → more hotels come on board → ... (the flywheel keeps spinning)
```

The strength of this flywheel hinges on one key question: **do users and hotels "multi-home" — that is, use multiple platforms at once?**

The answer: **yes, but only to a limited degree.**

- **On the user side:** travelers do compare prices across Booking, Expedia, and Airbnb. But Booking's advantages are: (1) the broadest coverage; (2) the most complete review system (hundreds of millions of reviews, built up over decades); (3) user preference data that powers increasingly accurate personalized recommendations. That means although users "can" comparison shop, **inertia and data lock-in** still lead most people to default to Booking
- **On the supply side:** independent hotels can theoretically list on multiple platforms at once, but managing availability, pricing, and reviews across several platforms is costly. Many small and midsize hotels effectively treat Booking as their "primary channel" — with **30-50% of bookings** coming from Booking, that level of dependence implies high switching costs

**The counterargument:** Network effects in the travel industry aren't as exclusionary as those in social networks (WeChat, Facebook). Users don't stick with Booking just because their friends are on it — travel decisions hinge mainly on price and supply, not social relationships. That means the moat is wide, but it's not the Great Wall.

### The Review System: A Time Barrier That Can't Be Copied

Booking.com has accumulated **hundreds of millions of user reviews** — this isn't just data, it's trust.

Would you book a foreign hotel you've never visited on a platform with only 5 reviews? Probably not. Would you book on one with 2,000 reviews and an 8.7 rating? Probably yes.

**The value of reviews is a function of time — they can't be bought, and they can't be caught up on quickly.** Even with unlimited money and traffic, Google can't replicate in a short time the review system Booking has built up over twenty years.

## Financial Quality: Why Booking Is a "Money-Printing Machine"

### Margin Comparison

| Metric | Booking (2025) | Trip.com (2025) | Expedia (2025) | Airbnb (2025) |
|------|---------------|------------|----------------|--------------|
| Gross margin | **87.4%** | ~80% | ~60% | ~80% |
| Operating margin | **32.8%** | ~25% | ~12% | ~22% |
| Free cash flow margin | **33.8%** | ~25% | ~15% | ~35% |
| ROIC | **43.7%** | ~15-20% | ~10% | ~30% |

Sources: company filings, GuruFocus

**An 87.4% gross margin means: for every $100 of revenue Booking brings in, only $12.60 is cost** — mostly payment-processing fees and a small amount of infrastructure expense. The remaining $87.40 is all gross profit.

Why so high? Because Booking owns no physical assets at all — it doesn't build hotels, doesn't operate a fleet, doesn't manage room cleaning. All the "heavy" work is borne by suppliers; Booking only handles "connecting" and "matching."

**An ROIC of 43.7% means: for every $1 of capital invested, Booking earns back nearly 44 cents in profit.** The industry median is only 3.6%. This is the product of a triple advantage stacking together — asset-light operations, two-sided network effects, and economies of scale.

### The Special Edge of Free Cash Flow

Booking's free cash flow margin (33.8%) has long exceeded its net margin (20.1%), thanks to the **working-capital advantage** created by the merchant model:

- Users prepay → the cash lands in Booking's account first
- Booking settles with the hotel only after the guest checks in
- The gap in between generates "float"

**This model closely resembles insurance companies — collect the cash first, deliver the service later.** That means Booking's real cash-generating power is stronger than what the income statement shows.

## Connected Trip: From "Booking Tool" to "Travel Operating System"

CEO Glenn Fogel's Connected Trip strategy is, at its core, about **raising each user's spend and usage frequency.**

### 2025 Progress

| Category | 2025 performance | YoY growth |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Accommodation | 1.235 billion room-nights | Core business |
| Flights | 68 million tickets, total gross bookings of **$16.8 billion** | **+37%** |
| Attraction tickets | Specific figures not disclosed | **~+80%** |
| Multi-category cross-bookings | — | **+20%+** |

Sources: Booking Holdings 10-K annual report, AInvest analysis

**AI is the accelerant for Connected Trip.** Booking's AI Trip Planner is already live in more than 10 countries, and Connected Trip transaction volume grew **30%** year-over-year in the second quarter of 2025 thanks to AI integration.

**If this actually succeeds, what does it mean?** Users shift from "only booking a hotel on Booking each trip" to "completing the entire journey on Booking" — average order value could double or even triple, user stickiness would rise substantially, and it would become much harder for competitors to break in.

**The counterargument:** Connected Trip sounds great, but flights are a low-margin business (margins far below lodging), and the attraction-ticket market is highly fragmented. Whether Booking can build the same kind of competitive edge in categories beyond lodging remains unproven. Management treats Connected Trip as a future growth engine, but its profit contribution so far is limited — investors need to distinguish "strategic vision" from "results already delivered."

## This Business in One Sentence

**Booking's business, at its core, is this: using algorithms and data to efficiently match global travelers with accommodation suppliers, taking a 15-18% commission on every transaction. It owns no physical assets, yet millions of hotels worldwide depend on it for customer acquisition. Two-sided network effects and a review system built up over decades form a moat that is wide but not monopolistic.**

What's good about this business? Asset-light, high-margin, strong cash flow, and network effects that keep compounding.

Where are the hidden risks? The moat is wide but not exclusive — users can compare prices, and hotels can list on multiple platforms. More importantly, if the search gateway gets reshaped by AI, Booking's value as an "information-aggregation middle layer" could be compressed.
