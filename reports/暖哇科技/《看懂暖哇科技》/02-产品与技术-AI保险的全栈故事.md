## What does Nuanwa actually do?

Every day, an insurer has to make decisions like: should we sell this stranger a policy, at what price, and when they file a claim, are they trying to cheat us — and how much should we pay out? These decisions used to be made manually. Now Nuanwa Technology wants AI to replace that work — or at least dramatically improve its efficiency.

Break the insurance business down into its two simplest stages: **underwriting** (everything before the policy is sold) and **claims** (everything after). Nuanwa Technology builds AI tools for insurers around exactly these two stages.

### Two product lines

| Product line | Share of revenue | Core functions | Flagship system |
|--------|-----------|---------|---------|
| **AI Underwriting Solutions** | ~**75%** | Product design, precision marketing, intelligent underwriting, user operations | The "Alamos" underwriting system |
| **AI Claims Solutions** | ~**25%** | Intelligent claims review, anti-fraud detection, health management | The "Lop Nur" claims system |

Source: Nuanwa Technology's prospectus

In plain terms:

- **Underwriting side**: helps insurers design insurance products, find the right customers, and decide whether to underwrite a policy. For example, when someone applies for health insurance, Nuanwa's system helps the insurer quickly assess that person's health risk and decide whether to sell them a policy and at what price.
- **Claims side**: when a customer files a claim, Nuanwa's system helps the insurer review the claim, detect potential fraud, and automate the handling of straightforward cases.

## Breaking down the seven core technology products

Nuanwa's prospectus lists seven core technology products. Here's a read on each:

| Product | What it does | Moat assessment |
|------|--------|---------|
| **Medical knowledge base** | Structures medical literature, disease-diagnosis rules, drug catalogues, and the like into a "knowledge foundation" for AI decisions | Moderate — requires extensive annotation by medical experts, but is not impossible to replicate |
| **Medical Data Platform (MDP)** | Integrates multi-source medical data to provide training data for risk-control models | Moderate-to-high — data volume and cleaning quality are the moat |
| **Commercial insurance risk control** | Scores applicants for risk to support underwriting decisions | Moderate — model effectiveness depends on accumulated historical data |
| **Commercial insurance anti-fraud platform** | Identifies claims fraud (fake claims, over-treatment, etc.) | Moderate-to-high — anti-fraud rules and anomaly-detection models require training on large volumes of real cases |
| **Medical services platform** | Connects to medical institutions, providing care navigation and health-management services | Low — a platform model with many competitors |
| **Customized insurance services platform** | Builds custom product solutions for insurers | Low — essentially consulting plus development, highly substitutable |
| **AI medical services platform** | AI-assisted treatment recommendations and chronic-disease management | Moderate — moderate technical barrier, but heavy regulatory constraints |

**In one line**: Nuanwa's technological core sits in three areas — **the medical knowledge base, the medical data platform, and the risk-control / anti-fraud models**. The other four are more service- or platform-oriented and carry a lower moat.

## How deep is the core technology moat, really?

This is the question most worth scrutinizing. Nuanwa claims to be "China's largest independent AI technology company in the insurance industry" — does that moat hold up?

### Moat one: data accumulation

By the end of 2025, Nuanwa's systems had processed more than **221 million** insurance cases cumulatively, served more than **44 million** insurance consumers, and facilitated cumulative first-year premiums of **RMB 13 billion**.

That scale of data genuinely leads the industry. An AI model's effectiveness depends heavily on training data — a model trained on 200 million cases should, in theory, be more accurate than one trained on 20 million.

**But there's a fatal problem: who owns the data?**

The raw ownership of insurance case data belongs to the **insurers**, not to Nuanwa. What Nuanwa can accumulate through the process is **model-training experience** and **de-identified statistical features** — but it cannot take the raw data with it.

**Counter-argument**: even if the raw data belongs to the insurers, the risk-control rules, anti-fraud models, and medical knowledge graphs that Nuanwa has distilled from 221 million cases are "experience assets" that hold real value and would be hard for competitors to replicate quickly. Analogy: Accenture doesn't own its clients' data either, but its industry experience and methodology are exactly its moat.

### Moat two: system switching costs

Once an insurer plugs into Nuanwa's underwriting or claims system, switching to another vendor is costly — system integration, data migration, staff retraining, and the risk of business disruption. This is classic **B2B SaaS stickiness**.

Nuanwa's data supports this:
- 2025 policy renewal rate of **98.4%**
- Cross-selling rate of **65.0%** (industry average 15%-25%, per the prospectus citing Frost & Sullivan)

**But here's the contradiction**: if system stickiness is really this high, why is the customer retention rate only **69.6%** — and falling?

One possible explanation: large customers are highly sticky (the system is deeply embedded and not easily replaced), but **small and trial customers churn at a high rate** (they decide after trying it that it's not worth paying for long-term). Of Nuanwa's 245 customers, the ones actually contributing core revenue may be just a few dozen at the top.

### Moat three: industry understanding

Insurance is not an industry that general-purpose technology can easily break into. Underwriting rules, medical-diagnosis logic, claims-review standards — all of this requires **deep industry knowledge**. Nuanwa was incubated out of ZhongAn Insurance's health-business unit, and its founding team comes from the insurance and insurtech industry — that "genetic" background is a genuine advantage.

CEO Lu Min's résumé: eBaoTech (insurance core systems) -> Alibaba Cloud -> General Manager of the IT division at the Shanghai Insurance Exchange -> founder of Nuanwa Technology. Twenty years of experience in insurance technology.

**But flip it around**: large insurers (Ping An, CPIC, PICC) all have their own technology subsidiaries, with talent pools and financial resources far exceeding Nuanwa's. The fact that they don't use Nuanwa may simply reflect a current judgment that "outsourcing is cheaper than building in-house" — once AI technology matures further and the barrier to entry drops further, the cost-benefit calculus for building in-house could flip.

## Could general-purpose large language models disrupt Nuanwa?

This is the hottest debate in insurtech in 2025-2026.

As general-purpose large language models like GPT, Ernie Bot, and Qwen rapidly improve, a question is becoming sharper: **do insurers still need a vertical AI vendor like Nuanwa? Why not just use a general-purpose model directly?**

| Dimension | General-purpose LLMs | Vertical AI like Nuanwa |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| Language understanding | Strong | Moderate |
| Medical knowledge | Broad but not deep | Narrow but deeper |
| Insurance business logic | Weak (needs heavy customization) | Strong (natively embedded) |
| Compliance review | Weak | Strong |
| Deployment model | Mostly cloud-based | Can be deployed on-premise |
| Data security | A major concern for insurers | Trust already established |

**Short term (1-2 years)**: general-purpose LLMs still cannot replace Nuanwa. The complexity of insurance business, compliance requirements, and sensitivity around data security mean insurers won't simply "use ChatGPT to underwrite."

**Medium term (3-5 years)**: the risk grows. The path of "general-purpose LLM plus insurance-industry fine-tuning" is maturing, and large insurers' technology subsidiaries may build their own capabilities on top of foundation models, squeezing Nuanwa's room to survive.

**Counter-argument**: large models might actually help Nuanwa. Nuanwa can integrate large-model capabilities into its own vertical products to strengthen them. The combination of "foundation model + vertical industry data + industry experience" could be more competitive than either a purely general-purpose model or a purely vertical one.

As of the end of 2025, Nuanwa Technology's customer profile:

| Customer type | Count | Characteristics |
|---------|------|------|
| Insurance companies | **115** | Covers 9 of the top ten insurers |
| Internet platforms and others | **130** | Traffic platforms, health-management companies, etc. |
| **Total** | **245** | |

Source: Nuanwa Technology's updated 2026 prospectus

**Change in top-five-customer revenue share**:

| Year | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | H1 2025 |
|------|------|------|------|--------|
| Top-five customer share | 92.3% | 82.9% | 78.9% | 73.6% |

Source: Nuanwa Technology's prospectus

Customer concentration is falling, but **73.6%** is still high. By comparison, at typical listed SaaS companies the top five customers usually account for **20%-40%** of revenue. Nuanwa's customer concentration is **2-3 times** the normal industry level.

**Key judgment**: Nuanwa's customer base is expanding, but core revenue is still highly concentrated in a small number of large customers. This is not a product that has been "broadly embraced by the market" — it looks more like a pattern of "a handful of large customers using it deeply, plus a large number of small customers just dipping a toe in."

## This article's core conclusions

| Dimension | Judgment | Confidence |
|------|------|--------|
| Technology moat | Present, but not deep — the core moat lies in data accumulation and industry experience, not irreplaceable technology | Moderate |
| Product capability | Full-stack coverage is a differentiator, but no single capability stands out | Moderate |
| Customer stickiness | High stickiness among large customers, fast churn among small ones — a polarized picture | Fairly high |
| Impact of large models | Limited near term, a real threat over the medium-to-long term | Moderate |
| Data assets | Holds experience value, but the raw data doesn't belong to Nuanwa | Fairly high |

**In one line**: Nuanwa's technology moat isn't "unscalable" — it's "a real barrier, but one that will erode over time." Its true moat isn't the technology itself but **the switching costs created by the complexity of the insurance industry**. And that moat is being eroded by two forces: general-purpose large models lowering the technical barrier, and large insurers' growing willingness to build capabilities in-house.
