# Liu Junning: Less Is More — The Wisdom of Concentrated Investing

Source: Liu Junning, *Investment Philosophy: The Lamp of Conservative Wisdom*
Reprinted from Sina Blog [original link](https://blog.sina.com.cn/s/blog_4462623d0102e2m7.html)

---

There are two seemingly paradoxical four-character maxims about the philosophy of value investing. One is "slow is fast" — meaning that haste makes waste, and that by waiting slowly one earns fast returns; I discussed this in "The Role of Time in Value Investing." The other is "less is more," meaning that quality of investment outweighs quantity of investment, which is the subject of this article. "Less is more" addresses the question of concentration versus diversification in investing.

What is called concentrated investing — or, more precisely, focus investing — means directing an investor's limited intelligence, energy, and capital into a small number of undervalued, high-quality assets in order to generate greater returns. The most authoritative advocate of concentrated investing was Mr. Fisher, an important founder of the conservative investment philosophy and the author of *Conservative Investors Sleep Well*. He once said: "Investors have been overly indoctrinated with the importance of diversification. Yet the fear of putting too many eggs in one basket leads them to buy too little of the stocks of companies they know well, and too much of the stocks of companies they do not understand at all. It seems they never consider that buying a company's stock without sufficiently understanding that company may be far more dangerous than insufficient diversification." Fisher's investment philosophy was: less means more. And the track records of Buffett and others have provided powerful empirical validation for the "less is more" philosophy of concentrated investing.

Should you put your eggs in one basket and watch over it carefully, or spread your eggs across different baskets to avoid losing everything at once? In other words, should you concentrate or diversify? The answer to this question lies not in how to concentrate or how to diversify, but in how one understands concentration and diversification in the first place. Institutional investors, especially fund portfolios, are subject to certain industry rules requiring their portfolios to diversify appropriately across market risk, sector risk, company risk, and even country risk. We set those industry requirements aside here. What concerns us in this piece is concentrated versus diversified investing in the absence of any such industry-rule constraints.

Two phenomena are often overlooked. The first: taking securities investing as an example, most investors start out diversifying — putting a small amount of money into a large number of stocks. Even today's universally recognized masters of securities investing all diversified when they were starting out. It is only that some of them gradually shifted from diversified investing to concentrated investing. Seen this way, concentrated investing and diversified investing are often a sequence rather than a binary choice.

The second phenomenon is that professional investors, alongside their institutional investment activities, all maintain their own small personal investments — whether in stocks, real estate, or collectibles. So privately, they too are retail investors. If you sort and categorize their private investments as a whole, in the broad sense they are all diversified investors. No one concentrates their entire personal wealth into just one or two holdings. The moment a person owns more than one property, or holds even a single share in different securities markets, their investments are already diversified. If an investor holds one stock each in the Shanghai and Shenzhen A-share and B-share markets, the SME board and the ChiNext board, and the Hong Kong and U.S. stock markets, they already hold five or more stocks.

We can see that diversification is a natural tendency. Unless one deliberately concentrates, investing is, as a matter of course, usually diversified. Diversified investing happens naturally, whereas concentrated investing must be learned. Excessive diversification is already the default state; relative concentration is the goal worth pursuing.

Why concentrate your investments? Because everyone is ignorant to some degree, and everyone's circle of competence is limited. The essence of concentrated investing is not simply a matter of the number of investment targets, but a matter of focus. When asked what was most critical to his success, Buffett answered: "focus" — "We only focus on a few outstanding companies. We are focused investors." The principle behind concentrated investing is the principle of a convex lens: gathering limited sunlight and focusing it onto a single point to produce the expected heat and effect. Without focus, you cannot reach the expected temperature.

A focused person is often able to concentrate their time, energy, and wisdom on the task at hand, thereby maximizing initiative, proactivity, and creativity in pursuit of their goals. Concentration and focus apply not only to investors but also to entrepreneurs, and indeed to anyone pursuing great achievements. The most successful entrepreneurs invariably concentrate their energy, talent, and resources on a single enterprise and achieve major success — think of Gates and Microsoft, Jobs and Apple, or Dell and Dell Computer.

The hallmark of concentrated investing is keeping the winners — continuously screening down to a small number of exceptionally outstanding assets. Diversified investing, by contrast, is often associated with a strange phenomenon in securities investing: "cashing out on the winners," or in other words, cutting and running the moment things look good. Among a basket of stocks, diversified investors tend to sell first the ones that have already done well (say, up 30%). If this approach is followed consistently, the inevitable result is: sell the winners, keep the losers — pull out the flowers and leave the weeds — until none of the stocks are doing well anymore.

A diversified investor may be lucky enough to buy into a stock that goes up tenfold, but is likely to have bought only 100 shares of it — and to have sold it once it was up 30%. How one treats the securities in hand is like how a card player treats the cards in their hand. A poor player carelessly discards their good cards, leaving themselves with a hand of bad ones; a good player works to discard the bad cards, keeping a few good ones in reserve for the crucial moment. Diversified investing tends to keep the inferior and discard the superior; concentrated investing keeps the superior and discards the inferior. Clearly, concentrated investing operates on a higher plane than diversified investing.

The philosophy of concentrated investing is especially closely tied to the concept of the circle of competence. Concentrated investing means confining your investments to within your own circle of competence, investing only in what you understand. The source of safety is understanding of the investment target, not the variety or quantity of investments — diversification cannot compensate for a lack of understanding of the investment target. When your financial advisor tells you to diversify, they are also protecting themselves — because they, too, do not know where the best place to put your money is. Investors should therefore protect themselves by deepening their understanding of their investment targets, not by increasing the number of holdings in their hands.

Advocates of concentrated and diversified investing alike share one prior consensus: no one can predict or control the future. A hurricane, an earthquake, a new policy, a technological advance, a product recall — any of these can easily cut the value of your holdings in half. Diversified investors believe that, because the future cannot be predicted, one must diversify. Concentrated investors believe that, because the future cannot be predicted, one must concentrate. But diversification does not eliminate risk — it merely trades one set of risks for another.

Both concentrated and diversified investing have their own undesirable extremes. The extreme of concentrated investing is betting everything on a single throw of the dice. The moment someone hears "concentrate your investments," they thoughtlessly pour all their capital into a single investment they do not even understand — that is speculation and gambling, not investing. The extreme of diversified investing is scattering pepper flakes: spreading limited capital across countless investment targets, so that broad sowing inevitably yields a meager harvest, or even no harvest at all.

Excessive diversification, in turn, produces meager returns — this is "more is less." Whereas if you sow your limited seeds on a few carefully chosen and carefully cultivated plots of land, you instead reap an abundant harvest. This is "less is more." Scattering pepper flakes is easy, and betting everything on one throw is easy too — both can be done thoughtlessly, without a second thought. But if diversified investing avoids scattering pepper flakes and broad sowing for meager returns, and if concentrated investing avoids betting everything on a single throw, then concentration and diversification cease to be a dilemma one must choose between.
