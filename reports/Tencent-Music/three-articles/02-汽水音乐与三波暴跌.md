# Qishui Music and the Three Waves of Crashes: What Is the Market Afraid Of?

> Reading time: about 10 minutes
> This is the second piece in a three-part Tencent Music series

---

## The 68% Decline Didn't Happen in a Single Day

In the previous piece we saw a contradiction: Tencent Music's profit grew 25%, yet its share price fell 68%.

The market hasn't lost its mind — it is pricing in a specific fear. To understand that fear, we need to go back to the stock chart and break the 68% decline down into three concentrated crashes. Behind each crash is a specific catalyst.

---

## First Wave: November 2025, Down 25%

**Trigger**: The Q3 earnings release, combined with data showing Qishui Music's monthly active users had doubled

In November 2025, Tencent Music released its Q3 earnings. The results themselves weren't bad — both revenue and profit were growing. But the market's attention was pulled toward a different number:

**Qishui Music's monthly active users (MAU) reached 120 million, doubling year-over-year.**

What unsettled investors even more was a new term — **"straddling users."** Analysts found in their research that more than **21 million** users were using both Tencent Music and Qishui Music at the same time, and this group was spending more and more time on Qishui.

This was an ominous signal. Users weren't uninstalling QQ Music outright — they were straddling both apps, gradually shifting their usage habits over to Qishui.

The market began reassessing a risk it had previously overlooked: **ByteDance was serious about this.**

Qishui Music was no longer a side feature of Douyin — it had become an independent, fast-growing competitor.

---

## Second Wave: March 17, 2026, Down 24.65% in a Single Day

**Trigger**: Three pieces of bad news detonating on the same day

This was the most brutal day. **The single-day decline of 24.65%** set a record for the largest single-day drop since Tencent Music's IPO.

Three "bombs" landed at once:

**Bomb one: "Padded" profit exposed**

After the Q4 and full-year 2025 results came out, analysts discovered that full-year GAAP net profit included a **RMB 2.37 billion deemed-disposal gain on its Universal Music Group equity stake** (booked in Q1 2025). This was a one-off paper gain — book profit arising from a reclassification of the investment, unrelated to the core business. Although Non-GAAP net profit had already excluded this gain, the "impressive" headline figure of GAAP net profit growing 66% year-over-year still misled some investors. The market felt it had been "played."

**Bomb two: Announcement that user-data disclosure would stop**

Starting with Q1 2026, Tencent Music will no longer disclose MAU and paying-user figures in its earnings reports.

This was **the most fatal blow**.

The reasoning is simple: **a company only chooses to stop disclosing data when that data is getting worse.**

If MAU were growing and paying users were accelerating, what management team would voluntarily hide its most flattering numbers? The decision to stop disclosing is itself a signal — it implies management expects these metrics' trends will not improve, and would rather remove them altogether than be grilled by analysts every quarter.

**Bomb three: Paying-user growth hits a new low**

In the "final quarter" before disclosure stopped, paying-user growth came in at just **5.3%** year-over-year — a record low since the IPO. This all but confirmed the market's biggest worry: the growth engine was decelerating.

**Market reaction**: Three major investment banks (Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Citigroup) published downgrade reports on the same day. Sell-side analysts were nearly unanimous that halting user-data disclosure marked the end of the growth narrative.

## Third Wave: May 2026, Down 30%

**Trigger**: Conditional approval of the Ximalaya deal + Q1 results missing expectations + rising defensive costs

The third wave was a stacking of several negative factors:

**1. Ximalaya approved, but it amounts to "hands tied"**

Antitrust approval came through on May 12, but five conditions attached (no price increases, no exclusivity, no bundling, no restrictions on hosts, and free content must be maintained) reset the market's expectations for synergies to zero. $2.9 billion had been spent, with no visible path to a return in the near term.

**2. Q1 Non-GAAP earnings per share (EPS) missed expectations by $0.02**

The miss was small, but with market sentiment already extremely fragile, even a one-cent shortfall was seized on and amplified.

**3. Marketing expenses surged 36% year-over-year**

Q1 marketing expenses rose sharply, which management attributed to "investment in brand promotion and user acquisition." But the market's translation was: **to defend against Qishui Music's encroachment, Tencent Music has to spend more just to hold on to its users.**

Rising defensive costs are a dangerous signal — they mean competition is intensifying, and the rival doesn't need to spend the same amount to compete (Qishui's users come from Douyin's traffic referrals, at close to zero acquisition cost).

**4. Spotify confirms a €975 million loss on its Tencent Music stake**

Spotify holds roughly a 9% stake in Tencent Music. In its own earnings report, it confirmed a **€975 million** investment loss. Although this is only a paper loss, when even your strategic shareholder is writing down your value sharply on its own books, it's hard for market sentiment not to be affected.

## Qishui Music: Just How Serious a Threat Is It?

Behind all three waves of selling lies one common shadow: **Qishui Music**.

It deserves a full section of its own.

### The fundamentals

| Metric | Qishui Music | Tencent Music (QQ Music + Kugou + Kuwo) |
|------|---------|--------------------------|
| MAU (March 2026) | **156 million** | ~528 million (last figure disclosed before reporting stopped) |
| Industry rank | **No. 3** (has surpassed NetEase Cloud Music) | No. 1 |
| Monthly subscription price | **RMB 8** | ~RMB 15-18 |
| Catalog size | ~50 million tracks | **260 million tracks** |
| User source | 82% referred from Douyin | Proprietary traffic + WeChat ecosystem |
| Monetization model | **Primarily advertising** | **Primarily subscription** |

The key takeaway from this table: Qishui Music and Tencent Music are **not the same kind of business**.

Tencent Music's business model is "get users to pay" — subscription memberships contribute more than 80% of revenue. Qishui Music's model is "get users to watch ads" — free listening, ads inserted, supplemented by a low-priced monthly card.

This mirrors the logic of the video industry: iQIYI relies on membership revenue, Douyin relies on advertising revenue. The two models can coexist, but on the dimension of user attention, they are playing a zero-sum game.

### Qishui's core edge: zero-cost user acquisition

The most frightening thing about Qishui Music isn't its RMB 8 monthly card — it's that **it doesn't need to spend money to acquire users.**

**82% of its users are funneled directly from Douyin.**

A user scrolling through Douyin hears a catchy piece of background music, taps "Listen to the full version on Qishui Music," and a zero-cost acquisition is complete. Douyin has more than 700 million daily active users, and the efficiency of this referral pipeline far exceeds any traditional marketing channel.

By contrast, Tencent Music's Q1 marketing expenses rose 36% year-over-year just to barely hold its user base steady. One side acquires users at zero cost while the other spends more and more — this is exactly why the market is anxious.

### Qishui's weaknesses: catalog and use cases

But Qishui Music also has clear weaknesses:

**1. A huge catalog gap**: 50 million tracks versus 260 million tracks — a **5x** difference. This doesn't matter to casual, light listeners, but for music enthusiasts, not being able to find the song they want is fatal.

**2. No premium-tier benefits**: Tencent Music's SVIP tier offers differentiated experiences such as lossless audio and master-tape-grade sound quality, and Qishui currently has no comparable product.

**3. Lacks social integration**: QQ Music is deeply integrated with WeChat — you can display the song you're currently listening to in your WeChat status, or share playlists on Moments. That kind of social stickiness cannot be broken by a price war.

**4. Monetization remains unproven**: Whether Qishui's ad-driven monetization model can sustain long-term development is still an open question. The advertising-monetization efficiency of music streaming is far lower than that of short video, and even Spotify's ad-supported free tier carries much lower margins than its paid tier.

## Declining MAU: The "Chronic Illness" That Stopped Being Disclosed

The threat from Qishui Music is compounding on top of a long-standing problem of Tencent Music's own: **its monthly active users have been steadily eroding.**

| Period | MAU | Notes |
|---------|----------|------|
| Q2 2021 | **636 million** | Historical peak |
| Q1 2023 | 593 million → 602 million | Methodology change; IoT (Internet of Things) devices added |
| Q4 2024 | 572 million | |
| Q4 2025 | 528 million | Last figure disclosed before reporting stopped |

Starting in the second half of 2021, Tencent Music's monthly active users have been in continuous decline, with a cumulative loss of more than **100 million users**.

The Q1 2023 methodology change, which folded in-car speakers, smart speakers, and other IoT (Internet of Things) devices into the count, produced a brief jump in the number, but it did not change the underlying trend.

By Q1 2026, the company simply stopped disclosing this figure altogether.

**A company does not hide data when it's improving.** The decision to stop disclosing all but confirms that MAU is still declining, and that management believes the trend won't reverse in the near term.

But a key question needs to be separated out: **which users are being lost?**

If the users being lost are free or light users (people who listen to a few songs a month and then leave), the revenue impact is limited. In fact, the paying-user count was still growing in Q4 2025 (even though growth had slowed to 5.3%), suggesting the core paying-user base has not yet been meaningfully eroded.

The counterargument is: today's free users are tomorrow's paying users. Losing free users means the "reservoir" for paid conversion is shrinking. Once that reservoir shrinks past a certain point, the paying-user count will eventually peak too.

## Qishui vs. Tencent Music: What Is the Real Relationship?

Putting the analysis above together, the threat Qishui Music poses to Tencent Music can be summarized in three layers:

**Layer one: capturing incremental users.** For the newest generation of young users (Gen Z, those born after 2000), the entry point into music is no longer QQ Music but Douyin. They naturally flow toward Qishui Music. Tencent Music's ability to acquire new users is declining.

**Layer two: pressing down the paying ceiling.** When you can listen to music for RMB 8, an RMB 18 price point starts to look expensive. Even though Qishui's catalog is far smaller than Tencent's, "good enough" users will choose the cheaper option. This constrains Tencent Music's room to raise prices.

**Layer three: accelerating light-user churn.** Users who were never that willing to pay in the first place, and stuck with QQ Music only out of habit, now have a stronger incentive to switch once a cheaper — or even free — alternative exists.

**But Qishui cannot replace Tencent Music in the near term.** A 5x catalog gap is a hard weakness, premium-tier benefits are unmatched, and WeChat's social integration cannot be replicated quickly. For core users already accustomed to a 260-million-track catalog and lossless audio, Qishui simply isn't an option.

## So What Exactly Is the Market Afraid Of?

Putting the three waves of selling together with the analysis of Qishui Music, the market's fear becomes clear:

**It's not a fear that Tencent Music will collapse tomorrow — it's a fear that the "chronic illness" will worsen.**

Specifically:

1. **The core user base is shrinking**, and by halting disclosure the company has made it impossible for the market to gauge how fast that shrinkage is happening

2. **The competitor's user-acquisition cost is approaching zero**, while Tencent Music's own defensive costs are rising

3. **Synergies from the Ximalaya acquisition have been blocked by regulatory conditions**, with no visible return in the near term on the $2.9 billion invested

4. **The price-increase thesis is being suppressed by Qishui's low-price strategy**, and the assumption of rising SVIP penetration may be too optimistic

None of these is fatal on its own. But taken together, they form a narrative that makes the market deeply uncomfortable: **Tencent Music's moat may be slowly eroding, and management doesn't appear to have a particularly effective response.**

To sum it up in one sentence: **Qishui Music is Tencent Music's chronic illness, not an acute emergency. But what the share price reflects is the market's fear of whether that chronic illness will progress into something terminal.**

Is this fear excessive?

Consider the question from a different angle: **if you bought Tencent Music's entire business today for 58.5 billion yuan (net of cash), you would collect 9.8 billion yuan of free cash flow every year — a 6-year payback period. And the margins on this machine are still rising.**

Is this deal a "steal" or a "trap"? How much could you lose in the worst case, and how much could you make in the best case?
