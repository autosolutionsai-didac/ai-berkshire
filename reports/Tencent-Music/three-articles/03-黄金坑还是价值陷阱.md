# Tencent Music at $8.47: golden pit or value trap?

> Reading time: ~7 minutes
> This is the third and final piece in a three-part Tencent Music series.

---

## First, let's answer one question: just how cheap is it?

The first two pieces covered what Tencent Music is and what the market is afraid of. This final piece takes on the question everyone really wants answered: **is $8.47 worth paying?**

Let's lay the valuation out first, cross-checked with three methods:

| Valuation metric | Value | Meaning |
|---------|------|------|
| PE (price-to-earnings) | **~10x** | The market is willing to pay $10 for every $1 of profit |
| Cash-adjusted PE | **~6x** | The real valuation after stripping out RMB 34.5 billion of net cash |
| EV/FCF (enterprise value / free cash flow) | **~6x** | If you bought the whole company outright, payback in about 6 years |
| FCF yield (free cash flow yield) | **~17%** | Each year the business "earns back" 17% of enterprise value |

All four metrics point to the same conclusion: **by the numbers, this company is very cheap.**

A 17% free cash flow (FCF, meaning the cash a business generates from operations, net of capital expenditure, that's freely available to management) yield means that if you could buy the entire company at the current price, without any improvement, the cash it generates each year alone would pay you back in under 6 years.

For comparison: the S&P 500's average FCF yield is roughly 4-5%. Tencent Music's is 3-4 times that.

## A head-to-head comparison with Spotify

Since both are music streaming companies, a comparison with the world's largest peer is unavoidable:

| Metric | Tencent Music | Spotify |
|------|---------|---------|
| Gross margin | **44.9%** | 33.2% |
| PE | **~10x** | ~37x |
| Paying users | 127 million | 293 million |
| 2025 revenue growth | ~16% | ~15% |
| Market cap | ~$13.0 billion | ~$89.0 billion |

Two comparisons in this table stand out.

**First: gross margin.** Tencent Music's gross margin of **44.9%** is nearly 12 percentage points higher than Spotify's 33.2%. That means for every dollar of revenue Tencent Music earns, it keeps more profit. On profitability quality, Tencent Music is the stronger business.

**Second: valuation multiples.** Spotify's PE is 37x, Tencent Music's is 10x — **Spotify is 3-4 times more expensive.**

Of course, Spotify's premium has reasonable justifications: stronger user-growth momentum, no local competitor like Qishui Music, and no exposure to Chinese-ADR policy risk.

But the question is: do these differences justify a **3-4x valuation gap**?

If you believe a reasonable premium is 1.5-2x (accounting for competitive risk plus a China-market discount), then Tencent Music's current valuation is still too low. If you believe the Chinese-ADR discount plus competitive risk justifies a 3-4x discount, then the current valuation is fair.

There's no universally correct answer to this — it depends on how you price the risk of China's internet sector.

## Three layers of margin of safety

Even taking a cautious view of the fundamentals, Tencent Music offers three layers of margin of safety at the current price:

**Layer one: RMB 34.5 billion in net cash.** That's 37% of market cap. It means that even if the operating business were worth zero (an extreme assumption), you'd only lose 63%. Cash is the hardest floor there is.

**Layer two: ~RMB 9.8 billion of FCF generated every year.** The company doesn't need outside financing — it generates nearly ten billion in cash on its own each year. As long as the business doesn't suffer a cliff-edge decline, this cash-flow engine keeps running.

**Layer three: the business fundamentals are still improving.** Subscription revenue's share is rising, ARPPU (average monthly revenue per paying user) is growing, and gross margin is expanding — none of these trends has reversed so far.

---

## The Q1 profit "halving" is an illusion

If you saw Q1 IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) net profit of **RMB 2.09 billion, down 51% year-over-year**, don't panic just yet.

That "halving" is entirely due to a **RMB 2.37 billion one-off gain in the year-ago Q1** — a paper gain from the deemed disposal of Universal Music Group's equity stake (a reclassification of the investment, not an actual sale). That gain has nothing to do with Tencent Music's music-subscription business.

Strip out that distortion, and adjusted net profit was RMB 2.33 billion, up **4.8%** year-over-year.

Not spectacular, but certainly not a "halving."

There's a lesson here: when reading a Chinese internet company's results, **always separate GAAP from Non-GAAP, and recurring items from one-off items.** Headline writers love using swings in GAAP profit to manufacture panic, but the actual underlying operations are usually far steadier.

## What would constitute a "value trap"?

Cheap doesn't equal good. **The real risk isn't "is it expensive now" but "will things get worse from here."**

If the following three things happen **simultaneously**, the current $8.47 is still not safe:

**1. Qishui Music succeeds at large-scale commercialization**

If ByteDance decides to push hard on paid subscriptions for Qishui (say, launching a RMB 15 membership tier plus a much bigger catalog) rather than continuing to treat it as Douyin's copyright hub and traffic product, Tencent Music's paying users and ARPPU would both face a direct hit.

For now, ByteDance still seems to position Qishui as an extension of the Douyin ecosystem rather than a standalone profit center. But that assessment could change at any time.

**2. The Ximalaya integration fails**

A USD 2.9 billion (roughly RMB 18.6 billion, cash plus stock) acquisition that fails to produce meaningful synergies would be a massive capital misallocation. The cash portion — roughly RMB 9.1 billion (USD 1.26 billion) — would directly consume cash reserves and weaken the company's financial flexibility and buyback capacity.

**3. Chinese ADRs face extreme policy risk**

If further deterioration in US-China relations forces Chinese ADRs to delist or face tight trading restrictions, the valuation discount would widen further rather than narrow.

**The probability of all three happening simultaneously isn't high, but it isn't zero either.** If you judge that more than two of these three risks will materialize, the current price may still not be cheap enough.

---

## What would constitute a "golden pit"?

Conversely, if the following conditions are met, the current price will look like a clear low point in hindsight:

**1. Paid-penetration breaks above 30%, SVIP penetration reaches 25%**

Currently paid-penetration is about 24% and SVIP penetration is 16%. If these rise to 30% and 25% respectively, subscription revenue would see significantly nonlinear growth. That means revenue and profit could keep growing even as total users decline.

**2. Qishui Music's commercialization stalls**

If ByteDance continues to position Qishui as Douyin's copyright hub and a user-retention tool rather than a standalone profit-making product, then Qishui's threat to Tencent Music is "diverting user attention" rather than "replacing the business model" — the former is a chronic drag, the latter would be a fatal blow.

**3. The Ximalaya integration makes real progress**

After the two-year restriction period (around 2028), if music-plus-audio membership bundling succeeds, user retention improves, and ARPU (average revenue per user) rises through cross-selling, the USD 2.9 billion acquisition will have proven its worth.

---

## The single most important signal

Above all the complex analysis, there is one metric that matters more than any financial figure:

**Whether monthly active users stabilize or recover.**

This is the metric the market is most anxious about. Since the second half of 2021, cumulative MAU losses have exceeded 100 million — a number large enough that the market has reason to suspect this isn't cyclical fluctuation but structural decline.

Once MAU stabilizes — even without growth, just no further decline — **the narrative of "users are continuously leaving" would be broken.**

And a shift in narrative often moves faster and more dramatically in capital markets than an improvement in fundamentals. A quarter of "MAU flat sequentially" data might drive more of a stock-price recovery than a quarter of "profit up 20%."

The problem is: the company has already stopped disclosing MAU data.

That makes verification harder, and makes fear harder to dispel. Unless management chooses to resume disclosure at some point (which usually signals improving numbers), or a credible third-party data source provides an alternative metric, this "information black hole" itself will keep suppressing the valuation.

## No answer given — a framework instead

At this point, I'm not going to tell you whether you "should" or "shouldn't" buy. Because the same data can lead different people to completely different, and equally valid, conclusions.

But before deciding, you need to answer three questions:

**Question one: how do you judge the threat from Qishui Music?**

If you believe Qishui is merely an appendage of the Douyin ecosystem, that its catalog shortfall can't be closed, and that its RMB 8 monthly-card model isn't sustainable — then the Qishui threat is overstated, and the current price overreacts to fear.

If you believe ByteDance's execution has always been formidable, that its catalog gap will gradually narrow, and that a free-plus-ad model is enough to erode willingness to pay — then the Qishui threat may have only just begun, and the current price discount may not be enough.

**Question two: can you stomach another 30% decline?**

A 6x cash-adjusted PE is already cheap. But "cheap" doesn't mean "can't get cheaper." Chinese ADR valuation discounts can persist for a long time in extreme scenarios. If you buy in and the price falls another 30% to $6, would you add to the position or cut your losses?

If the answer is cut your losses, that suggests your position is too large, or your conviction in this investment isn't strong enough.

**Question three: is your holding period long enough?**

Ximalaya's regulatory restriction runs two years (until around 2028), lifting SVIP penetration will take time, and Qishui Music's endgame won't become clear in a quarter or two either.

If you need to see results within a year, Tencent Music may not be for you. If you have the patience for three years or more, the current price at least offers reasonably attractive odds — **the core precondition being that fundamentals don't deteriorate to an irreversible degree.**

**One actionable metric to watch**: track third-party data (QuestMobile, etc.) for changes in Qishui Music's MAU each quarter. If Qishui's MAU growth slows to single digits, it suggests its natural traffic pool (Douyin referral) is peaking and pressure on Tencent Music will ease; if Qishui's MAU keeps doubling and launches a large-scale paid push, the situation needs to be reassessed.

## Series summary

After three pieces, Tencent Music's full picture can be condensed into one sentence:

**This is a company whose profits are growing, whose cash is piling up, and whose business is transforming — but the market believes its core user base is slowly being eroded, and by halting data disclosure, the company has made it impossible for the market to falsify that worry.**

A 6x cash-adjusted PE is pricing in a script where "a chronic condition might worsen." If you believe that script, it's still not cheap enough. If you believe the chronic condition is manageable and won't turn terminal, this might be one of the best-odds opportunities among Chinese ADRs in recent years.

All the data is already on the table. **Cheap is never a reason to buy on its own — but when something is cheap enough that "even if you're wrong, you won't lose much," it's worth thinking seriously about.**
