# Jensen Huang Says AI Is a Five-Layer Cake — Who's Making Money at Each Layer?

This year, Jensen Huang has repeatedly laid out one framework at Davos and GTC: AI isn't a product, it's an entirely new industrial system, with five layers from bottom to top.

He calls these five layers the "AI cake." I spent a week pulling apart each layer, and found the investment logic inside is far clearer than expected.

## First, let's lay out the five layers clearly

```
Layer 5  Application layer      AI agents, autonomous driving, robotics
Layer 4  Model layer            Large language models, multimodal models
Layer 3  Infrastructure layer   Data centers, networking, cooling
Layer 2  Chip layer             GPUs, memory, advanced packaging
Layer 1  Energy layer           Power, transformers, nuclear energy
```

In Jensen Huang's own words: "In the future every company will have two factories — one producing products, and one an AI factory. The AI factory does only one thing every day: producing tokens."

He offered a core metric for measuring an AI factory's efficiency: tokens produced per watt. What this metric implies is that the AI business will ultimately resemble manufacturing — competing on how much intelligence can be produced per unit of electricity.

## Layer 1: Power — the most boring but most certain opportunity

Global data center electricity consumption reached 485 terawatt-hours in 2025, and is set to double within five years. But nearly half of planned data center projects in the US have already been delayed or canceled. Not because of a GPU shortage — because of a shortage of power and transformers.

Lead times for large power transformers have already stretched to four years. That means 2026 capacity was effectively locked in back in 2022 — an order placed today won't be delivered until 2030 at the earliest.

This layer is the least glamorous, but the most certain. The transformer shortage won't ease before 2028. Power equipment makers like Eaton and GE Vernova are the most reasonably valued beneficiaries in the AI supply chain.

## Layer 2: Chips — the $5 trillion monopoly king

Nvidia is still the "standard unit of measure" for AI compute. FY2027 Q1 revenue was $81.6 billion, up 85% year-over-year; data center revenue was $75.2 billion, up 92%; gross margin was 75%. Market cap: $5.2 trillion, the largest in the world.

But the change most worth watching: custom-chip growth (+45%) has for the first time overtaken general-purpose GPUs (+16%). Google, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft are all developing chips in-house or having Broadcom/Marvell design custom AI chips for them, with the goal of reducing dependence on Nvidia.

Broadcom's AI revenue this quarter was $10.8 billion, up 143% year-over-year — growing even faster than Nvidia. CEO Hock Tan said he's "confident AI chip revenue will exceed $100 billion in 2027," with $73 billion in backlog orders on hand.

Five key players at the chip layer: Nvidia (standard-setter), Broadcom (king of custom chips), AMD (the only general-purpose alternative), TSMC (manufacturing monopoly), SK Hynix (62% share of HBM memory).

## Layer 3: Infrastructure — a $725 billion arms race

The four major cloud providers' combined 2026 capex is $725 billion, doubling year-over-year. Not one of them is cutting back.

| Company | 2026 capex | YoY |
|------|--------------|------|
| Amazon | $200B | +60% |
| Microsoft | $190B | +138% |
| Google | $180B | +97% |
| Meta | $135B | +80% |

Where's the money going? Roughly 40-60% on GPUs/chips, 20-25% on building data centers, 15-20% on server components, 10-15% on networking equipment.

$725 billion is real money, not a slide deck. That money flows down the supply chain, sustaining optical modules (Innolight's 2025 net profit of RMB 10.8 billion), networking equipment (Arista), liquid cooling (Vertiv), and servers (Dell).

Worth calling out is Innolight — the one point in the entire AI supply chain where a Chinese company holds the strongest pricing power. It accounts for roughly half of the world's 800G optical modules, and gets 35% of Nvidia's orders.

## Layer 4: Models — the fastest revenue growth in history

Anthropic's annualized revenue went from $1 billion to $47 billion in just 14 months. This may be the fastest corporate revenue growth in the history of commerce.

| Company | Valuation | Annualized revenue |
|------|------|----------|
| Anthropic | $965B | $47B |
| OpenAI | $852B | $24B |
| xAI (merged with SpaceX) | $1.75T target | $3.2B (xAI portion) |
| DeepSeek | $51.5B | Undisclosed |

The second half of 2026 is shaping up to be a big year for AI company IPOs — Anthropic has already filed confidentially, and OpenAI is targeting a September listing. This will be the moment that tests whether these sky-high valuations can withstand public-market scrutiny.

DeepSeek is the most interesting variable at this layer. V4-Pro already surpasses Claude on math reasoning, with API pricing at just one-tenth to one-seventy-fifth of Western competitors. It proves one thing: the capability gap between models is narrowing, but the commercialization gap is still an order of magnitude.

## Layer 5: Applications — few companies are actually making money from AI

The harsh reality at this layer: for most "AI concept stocks," AI revenue still makes up a small share of the total. But valuations have diverged to an extreme degree.

| Company | AI revenue growth | P/E |
|------|-----------|-----|
| Palantir | +85% | 105x |
| Tesla | FSD subscription +51% | 317x |
| ServiceNow | AI cross-sell up 10x | 25x |
| Salesforce | Agentforce +200% | 14x |
| Adobe | Firefly credit consumption up 3x | 10x |

Palantir is the purest AI application company, with 85% growth crushing peers, but a 105x P/E has already priced in a lot of that. Salesforce and Adobe offer the best value — 14x and 10x P/E respectively, with AI products growing rapidly but the market assigning low valuations, because total revenue growth is only 10-12%.

If you believe monetization at the AI application layer is still in its early stages, then Salesforce's 14x P/E might be the most undervalued name in the entire AI supply chain.

## Who's the strongest player at each layer in China?

| Layer | China's strongest | Gap vs. global leader |
|------|---------|-------------|
| Chips | Huawei Ascend | One generation behind on hardware, a whole era behind on the CUDA ecosystem |
| Infrastructure | Innolight | Has global pricing power in optical modules — an exception |
| Models | DeepSeek | 3-6 months behind on technology, 100x behind on commercialization |
| Cloud | Alibaba Cloud | 6x behind on revenue, a huge gap in globalization |
| Applications | iFlytek | Not weak in industry-specific deployment, but far behind in general-purpose applications |

China's AI biggest progress is at the model layer (DeepSeek), its biggest bottleneck is at the chip layer (the CUDA ecosystem), and its most underappreciated gap is in commercialization — the technology has caught up, but the ability to turn "technology into money" is still an order of magnitude behind.

## A final word

Jensen Huang's five-layer-cake framework offers a very clear way of thinking: the lower the layer, the more physical, the more unavoidable the link — the higher the investment certainty.

The most glamorous layers are models and applications — Anthropic's $47 billion in revenue, Palantir's 85% growth. But these are also the least certain: valuations have already flown sky-high, and once growth slows, the drawdown will be brutal.

The most boring layers are energy and infrastructure — transformers, liquid cooling, optical modules. But these are the AI industry's real chokepoints. A four-year transformer lead time is a bottleneck nobody can get around.

Along this AI supply chain, selling shovels is always more certain than panning for gold. It's just that these days, even the best shovels aren't cheap anymore.
