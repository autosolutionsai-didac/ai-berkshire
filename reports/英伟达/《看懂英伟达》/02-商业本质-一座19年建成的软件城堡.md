## What NVIDIA Really Sells

One detail makes the point well: a Caltech data-center administrator said that **"professors would rather wait 18 months to get NVIDIA's chips than switch to another supplier."** They wait a year and a half rather than turning to competing products that are readily available and cheaper.

This isn't brand loyalty — it's a switching cost too high to bear. NVIDIA doesn't sell shovels; it sells **an entire mining operation: shovels + ore carts + tunnels + an operating manual + a training school** — in industry terms: **GPU chips + the NVLink interconnect network + the CUDA software ecosystem + AI framework integration + enterprise services.** You can swap out just the shovel, but you can't carry away the other four.

The core of this system is CUDA.

## What Exactly Is CUDA's Moat

### An Imperfect but Useful Analogy

Think of CUDA as "the Windows of the GPU world."

Microsoft Windows's moat isn't the operating system itself — Linux has long been more pleasant to use than Windows. Windows's moat lies in decades of accumulated applications (millions of them), the usage habits of billions of users, and deep entrenchment across the world's corporate IT systems. You can build a better operating system, but you can't move the ecosystem sitting on top of it.

CUDA is the same. Its moat isn't in any single technical point — it's in a **five-layer stacked ecosystem built through 19 years (2006-2026) of systematic investment**:

| Layer | Specific content | Key figures |
|------|---------|---------|
| Layer 1: Hardware abstraction | CUDA Driver / Runtime (co-developed from the same source as NVIDIA's chips) | Dedicated optimization for every GPU generation |
| Layer 2: Core math libraries | cuDNN (**100,000 lines** of hand-optimized code), cuBLAS, NCCL, TensorRT | AMD's MIOpen trails by **30-50%** |
| Layer 3: Domain libraries | cuDF, cuML, cuQuantum, RAPIDS, Modulus, Isaac | AMD has **almost zero coverage** |
| Layer 4: Framework layer | PyTorch / JAX / vLLM default to CUDA-first | ROCm is a second-class citizen, lagging **6-12 months** |
| Layer 5: Application layer | Hugging Face, TensorRT-LLM, Stable Diffusion, ComfyUI | **80%** of mainstream models offer partial AMD support |

> Data sources: NVIDIA's official website, ThunderCompute's 2026 benchmark review, the PyTorch community

**The moat = the product of the five layers, not their sum.** Breaching any single layer isn't enough — all five must be breached simultaneously to truly replace CUDA. That's why after 10 years of building ROCm, AMD is still "a CUDA alternative," not "a CUDA replacement."

### The Three Deepest Moats

Among the five layers, three are hardest to break through:

**1. TensorRT-LLM (the inference optimization engine)**

NVIDIA's inference optimization engine lets the same GPU deliver inference performance **3-10x** faster than running bare. AMD has no equivalent product.

Measured data: running Llama 3.1 8B on an H100 with an NIM container (built on TensorRT-LLM) achieves **1,201 tokens/s** of throughput, versus just 613 tokens/s for bare PyTorch — **on the same hardware, software makes a 2x difference.**

**2. NCCL (the communication library for 10,000-GPU clusters)**

Training frontier large models at the GPT-5 / Claude 4 level requires tens of thousands of GPUs working in concert. NCCL is NVIDIA's cluster communication library; combined with the NVLink and InfiniBand networks, it's **30%+** faster than AMD's RCCL on all-reduce communication at 10,000-GPU scale.

This isn't a gap that can be "quickly closed" — a Meta paper shows that fault detection and recovery in 100,000-GPU-scale training clusters requires response times on the order of 3 minutes; this kind of system-level optimization requires years of engineering accumulation.

**3. cuDNN (core deep-learning primitives)**

With every new GPU generation, the cuDNN team performs **individual, hand-crafted optimization** of the chip's cache hierarchy, warp scheduler, and tensor cores, pushing hardware utilization to the 90%+ limit. NVIDIA's version of FlashAttention v2/v3 is typically integrated **6-12 months** ahead of the AMD version.

## Why Jensen Huang Says "Other People's Chips Are Too Expensive Even for Free"

The line sounds arrogant, but it's backed by data.

**Core logic**: chip cost is only part of the total cost of AI infrastructure. The bigger costs are software adaptation, cluster tuning, staff training, and migration risk.

| Cost item | Buying NVIDIA | Buying AMD (example) |
|--------|---------|--------------|
| Chip price | $30-50K/unit (B200) | $10-15K/unit (MI300X) |
| Software adaptation | Zero cost (native CUDA) | Months of engineering time |
| Performance loss | 100% baseline | ~70-85% bare (requires extra optimization) |
| Cluster communication | NCCL + NVLink (validated at 10,000-GPU scale) | RCCL + Infinity Fabric (validated at 1,000-GPU scale) |
| Operational risk | Industry standard, mature fault tolerance | New platform, few production-environment cases |
| **TCO (total cost of ownership)** | **Baseline** | **Potentially higher** (saves on chips but costs more in labor) |

**Counterargument**: this logic holds for universities and startups, but **not necessarily** for companies like AWS, Google, and Microsoft, which have thousands of chip engineers. They have the capability, the motivation, and the capital to build their own chips — and they're already doing it. The TCO scale is tilting toward in-house chips, especially in inference scenarios.

## Cracks Are Appearing in the Moat — But It Hasn't Collapsed

### Crack 1: AI coding tools have crashed the cost of code migration

In January 2026, a developer used Claude Code to **port a CUDA backend to AMD's ROCm platform in 30 minutes**, without relying on any intermediate translation tool, with performance loss of under 10%.

This would have been impossible in 2023. Back then, migrating from CUDA to ROCm required an engineering team spending months.

The advance of AI coding tools is rapidly eroding CUDA's "code lock-in" layer:

| Time | AI coding capability | CUDA migration cost |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| 2023 | Could only write basic code | A team + several months |
| 2025 | Could write kernels involving tensor cores | 1-2 people + a few weeks |
| 2026 | Can port a CUDA backend end-to-end | **1 person + a few hours** |

> Data sources: Techstrong.ai, the CASS paper (95% source-translation accuracy), ThunderCompute benchmarks

**But the moat won't collapse overnight** — what gets ported in 30 minutes is a simple kernel. AI tools still can't crack complex cluster-communication code, extreme-optimization inference engines, or 10,000-GPU-scale system tuning.

### Crack 2: The ROCm gap has narrowed from 3x to 30%

| Year | ROCm vs. CUDA performance gap (compute-intensive tasks) |
|------|--------------------------------------|
| 2023 | **2-3x** |
| 2026 | **10-30%** |

> Data source: ThunderCompute independent benchmarks, April 2026

AMD's ROCm 7.0 strategy explicitly states it is "aligning more closely with CUDA semantics" — it is systematically closing the gap.

### Crack 3: The Triton compiler is building an "escape hatch"

OpenAI's open-source Triton compiler (version 3.6.0, released January 2026) already supports an AMD backend. PyTorch's TorchInductor compiler is also pushing a "backend-agnostic" programming paradigm — developers write code once, and the compiler automatically adapts it to different chips.

**This means**: future AI developers may no longer need to write CUDA directly — they write Python / Triton, and the compiler automatically generates the underlying code. If this path succeeds, CUDA's "code lock-in" will be fundamentally bypassed.

### But the Moat Is Far From Collapsing

**Parts that remain solid**:

| Moat layer | Status | Reason |
|-----------|------|------|
| TensorRT-LLM extreme inference optimization | **Solid** | 3-10x performance, no AMD equivalent |
| NCCL 10,000-GPU cluster communication | **Solid** | No replacement for 100,000-GPU-scale training |
| cuDNN per-generation manual optimization | **Solid** | Must stay in sync with hardware; AMD lags 6-12 months |
| NVLink / NVSwitch interconnect | **Solid** | Full interconnect across 72 GPUs (NVL72); no competing equivalent |
| First-mover support for new algorithms | **Solid** | NVIDIA is typically first to support things like FlashAttention |
| The reverse flywheel of AI coding | **Solid** | 99% of GPU code on the internet is CUDA; AI finds it easier to write CUDA than ROCm |

**Parts being eroded**:

| Moat layer | Status | Reason |
|-----------|------|------|
| The barrier to basic CUDA code migration | **Already substantially weakened** | AI tools + Triton + HIPIFY |
| PyTorch's default reliance on CUDA | **Loosening** | TorchInductor supports multiple backends |
| CUDA lock-in in simple inference scenarios | **Essentially gone** | AMD's MI355X TCO has already overtaken NVIDIA in some scenarios |

## The Moat Is Changing Shape — From "Software Lock-in" to "Full-Stack Factory"

A more precise judgment is that CUDA's moat isn't "disappearing" — it's **changing shape**.

| Period | Form of the moat | Core barrier |
|------|-----------|---------|
| 2006-2020 | CUDA software lock-in | Extremely high code-migration cost |
| 2020-2025 | CUDA + full-stack integration | GPU + NVLink + software + networking |
| 2025-2030 (forecast) | Full-stack AI factory | Generational hardware lead + system-level sales of NVL72/576 + services |

NVIDIA used to make money because "your code is locked into CUDA, and you can't leave even if you want to." In the future, it will make money because "my whole system is 2-3x better than what you could piece together yourself, so you don't want to leave."

The former is **lock-in**; the latter is **competitiveness**. The latter is healthier, but pricing power gets compressed — customers now have choices, and NVIDIA can no longer name any price it likes.

**Counterargument**: the full-stack AI factory strategy means NVIDIA must continuously invest $15 billion+ a year in R&D to maintain its generational lead. If any single generation of products (say, Rubin Ultra) is delayed or underperforms, the moat could narrow quickly. This is a battle that requires "winning every single year" — unlike Kweichow Moutai, whose moat doesn't shrink if you leave it alone.

## A Key Question From Duan Yongping

In an April 2024 post on Xueqiu, Duan Yongping made a very pointed remark:

> "What I haven't been able to figure out is this: **how long can this kind of monopoly actually last? How long will demand for NVIDIA's chips actually last?** If I can't figure that out, it's hard to bet big. And if you can't bet big, none of this really matters."

Two years later (Q4 2025), Duan Yongping had raised NVIDIA to **7.7%** of his portfolio (his third-largest holding), but never took it above 10%. He later explained:

> "I can't say I've fully figured it out, but I do feel that NVDA's moat is fairly formidable. Looking at it, this is clearly a good company — buying some will probably make money too."

> "I accept Jensen's view that DeepSeek's innovation won't reduce demand for compute, and I broadly understand his point that NVDA's position in compute won't be threatened."

**Duan Yongping's stance is: a good company, a moat with some real depth, but he doesn't have a 10-year read on it — so he takes a medium position, not a heavy one.**

This is a highly disciplined decision framework: **acknowledge you don't fully understand it, but be willing to participate through position sizing.**

## Conclusion of This Piece

| Question | Conclusion |
|------|------|
| Is CUDA's moat deep? | **Yes, but it's being eroded from the top** |
| How fast is the erosion? | The code-lock-in layer will **weaken significantly within 2-3 years**; system-level moats (10,000-GPU communication + extreme optimization) will **remain solid for 5-10 years** |
| Will the moat disappear? | **No, but it will change shape** — from "software lock-in" to "full-stack competitiveness" |
| What does it mean for valuation? | The probability of a **structural downshift in gross margin** (75% → 65-70%) is rising |
